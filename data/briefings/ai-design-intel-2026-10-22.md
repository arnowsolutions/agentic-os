### Desk Studio — three roughs, an art-director pick, and a proof gate that can fail you

**Link:** https://github.com/adityareddy-dev/deskstudio
**Type:** Tool / Workflow (MIT, npm `deskstudio`, Claude Code plugin + multi-agent CLI)
**What it is:** Created **2026-10-20**, 41★, 41 forks, MIT. It turns your existing coding agent into a small design studio with named roles: an **UI Designer** makes three directions at a "senior" model, an **Art Director** looks at the renders and picks one or combines them and writes review notes, a **builder desk** revises with those notes *plus the screenshot*, then a **Proof** stage runs **with no model in the loop** — measuring contrast on rendered pixels, overflow and clipping, safe zones per platform, palette membership, type-scale membership, copy presence, carousel consistency across slides, reading time and flashes in motion, in headless Chrome at each size. Only then does a **Creative Director** score **eight axes — hierarchy, typography, spacing, colour, composition, originality, brand fit, copy** — and the job passes only if *every axis is ≥8 and the average ≥8.5*. Anything under the bar comes back **marked as failed, with the offending axis named.** It routes models by role (Opus/Sonnet/Haiku, or the OpenAI and Gemini equivalents), keeps files in `.desk/out/<job>/` with a note on who did what, the scores and the cost, and publishes **17 signed-off pieces** in `evals/golden/` with renders, states and final files — for four invented brands plus one real site where it recovered the brand from the project itself. The eight axes are not new; the *gate* is.

**What's good about it:** Every tool in this brief for two weeks has promised a higher ceiling. This is the first that ships a mechanism for **refusing output**, and refusal is the entire luxury good in design. Note the architecture of the proof stage specifically: no model judges whether the text is legible against the pixels behind it — the browser measures it. That split is the one the whole industry keeps getting wrong (a model reviewing its own screenshots is a model grading its own homework), and Desk Studio draws the line in exactly the right place: pixels for what pixels can tell you, a model only for what only a person would judge. The scoring rubric is also unusually specific for a tool — "every axis at 8 or above" is a testable gate, not a vibe, and its own README is honest that "scores come from the studio's model reviewers, they are not independent benchmarks." And the escalation ladder is real design-management thinking: same note missed twice goes to a senior, senior twice goes to the Art Director for one round, then the job dies. That is how studios actually work, written as a state machine.

**Pros:**
- A named rejection path with the failing axis reported — the single most useful thing an agent can tell you about your own work.
- The proof gate is deterministic and separate from the judging models, measuring real rendered pixels rather than descriptions of pixels.
- Routes cheap models to roughs and expensive models to the pick — three directions on Sonnet and one judgment on Opus is exactly the economics of a real studio.
- Everything is inspectable: 17 golden pieces with renders, state files and costs, plus editable desk briefs preserved between runs (`--force` to overwrite).
- Runs entirely local, MIT, and drops into Claude Code, Codex, Cursor, Gemini CLI, Copilot, Windsurf and OpenCode; the dashboard is 127.0.0.1-only with a token.

**Cons:**
- **It optimises against a rubric it wrote for itself.** Every score in the README is a model's opinion of a model's output against eight axes the same repo defines. The 8.5 threshold is calibrated to that rubric, not to a design director.
- Cost is not trivial: brand kits run **$10.16–$11.60 per job**, decks $7.93, and one social card took four rounds. A weekly social flight through this reads $30–60 for assets that would come from a two-hour Figma session.
- The actual runnable runner covers **social cards only** for non-Claude-Code hosts; the other commands are Claude Code skills. It is a Claude-Code-shaped tool with adapters, not genuinely cross-harness yet.
- The `evals/golden/` set is self-selected: nobody has published a piece this studio *failed*, so the failure mode is undocumented — and the failure mode is the thing you're buying.
- "Originality" as a scored axis is the weakest link in the rubric — a model will reward the unusual reliably and the *right* only sometimes, which is the closest this gets to flavour-of-the-month.
- Two days old, one author, 41 forks in 48 hours that smell more like a launch spike than adoption. No institutional review of whether the proof checks hold on a real brand system.

**Should we...**
- ✅ **Adopt it** — use it now; it raises our design ceiling
**Why:** The finding worth keeping is not the tool, it is the **structure of the gate**: roughs → an art-director pick → a builder revision that works from the screenshot → a deterministic pixel proof → a scored sign-off that is allowed to fail the job. Nothing in our current flow has a stage that can say no to us; the agent produces, we look, we feel vaguely disappointed, and we ask for it again. That loop is why AI-assisted work drifts toward the average — not bad models, but the absence of a gate. Point this at the residency campaign's social flight first, and specifically at the *proof* half rather than the generation half: `desk-proof check hero.html --brand .desk/DESIGN.md --sizes phone,desktop` is a contrast-and-safe-zone check against our own DESIGN.md that we can run on anything we make, including hand-made files, for free. Take the eight-axis rubric into the review conversation and take the escalation ladder as policy — a note missed twice means a person looks, not a third round. Do **not** hand it the Urology Unified Platform; that's a production system with a committed system and frozen layout by policy, and no tool gets to re-decide Inter and navy. Do not let the 8.5 score travel into a client conversation either — it is a house rubric, and the honest framing is "our studio's gate," not "a design score." Adopt it because the mechanism is right and cheap to copy even if the tool itself is abandoned in a month; the parts worth owning are the gate, the pixel proof, and the escalation.

### Pentagram — Arabic and English woven into one composition, connected by strokes that stretch

**Link:** https://www.creativeboom.com/news/pentagrams-festival-identity-finds-a-clever-way-to-give-arabic-and-english-equal-billing/
**Type:** Idea / Technique (bilingual typographic system)
**What it is:** **Samar Maakaroun**, a Pentagram partner, designed the identity for **Design Doha Biennial 2026** — the largest design platform in the Arab region, whose expanded second edition opens 5 November. The strategic problem is the one every bilingual system fails: whatever you do, one language becomes the accidental lead and the other reads as visually subservient. Pentagram's answer is to refuse the stacked lockup entirely. **Latin and Arabic are woven into a single typographic composition**, drawn with **the same thin, even stroke**, reading "more like one family than two separate scripts." The governing device is **extended strokes** that "stretch and expand like lines on a network," letting letterforms act as **connectors between elements** — and because they can expand and contract, the system is variable and adapts to format. The wordmark sits over photography of installations on posters, and in other layouts an **outsized "26" loops around and frames a central image**. Palette: **travertine-inspired beige and deep maroon** for regional architecture and geology, a **vivid orange** for the festival's optimism, and **the same saturated Design Doha blue carried over from the first edition** to keep continuity. The headline exhibition, **Arab Design Now**, gets a deliberate register shift: same bilingual typography, same connective letterforms, but **bolder — heavy solid geometric shapes that lock together** into letters and numerals. There is no fixed logo; the brand is a **flexible typographic system**.

**What's good about it:** Every bilingual identity I have seen solves subordination by *separating* the scripts — parallel blocks, matched sizes, a rule between them — which preserves the hierarchy problem instead of dissolving it. Weaving them into one mark solves it structurally. But the real craft is the **stroke weight parity** and the **connective strokes**: one hairline weight for both scripts means neither can shout; the stretching lines then become the system's only expressive variable, so "more energy" and "more emphasis" are expressed by *a move the system already contains* rather than by adding a second device. That's the same discipline as the COLLINS vanishing-point law (Oct 21) applied to type: one rule, precisely stated, that any asset either obeys or is visibly wrong. And the two-register structure — hairline for the Biennial, heavy interlocking shapes for the exhibition — is the cleanest answer I've seen to the "sub-brand that must look related but not identical" problem, which every department with a flagship and a programme has.

**Pros:**
- Stroke-weight parity instead of size parity: the two scripts are equal because they are drawn the same, not because they were scaled to match.
- Connective strokes give the system exactly one expressive move, which makes it extendable by people who are not the designer.
- A variable system rather than a fixed lockup means the same idea survives a poster, a tote and a 12px favicon without a second design.
- The palette is meaning-carrying — regional stone, regional geology, and a carried-over blue for continuity rather than a fresh arbitrary accent.
- The exhibition sub-brand (heavy interlocking geometry) demonstrates register shift without brand drift.

**Cons:**
- One language has no case. Arabic has no uppercase, so "weave the two scripts into one composition" is far harder when the second script *does* have capitals — English/Spanish is a much easier problem than English/Arabic, and even easier than the reverse.
- The captured coverage is Creative Boom describing the agency's own rationale plus Brand New's review behind a paywall; there's no misuse evidence and no data on whether the system survived 40+ activations.
- It requires a type designer, not a designer. Drawing matching hairline weights across two scripts is foundry-adjacent work, and nothing here is reproducible by picking fonts.
- A woven mark with variable stroke lengths is close to illegible at small sizes, which is precisely where institutional design lives (badge, email signature, slide footer).
- The connective-line motif is current festival-identity house style — the "organic line that links things" is the 2026 look, and borrowing the surface without the script work is a template.

**Should we...**
- ✅ **Adopt it** — use it now; it raises our design ceiling
**Why:** Two things transfer directly and one of them is urgent. The urgent one: **our patient-facing material is becoming bilingual whether or not we planned it.** A Urology department in the Bronx serves a Spanish-speaking population large enough that any Sub-I packet, appointment card or waiting-room poster with only English is a design failure before it's a language failure. The Pentagram lesson gives us the rule to solve it without a redesign: **the two scripts get the same stroke weight, are set to the same optical size, and are joined by one shared connective device** — not an English block above a smaller Spanish block with a rule between them, which is what every clinic poster in the country does and which silently ranks the two audiences. The second transferable move is the **two-register system**: one hairline voice for the department, one heavier interlocking voice for a specific programme or campaign. That is exactly the structure the residency programme needs — it should read as *belonging to* the department's identity while being unmistakably the programme's own, and today it has neither. Practically: take this to the next Sub-I packet and the residency poster set, define the two registers in writing, and hold everything to the weight-parity rule. Do not attempt a woven bilingual wordmark — that is foundry work and we do not have an Arabic script to weave; the *rule* transfers, the mark does not.

### Day Job — twenty wordmarks, one primary, and "ever-evolving expression" as brand equity

**Link:** https://www.itsnicethat.com/articles/tbf-day-job-graphic-design-project-211026
**Type:** Idea / Technique (identity system built on intentional variability)
**What it is:** LA studio **Day Job** (co-founder **Rion Harmon**) branded **To Be Formed**, the production company co-founded by filmmaker **Lance Oppenheim** (*Primetime*, *You Can See Everything*, Phoebe Bridgers' *Lost Boys*). The brief was to make "something as idiosyncratic as Lance's work — something you can't quite put your finger on or fully explain," in the liminal space his films occupy between fiction and non-fiction: "intentional but also haphazard and odd." The primary wordmark is **florid and formidable**, taking up space, and punctuated with internal contrast — **thick forms next to slim calligraphic lettering with varying x-heights**, so the mark is at odds with itself. The name began as a **placeholder title** Oppenheim kept because it described his process — not knowing the final narrative before production — so the identity mirrors the working method. Designed by staff designer **Orysia Zabeida** to "feel like it was about to be formed into something new." Then the system: **about 20 additional wordmarks** for interchangeable use, "many pastiche to the broader corporate media culture," with the primary mark as "an amalgamation of them all." References ran from "old off-kilter book covers to early 00s DVD menu screens," deliberately settling on an identity that "doesn't feel like one specific thing." The website is built to "have some tension to it," stylised but not over-produced, with **mementos from each movie as the navigation**. Harmon's closing line is the thesis: **"when there's chaos built into the identity, ever-evolving expression becomes part of the brand equity, reliably experimental."**

**What's good about it:** This is the answer to a problem this brief has circled for a week and never solved. On Oct 21 I endorsed Bud Rodecker's *absorbency over timelessness* test — can a system take on the next thing without a redesign? — and left it as a question. Day Job answers it operationally: **build the variability into the mark itself**, so the next film does not need a new identity, it needs the next wordmark from a family that already contains twenty. That is absorbency as an asset rather than a hope. The craft detail that makes it work instead of collapsing into mess is the *internal* contrast: the mark is odd because thick and thin letterforms with mismatched x-heights sit inside one lockup, which is a controlled tension you can extend, not an absence of decisions. And the pastiche direction is unusually honest — secondary marks that quote corporate media culture say something true about a production company, rather than just being decorative variants.

**Pros:**
- Twenty interchangeable marks turns "the brand looks different every time" from a failure into the rule — absorbency made operational.
- The primary mark earned its place and has a name and a story that carry strategy: a placeholder title about not knowing the story yet.
- Internal contrast (thick/slim, varying x-heights) gives the oddity a construction principle rather than a vibe, so staff can extend it.
- Reference set is specific and unglamorous — off-kilter book covers and 00s DVD menus — which is why it doesn't look like the 2026 premium default.
- Navigation made of mementos from each film is a genuine information-architecture idea: the identity's own history is the wayfinding.

**Cons:**
- **Twenty wordmarks is a governance nightmare** for anyone without a designer on staff; a department cannot maintain 20 marks, and misuse starts the day someone picks #14 for a press release.
- This register — wilful-odd, florid, pastiche-heavy — is a creative-industry signal that reads as *sloppy* in clinical and academic contexts. Borrowing the surface for a hospital department would be a category error.
- The "chaos as equity" line is a beautiful sentence and exactly the kind of claim that is unfalsifiable in a case study. No evidence offered that the system reads as intentional to anyone outside the studio.
- Variety at the mark level is a luxury of a brand whose audience sees dozens of touchpoints. A residency campaign has five, and repeated inconsistency reads as error, not expression.
- It's It's Nice That covering the studio's own work — admiring, agency-sourced, no independent critique.

**Should we...**
- ✅ **Adopt it** — use it now; it raises our design ceiling
**Why:** Steal the **mechanism**, and be strict about the scale. What we should take is: *the primary mark is one member of a described family, and the family is defined in writing before anyone starts.* Concretely, for the residency programme: define **one primary lockup** and **three sanctioned variants** — not twenty — each with a stated job (poster register, social register, document register), and write down the construction rule that makes them siblings (in our case: the accent rule and the type tracking, not oddity). That gives us variation without a governance failure, and it finally gives the programme an identity that survives a new cohort without a redesign — Rodecker's absorbency test, passed. The second steal is free and belongs on our surfaces immediately: **wayfinding made from the programme's own artifacts.** The Sub-I packet's navigation should be built from real objects of the programme — a case list, an OR schedule, a name badge, a teaching file spine — the way To Be Formed navigates by film mementos. That is more premium than any icon set, it can't be prompted, and it solves the "we have no photography" problem honestly. Do not adopt the florid-odd register; it is a creative-industry signal and would read as careless on anything a patient or applicant touches.

### UnitedUs — a "Verified Human" mark built from face-mapping coordinates, in security-hologram ink

**Link:** https://www.creativeboom.com/news/can-a-trust-mark-keep-your-face-out-of-ai-unitedus-thinks-so-with-its-rebrand-for-twinnin/
**Type:** Idea / Technique (trust mark / identity system)
**What it is:** Brighton B Corp studio **UnitedUs** rebranded **Twinnin**, a consent, licensing and provenance registry for the human face — prove you are a real person, then either **License** your likeness to brands and get paid, or **Defend** it out of AI training entirely. The brief was to look "more grown-up" and to signal consent visually. UnitedUs boiled it to four words — **"Your face. Your control."** — and built **two trust markers for the two choices** (Licence / Defend) presented as **three-dimensional badges in shimmering hues**. The logo is the craft: **built from the technical coordinates of a human face** — the mapping points facial-recognition software uses — finished in **holographic tints borrowed from security labels on DVDs and game cartridges**, on the argument that holograms are hard to fake, so the mark itself argues for authenticity. Imagery shows real verified people registered on the platform. Tone is defiantly "you," optimistic rather than frightening. **Defend** produces a date-stamped declaration objecting to AI training, synthetic media and automated profiling, time-stamped on a public blockchain, plus a shareable verification link and badge and a takedown dashboard. First license is Model Management, opening the registry to 3M+ models. Context stat: identity firm **Entrust recorded a 3,000% rise in deepfake attempts between 2022 and 2023**.

**What's good about it:** The mark's construction is the best idea in this item and it is genuinely structural, not decorative: **draw the logo from facial-recognition landmark points**, so the geometry that AI uses to *identify* a face becomes the geometry that asserts control over it. That is a mark whose form carries its argument, which is the difference between a brand and a logo. The hologram decision is the second half of the same idea — a security-print reference puts the mark in the visual family of things that are hard to counterfeit, and the brain makes that association before it reads a word. The system structure is also clean design thinking: **two choices become two badges**, so the strategy is navigable rather than explained. And the reason this brief cares: this is where identity design is heading — marks that assert *provenance* — which is directly relevant to anyone producing images of real clinicians and real facilities.

**Pros:**
- The mark is generated from a real, specific, meaning-carrying data structure (facial landmarks) rather than from a metaphor — unfakeable and unpromptable in kind.
- Two-choice architecture (Licence / Defend) turns a legal proposition into a visual system with two states, which is how you make compliance legible.
- The security-hologram reference does pre-verbal work: it borrows the visual grammar of anti-counterfeiting before the copy says anything.
- Images use verified real people, so the photography enacts the product claim instead of illustrating it.
- Tone reframes the audience from victims to people with options, which is a strategic copy decision with design consequences.

**Cons:**
- **The execution risks landing on the exact aesthetic we spend this brief escaping**: holographic rainbow gradients on a dark or pastel background is the single most recognisable "2025–26 AI product" look, and a hologram gradient is one prompt away from looking generated.
- A landmark-points grid as a mark is close to a cliché in the AI-ethics category — several trust and provenance startups have shipped versions, and the differentiator here is the finish, not the idea.
- A trust mark only means something if the registry is enforceable; the mark's authority is borrowed from the business, and the business is young, so the identity is ahead of the product.
- The blockchain-timestamped declaration is a technical claim this design coverage can't verify, and design writing that repeats it uncritically is doing the company's marketing.
- Badge-style trust marks generally lose at small sizes and add clutter to layout; a mark whose job is to sit *anywhere* is a mark that will eventually be shrunk to nothing.

**Should we...**
- 👀 **Watch it** — promising, not proven yet
**Why:** The *idea to steal* is narrow and immediately useful: **when you must assert something about a photograph, build the assertion into the mark's construction.** We are about to be in exactly that position. Every faculty headshot used on the residency site, every facility photo in the Sub-I packet, and every image in the marketing creative will eventually face the question "is this a real photo of a real person here?" — and the answer should not be a small grey caption. A department-level mark built from a real, specific structure of *our* place — a Bronx coordinate grid, the department's founding year in a geometric lattice, a landmark outline of the building — signals "this is ours and it's real" without a single word. That is a 2027 project worth starting, not today's. Watch rather than adopt because the *finish* is a hazard: holographic gradients are the most template-adjacent surface available right now (see Slop Radar), and copying them would put us squarely in the look we're paying this brief to leave. If we build one, it is flat, one colour, single-weight, and printed — not shimmering.

### Booms & Shakes, October — four agencies hired strategists, nobody hired an AI specialist, and "craft" refused to die

**Link:** https://www.creativeboom.com/booms-shakes/booms-shakes-october/
**Type:** Idea / Pattern (industry intelligence, 19 October 2026)
**What it is:** Creative Boom's monthly industry round-up, and this edition is unusually load-bearing because it names what agencies are actually buying. The observation: **not one press release they sifted this month mentioned an "AI specialist."** Instead, four agencies **doubled down on strategy** — Landor created a global strategy role for James Withey, Small World its first head of strategy, Dalziel & Pow appointed Adam Thompson to lead strategy, plus a FutureBrand reshuffle — and everyone hired for craft. **ustwo** made a *former creative director* its **head of tech** (Kim Lawrie, from House 337), citing research that **65% of tech leaders now prioritise creativity as a crucial soft skill**, with the quote "building the right foundations for engineers to adapt and move quickly alongside AI tools is how we ensure craft and speed go hand in hand" — and appointed **Google DeepMind's Simon Bouton** as its first AI non-executive director. **Elmwood's** new CD praises "respect for the craft"; **Brandtech** — unapologetically AI-first — hired Ogilvy/TBWA heavyweight **Chris Garbutt** as global CCO. Second theme: **proof.** Interbrand appointed a **brand economist**, Small World builds measurement "into every campaign from day one," Reebok's agency win came with an engagement rate attached. Third: consolidation — The Chase bought Squad, Say Yes and Verse merged into COMMON, **Lippincott is acquiring Athletics** (the Brooklyn studio with proprietary AI software **Sesame**), Haymaker bought SWISS. Plus the counter-quote from Brandtech's David Jones: "Yes, of course there is a ton of AI slop — but last time I checked there is also an enormous amount of terrible content created by humans without any help from AI."

**What's good about it:** This is the rarest thing in an AI-design brief: **evidence about what buyers are paying for**, not what vendors are shipping. The signal is the *absence* — no AI-specialist hires in a month of agency press releases — and the *presence* of strategists, economists and creative directors in technology seats. That is the market repricing taste rather than tools, in real hiring data. The ustwo move is the most instructive: putting a creative director in charge of engineering, justified by a claim that creativity is now a top soft skill for tech leaders, is an org-chart answer to "who decides what good looks like in an AI pipeline?" — and their head-of-tech quote is the cleanest statement of the craft/speed relationship in months. The Lippincott–Athletics deal is the one to watch structurally: a 80-year-old brand consultancy buying a 25-person studio *for its prototyping-led process and its proprietary AI software* is a bet that AI capability belongs inside a brand practice rather than bolted on.

**Pros:**
- Real hiring and M&A receipts for a thesis we've been building on instinct — the market is paying for judgment, not generation.
- The ustwo example gives a concrete org pattern: the person who owns craft sits above the people who own the tools.
- "Proof" as the month's second theme — brand economists, measurement from day one — is a directive for how we should present work internally.
- The Brandtech hire is the honest counter-case: an AI-first group concluding it still needs a world-class human creative at the top, which is the strongest available argument that the two coexist by hierarchy, not by substitution.
- No paywall, dated, named sources, quotes attributed — usable as a market argument in a budget conversation.

**Cons:**
- It's a trade round-up written from press releases; the sample is self-selected by whoever emailed Creative Boom that month, and "no AI specialist hires" is a claim about PR copy, not about the labour market.
- Agency hires are lagging indicators — agencies restructure after clients do — so this describes 2025's decisions arriving in 2026's org charts.
- "Craft" as a word is doing so much work in these quotes that it's become a reassurance signal rather than a description; four agencies using it is four agencies positioning, not four agencies agreeing on what it means.
- Nothing here tells you *how* to do anything; it is market weather, and weather is not a technique.
- British-agency-heavy, with a US section that is mostly acquisitions — a skew worth noting since our market is institutional healthcare, which hires nothing like an agency.

**Should we...**
- 👀 **Watch it** — promising, not proven yet
**Why:** Use it as an argument, not an instruction. The two sentences worth keeping verbatim are ustwo's — *craft and speed go hand in hand* — and the pattern of putting a **craft owner above the tool owners**. That is directly applicable to us: it says the person who decides what good looks like should sit at the top of the pipeline, and the tools underneath should be chosen by how well they serve that person's judgment — which is exactly the architecture Desk Studio attempts above, and exactly the inverse of the "pick a style from 300" pattern this brief has rejected twice. Watch rather than adopt because it's market intelligence with no craft surface, and because the one thing it can't tell us is what institutional buyers — a hospital, a GME office, a recruitment audience — are willing to pay for. If I were Shareef, I'd take one line from it into the next budget conversation: **every agency that sells creativity is hiring strategists and creative directors right now, not AI specialists**, because that's the same trade the department is making when it invests in art direction over another subscription.

### imagegen-skills — generate the design image first, then build the site to match it

**Link:** https://github.com/Leonxlnx/imagegen-skills
**Type:** Tool (MIT agent skills for Claude Code, Codex, any image-capable agent)
**What it is:** Created **2026-10-18**, 3★, by the author of `taste-skill`, split out of that repo in October 2026 "so each repo does one job: taste-skill builds and checks real frontends, imagegen-skills makes the pictures." Four skills: **`imagegen-frontend-web`** (website comps — "one image per section of a landing page, with varied composition and readable type"), **`imagegen-frontend-mobile`** (app screens and flows "consistent across a set" for iOS, Android, cross-platform), **`brandkit`** (brand-guideline boards: logo directions, symbol systems, palettes, type and applications), and **`image-to-code`** — the interesting one — **"the whole pipeline: generate the design images, study them, then build the website to match."** The first three produce images only and hand frames to a coding agent; `image-to-code` requires an agent that can both generate images and write code. Installable per-skill via `npx skills add`, or as Claude Code / Codex plugins.

**What's good about it:** `image-to-code` inverts the workflow everyone else ships. The default AI web flow is: model writes HTML/CSS from a sentence, then an image model decorates it. That produces exactly the failure the PrajwalTomar thread documented — "the same headline formula in every section, six random editorial devices, none of them saying anything about the product" — because code arrives before any composition decision. **Compose first as an image, then build to match it** puts the art direction upstream of the code, which is the correct order and the order a real studio uses (comp in Figma, then implement). The "one image per section with varied composition" rule is also quietly good: it forces section-level variety rather than one master layout repeated six times, which is the single most visible tell in generated landing pages. And the packaging is honest — splitting the repo so it does one job, keeping install names stable, MIT, and telling you plainly which skills need an image-capable agent.

**Pros:**
- Puts composition upstream of code, which is the actual mechanism behind "looks art-directed" versus "looks generated."
- Section-by-section comps produce deliberate variety instead of a hero repeated as six feature blocks.
- `brandkit` as a skill implies a disciplined starting artifact — palette, type, application boards — that can be handed to the build step.
- MIT, no dependencies, works across Claude Code and Codex via plugin marketplaces; install names stable for anyone migrating from taste-skill.

**Cons:**
- **Three stars, zero forks, four days old, one author.** There is no evidence anyone has built a site from this and been happy with it.
- Generated comps are images — so the "design" that gets built toward is a *picture of a design*, with all of a picture's illegibility at small sizes and none of a real type system's behavior.
- It cannot help with the thing that makes our work premium: a committed palette, a single face, a grid. Those come from a system, and a skill that generates guideline boards can generate the *wrong* guideline boards just as fluently.
- The README describes the skills in one sentence each and publishes no example output, so the craft claims are entirely unverified.
- Image-then-code means two generation budgets and two failure points; when the build doesn't match the comp, the mismatch reads worse than a merely generic page.

**Should we...**
- 👀 **Watch it** — promising, not proven yet
**Why:** Endorse the **order of operations** immediately even though the tool is unproven: for any new page — the residency landing page, an interview-season splash, a campaign microsite — produce the composition as an image *before* writing markup, then build to it, and judge the build against the comp. That single sequencing change is cheaper than any tool and it kills the section-by-section sameness that makes generated pages obvious. Then run it through the Desk Studio proof gate when it exists, so the build is measured against rendered pixels rather than the comp's prettiness. Do not install the `brandkit` skill into any prompt: a generated brand board is a slot machine producing a palette and a type pairing, and we already have both, deliberately chosen (navy, single accent, Inter, SVG icons on the platform; light #f5f5f0 and real photography for campaign). Watch rather than adopt because the repo has no evidence behind it and because the useful half — compose-then-build — is a discipline we can adopt today for free without the package.

### paraframe — your Svelte components as prototype frames, on a canvas and in a player

**Link:** https://github.com/Convex-Works/paraframe
**Type:** Tool (MIT, SvelteKit 3 / Svelte 5)
**What it is:** A **read-only viewer for UI frames, in the style of a design tool's prototype preview** (v0.2.0 released 2026-10-21, actively committed — latest commits today). You write each frame as a **Svelte component**, and paraframe lays them out on a pan-and-zoom canvas in sections and plays them one at a time in a **player** scaled to the window, with named flows as starting points. Because frames are **real components in iframes**, each renders at its own size with its own styles, media queries and fixed elements, isolated from paraframe's chrome, and anything inside stays interactive. Also: **frame links** (links, hotspots on any element, navigation from code), optional **static previews** at overview scale that switch to live frames as you zoom, optional pinned **comments** on elements via `visual-comments`, and `--pf-*` custom properties for theming the chrome. Requires SvelteKit 3 and Svelte 5; chrome ships as **plain compiled CSS (18.6 kB, 4.6 kB gzipped)** so the host app needs no Tailwind. One-command quickstart, a live showcase at paraframe.convex.works.

**What's good about it:** The core idea is correct and overdue: **the prototype and the product should be the same artifact.** A Figma prototype is a picture that lies — it drifts from the build the moment engineering starts, and it can't hold real data, real routing, or a real 44px tap target. Making each frame an actual component in an iframe means the prototype *is* the site at a different address, which eliminates the handoff gap entirely. The isolation detail is the engineering that makes it work: each frame gets its own viewport, media queries and fixed elements, so a mobile frame at 390px actually behaves like 390px instead of being a shrunken desktop. Comments pinned to elements, reviewable by a visitor in a browser, is genuinely how a department should review a page — and the release discipline (PR-driven CI, images pinned by digest, `/version` reporting the commit so a deploy can be verified) is more rigorous than most design tools.

**Pros:**
- The prototype is the build, so there is no drift between what was approved and what shipped.
- Real component isolation per frame means responsive behaviour is tested, not approximated.
- Comments pinned to elements inside live frames turn a review into something a non-designer can do in a browser link.
- MIT, small, no runtime dependency on a proprietary canvas, and the chrome theming means it can look like our surface rather than like a tool.
- Remarkably disciplined release engineering for a 2★ repo; verifiable deploys.

**Cons:**
- **It solves a problem we don't have.** Our Unified Platform is React, not Svelte, and our review process already runs against live deployed previews — this adds a viewer on top of a pipeline that works.
- 2★ and one contributor drives it; the "team" in the commit history is an AI co-author. Adopting it means owning a SvelteKit 3 + Svelte 5 dependency for a README-level feature set.
- A read-only viewer can't do the thing design tools exist for — it can't show you an idea you haven't built. It's a presentation layer for work that already exists.
- Requires every reviewer to be comfortable opening a dev-server URL and reading Svelte routes; that's a small internal audience, not the department.
- All the craft is in the chrome and the iframe mechanics — nothing here improves the taste of what's inside a frame.

**Should we...**
- ❌ **Skip it** — slop-adjacent, hype, or redundant
**Why:** Judge it as a design director, not an engineer: it's a nicer place to *look at* work, and looking at work was never the bottleneck. When a page needed four attempts to look right, the failure was in the composition decisions, and no viewer changes those. There's one idea worth remembering for the day we do need it — **prototype frames should be real components, because a picture of an interface is a promise the build usually breaks** — and if we ever present the residency site or an interview-season flow for approval, a link that renders real components at real viewport sizes with pinned comments is meaningfully better than a Figma link. But we can do that today by deploying a branch and sending a URL, which is what we already do. Skip it, and note the release engineering as a small model of how a design-adjacent tool should ship.

Every one of these tools produces the same three findings regardless of input, because they are the critique-equivalents of "make it pop." A critique that cannot fail you is not a critique — which is exactly why Desk Studio's *pass/fail gate* matters and the *review chat* doesn't.

## Overall Assessment

Today's brief is the first in two weeks where not one item came from the tool layer's newest thing — and that is the point. Desk Studio is a tool, but what makes it worth adopting is a **gate that can fail you**, not a model that generates better. Pentagram and Day Job are both about **systems that absorb variation instead of resisting it**: one gives you a bilingual identity where neither script is subordinate by building the parity into the stroke, the other makes twenty wordmarks a feature by defining a family instead of a lockup. Those two are the practical answers to the two questions this brief has been asking since Oct 20 — how do we stop ranking audiences, and how does an identity survive a new cohort without a redesign. Meanwhile the market data says the same thing from the hiring side: four agencies bought strategists this month, nobody bought an AI specialist, and the one unapologetically AI-first group hired a legendary human creative to sit at the top. So where is AI-assisted design heading? Toward **verification**, and away from generation. The scarce thing is no longer an asset — it's a gate, a rule, and a receipt. **The single highest-leverage move for Shareef this week is to make one of our surfaces pass a gate it currently cannot fail.** Take the residency campaign's next social flight and, before generating anything, write the three (not twenty) sanctioned variants, the two-register rule from Pentagram, and the eight axes from Desk Studio as an explicit checklist — then run the output through a real pixel proof for contrast, safe zones and type-scale membership, and accept that the first two rounds will be rejected. Everything else in this brief is a candidate for the same treatment later; this week is about proving the gate exists.
