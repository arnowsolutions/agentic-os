### Antislop
**Link:** https://github.com/richyrach/antislop · field guide: https://richyrach.github.io/antislop/ · created **12 November 2026** · MIT · 3 stars, 0 forks, 1 release · by richyrach
**Type:** Technique (a written method plus a measured eval)
**What it is:** Six Markdown files an AI reads before it designs anything, installable as a skill (`npx skills add richyrach/antislop`) or pasteable as a single raw URL into ChatGPT, Claude, Cursor, Codex or Gemini. The claim is backed by a run: **ten one-line requests** ("I need a website for my family's bakery in Lisbon"), each sent to Claude Sonnet twice — once alone, once with the guide — then a script counted **27 known marks of AI-built pages** in each result.

**What's good about it:** The numbers are small but they are counts, not opinions. Marks per page averaged **4.4 without the guide and 1.3 with it**; pages carrying four or more marks went **7 of 10 → 0 of 10**; pages with invented proof (reviews, founding dates, customer counts) went **3 of 10 → 0 of 10**; pages using only the default sans-serif went **8 of 10 → 0 of 10**. What makes the repo worth reading, though, is not the table — it's that the author published his own failure mode. The guide's first "good" example was a cream page with a serif, a red accent and a line of facts joined by dots. That is not a solution; **that is the second AI archetype**, and he now ships it as plate 2 of the field guide. This is the single most useful thing in the whole anti-slop genre: the field guide's four plates are not one bad page and three good ones, they are **four kinds of generated page** — purple SaaS landing, cream editorial, black-and-green developer page, dashboard of stat cards — and the second and third exist *because people told their AI to avoid the first*. "A model that is only told what to avoid moves to its next most likely answer, and that answer is the same for everyone." The replacement is an eight-step method, and step 4 is the one I'd steal for the desk: **"Swap in a competitor's name. If the design still fits, start again."** That is a professional art-direction test, not a filter. Two engineering details also punch above their weight for a healthcare platform: the page must render with **every third-party host unreachable** (a blocking font CDN is a blank page on a filtered hospital network), and RTL / non-Latin script handling with logical properties and no letter-spacing on Arabic.
**Pros:**
- The eval is reproducible and the method is disclosed — ten fixed prompts, one model, a script in the repo, per-page outputs preserved in `evals/out/sonnet`, and the pre-revision guide kept in `evals/history/guide-v1`.
- It publishes its own weaknesses in a section called "Read this with care": ten requests and one model, the guide and the counter share an author, "part of the gap is the guide teaching to its own test," and a count of marks "does not say a page is good."
- The residual failures are documented, which is rarer than the successes: five of ten guided pages still had small labels in spaced capitals, two still used cream paper with a serif.
- Step 4 — swap in a competitor's name — is a **falsifiable** test a non-designer can run on a draft, which makes it usable as a gate rather than a taste argument.
- Nothing to install and nothing to pay, so it can live in a repo as plain text next to `CLAUDE.md` or `AGENTS.md`.
**Cons:**
- The metric rewards absence. The author says it plainly: the first guide "produced pages that avoided every mark and had nothing on them." A low mark count is compatible with a bad page, and the guide was revised once for exactly this reason.
- `evals/detect.mjs` reads source text only and was written for the test — it is not a scanner. Do not wire it into CI and believe it.
- One model, one run, ten prompts, English only. No GPT, no Gemini, no measurement of whether the guided pages *converted* or read better to a human who wasn't counting.
- Three stars and one author. There is no community vetting, no issue history to read for caveats, and no evidence anyone outside the repo has rerun it.
- It competes with larger, better-funded packs (impeccable, Taste Skill, Hallmark) that ship actual scanners and ready-made themes. Antislop is a ten-minute read, and a ten-minute read is all it is.
**Should we...**
- ✅ **Adopt it** — use it now; it raises our design ceiling
**Why:** This is the only anti-slop artifact I've seen this month that a design director can hand to a writer or an admin without translation, and the reason is step 4. Everything else in the genre is a ban list, and a ban list produces plate 2 — which is precisely what the Sub-I welcome packet and the residency social templates are at risk of becoming the moment anyone starts prompting them. The concrete move for Shareef: put `GUIDE.md` in the repo behind the Sub-I materials and run it *before* the first draft, then apply its competitor test to what comes back — if the same welcome page could carry another department's logo and still make sense, it hasn't been art-directed, it's been generated. The two engineering points (render with third-party hosts blocked; no letter-spacing on non-Latin script) also matter more than they look: Montefiore's network filters third-party CDNs, and a welcome page whose font never arrives is the first impression.

### Wensity Design Skills
**Link:** https://github.com/wensity/skills · guides: https://ui.wensity.com/skills · created **10 November 2026** · MIT · 5 stars, 0 releases · 3 of 13 skills free, 10 behind Wensity Pro
**Type:** Tool (agent skill pack, Agent Skills format)
**What it is:** Three free `SKILL.md` folders — `wensity-typography`, `wensity-layout`, `wensity-ui-copy` — that install into Claude Code, Codex, Cursor, Copilot, Windsurf, Gemini CLI or OpenCode and load themselves when a request matches. The paid pack adds ten more, including a "design director" router, motion, colour/dark mode, surfaces, accessibility states, landing pages, dashboard UI, UI review and a `DESIGN.md` generator.

**What's good about it:** The typography skill is written the way a type-aware designer actually specifies type, which is to say in numbers and roles rather than adjectives. **Seven text roles** — display, title, heading, body, label, caption, code — with separate values for marketing pages and for dense app UI, wired into Tailwind. Line height drops as text grows; large text is tracked tighter while body is left alone; paragraphs held to **60 to 75 characters**; balanced headings and pretty-wrapped paragraphs; **truncation as a last resort**; **tabular figures for anything that counts or lines up**. And the font-loading rule is the one that separates a professional build from a demo: `next/font` in Next.js, or self-hosted WOFF2 with a **matched fallback** so the page doesn't jump when the font arrives. The layout skill is the same discipline applied to space — one 4px scale where each step has a job "so values like 13px never show up," grouping by distance, more space above a heading than below, sections that don't all share one gap, one start edge per section, numbers aligned at the end, 44px touch targets, container queries, `dvh`, safe areas. The UX-writing skill covers the parts of copy that get skipped: error messages that say what went wrong and how to fix it, empty states that say what to do next, toasts in the past tense, **Undo in place of a confirm dialog**, and realistic names and data instead of lorem ipsum.
**Pros:**
- Specific enough to be checkable — "60 to 75 characters," "4px scale," "line height drops as text grows," "tabular figures for anything that counts." Most of this genre ships vibes.
- It explicitly keeps an existing design system rather than replacing it, which is the only acceptable behaviour for a platform that already has Inter, a single accent and a dark premium surface.
- Works across seven agents from one folder, and installs per-skill (`--skill wensity-typography`) so you can take the good one and leave the rest.
- The font-loading and fallback-metric guidance is the difference between a page that renders instantly and one that flashes its fallback — a detail almost no AI design tool mentions.
**Cons:**
- It is a funnel. Ten of thirteen skills sit behind a Wensity Pro subscription, including the "design director" router that would be the most interesting one to read, and the repo is regenerated from Wensity's source on every release so contributions don't stick.
- **No eval at all.** Unlike Antislop, there is no before/after, no counted tells, no per-page output. The rules are plausible; they are not demonstrated.
- 5 stars and zero releases. It is four days old and no one has stress-tested it in a real codebase.
- The three free skills are the three easiest to write. Typography, spacing and microcopy are exactly where a competent designer already knows the answer — the hard ones (motion, states, colour in dark mode) are the paid ones.
- The UX-writing "words that sound generated" list is the same list that appears in every pack published since September, which is itself a sign the category is converging.
**Should we...**
- ✅ **Adopt it** — use it now; it raises our design ceiling
**Why:** Take the typography skill and nothing else. The Unified Platform already has the hard decisions made — Inter, one accent, a dark premium surface, SVG icons — so a system generator has nothing to add and plenty to break. What it does *not* have written down is the role scale: which size is a title and which is a heading, where the line height steps down, whether the numbers in a schedule table are tabular (they should be — a call schedule is a column of times that must line up), and what the fallback metrics are so the page doesn't reflow when Inter lands. That is a two-hour job with this skill installed and it is invisible work that reads as expensive. The same logic applies to the Sub-I packet: 60–75 characters per measure is the difference between a page that looks typeset and one that looks like a Google Doc with a logo on it.

### OpenDesigner
**Link:** https://github.com/ckryptickunal/OpenDesigner · docs: https://opendesigner.dev/docs/install · surfaced on X by **@DanKornas, 8 November 2026** (link: https://x.com/DanKornas/status/2119375870347370803) · MIT · no tagged release
**Type:** Tool (agent skill pack + a Python engine that writes and checks a design system)
**What it is:** An add-on for the AI tool you already use — Claude Code, the Claude app, ChatGPT Projects, Codex, Cursor, Copilot, Gemini CLI. It reads your project first, asks **up to five questions** with a recommended answer and a reason for each, and writes `DESIGN.md`, `PRODUCT.md` and an `opendesigner/` folder containing **eleven token files** (primitives, semantic roles, modes for light/dark, three densities, standard/reduced motion, and a resolver), CSS variables, a Tailwind v4 theme, DTCG tokens, Figma variables, Swift and Jetpack Compose exports, a decision log, and `overview.html` — a playground page showing every colour in both modes with its contrast ratio.

**What's good about it:** Two mechanisms, and they are the two that matter. First, **`engine.py review`** — Claude Code's own documentation concedes that `CLAUDE.md` and friends are "context, not enforced configuration," so the skill tells the model to run a review at the end of each piece of work, and the review prints what to fix, name for name: `app.css:2  #4f46e5  ->  use var(--ds-color-bg-action-primary)`, `border-radius: 7px  ->  use var(--ds-radius-control) (6px, the control radius)`. Second, **contrast stops the build** — every text, border, icon and focus-ring pair the tokens promise is checked in light and dark, including text on hover and pressed fills, and a pair under **4.5:1 for text or 3:1 for borders, icons and focus rings fails the build** rather than warning. That is enforcement rather than description, which is exactly the gap yesterday's Supernova test opened: colours and components travelled from a token file into generated UI, **spacing did not**, because spacing arrived as a judgement the model felt entitled to make by eye. A checker is the answer to that finding and this is a checker. The engine is also one Python file using only the standard library, makes **no network calls**, needs no account, and runs standalone — `python3 engine.py sketch --name "Acme" --feel friendly,minimal` produces the same output with no AI in the loop at all.
**Pros:**
- The review pass names the replacement token, not just the violation — which is the difference between a linter that gets ignored and one that gets fixed.
- Contrast failures block rather than warn, in both themes, including hover and pressed states. Almost nothing else in this category checks states.
- Project-aware setup: it reads CSS, Tailwind config, `package.json` and token files first and "doesn't ask what they already answer."
- Genuinely portable output — DTCG, CSS variables, Tailwind, Figma variables, Swift, Compose — plus a decision log, which is the artifact design systems usually lose.
- No network, no account, MIT, and the engine has a no-AI mode, so the tokens can be regenerated reproducibly.
**Cons:**
- **No tagged release.** It is four days old on the repo front and the vendor says so on X: "It's early: the README notes there's no tagged release yet."
- The documentation contradicts itself at week one — the install page says the plugin "installs eight skills," the Claude Code page says "nine." Small, but it is the kind of drift that becomes a support problem.
- The generator's core job is to **invent** a system from five answers. For a platform whose look is already locked and defended, that is the wrong operation, and the risk is a confident, competent, generic system that quietly overwrites deliberate choices.
- Its own docs concede the central weakness: the `DESIGN.md` note is "context, not enforced configuration," so everything depends on the model choosing to run the review. The enforcement is real only when a human or CI runs it.
- Sixth entrant in eight days. Brandrail shipped a comparable `validate_snippet` brand-score checker on 6 November; wensity, impeccable, Taste Skill, Hallmark, Cards and ai-ready-design-system all occupy the same shelf and each will tell you the others are unnecessary.
**Should we...**
- 👀 **Watch it** — promising, not proven yet
**Why:** The right use of this repo is the opposite of its pitch, and that is worth being precise about. Don't run `sketch` against the Unified Platform — a generator asked to produce a design system will produce a defensible one, and defensible is how a premium dark interface with one accent becomes a competent dark interface with two. Run `engine.py review` against the existing code instead: it will list every hard-coded hex, every 7px radius, every duration that isn't a token, and name what should have been used, and that list is a real audit that has never been run on this codebase. The contrast gate is the second thing to take, because a dark UI at low elevation is exactly where 3:1 borders and focus rings fail silently and nobody notices until someone can't see the field they're tabbing into. Watch the release tag before wiring any of it into CI — but the review output is worth reading this week.

### Editmamei
**Link:** https://github.com/editmamei/editmamei · https://editmamei.com · **v1.10.2 published 12 November 2026** (v1.10.1 on the 11th, v1.9.7 on the 9th) · FSL-1.1-MIT · 18 stars, 4 forks, 22 releases · created 5 August 2026
**Type:** Tool (desktop MCP server driving Photoshop and GIMP)
**What it is:** A local MCP server that lets Claude (Desktop, Code, Cursor or any MCP client) operate Photoshop 2026 or GIMP 3.2 with the editor's own tools, layer by layer, and hand back an editable layered file. **112 tools** — 83 for Photoshop (62 Community, 21 Pro) and 29 for GIMP. It generates **no pixels**: when an edit uses Sky Replacement, that is Photoshop's tool doing the work. Adjustments land as adjustment layers, destructive operations run on an automatic duplicate, GIMP adjustments stay live filters, and the model checks its own result with histograms, region comparisons and previews.

**What's good about it:** It attacks the actual failure of AI creative in a real pipeline, which is not quality — it's that the output is a flat JPEG. The three worked examples are the pitch: a sky replacement on a waterfall with instructions to warm the foreground so the light matches the new sky and then "check the horizon at full size for halos or leftover patches of the old sky" (about 8 minutes); a beach photo graded to a "bright, airy summer-afternoon look" with the sand lifted and the umbrella's colours pushed "without looking fake" (about 6); and one photo delivered as an Instagram post, an Instagram story **with room at the top and bottom for text**, and an 8x10 print, "keeping the puppy's face and both ears in frame in all three" (about 4). There is a real run of **73 travel photos graded from one sentence**, three at a time, no generated pixels. On-device ONNX models handle face, object and scene detection, so "the person on the left" works without a cloud vision call. Two things are genuinely design-director-grade: the constraints are phrased as a brief ("keep the waterfall, the cliffs and the spray exactly as they are"), and the model is expected to **verify** rather than assume — histograms, region comparisons, and selection previews exist so it can measure its own work.
**Pros:**
- The output stays editable. Adjustment layers, masks, live filters and named layers mean the file survives contact with a human, which no generated PNG does.
- It is a retoucher's assistant, not a designer's replacement — the framing is explicitly "do the parts of the edit you don't want to, keep the final touches for yourself."
- Verification is built into the loop (histograms, previews, region comparisons) instead of hoped for, and the ship rule is that "a new tool stays out of the tool list until it has passed live sessions against real Photoshop or GIMP."
- Batch work is where the hours actually go, and it does batch: one sentence, 73 photos, consistent grading.
- Source-available with the Community stack developed in the open, and it says so honestly — "Editmamei is not open source under the OSI definition, and we don't call it that."
**Cons:**
- **Four to eight minutes per edit, and every minute spends your tokens** — the server ships no model of its own. For a 60-image faculty headshot pass that is a working day of waiting and a meaningful bill.
- Hard requirements: Photoshop 2026 (v27.x) on Windows 10/11 or macOS 13+, or GIMP 3.2. No browser route to the desktop product.
- The FSL licence holds back competing commercial use for two years per release. Fine for internal work, an obstacle if any of this ever becomes something Shareef sells.
- Every example is the vendor's own, unverified, and all three are **photographic grading**. There is no evidence it composes a Sub-I welcome page, a poster or a social grid — the layout work is still yours.
- GIMP coverage is thinner than Photoshop's (no heal/clone, no AI subject or sky selection), and the Pro split is Photoshop-only, so the free path is narrower than the tool count implies.
**Should we...**
- 👀 **Watch it** — promising, not proven yet
**Why:** The idea here is the one that defeats slop structurally: an AI that operates professional tools leaves professional artifacts — layers, masks, adjustment stacks — so a human can take over at any point and the file never becomes an unrecoverable flat image. That is the opposite of asking a model for "a photo of a urologist in a bright corridor," which is how marketing libraries fill up with images that can't be colour-corrected, can't be cropped for print, and can't be matched to the next photo in the set. Where it actually fits Shareef's work is the faculty photo library and the 28 interview clips: consistent grading across a batch, three crops per portrait with the face and shoulders preserved, and an 8x10 that doesn't clip a head. Watch it because 4–8 minutes a photo is real money and the vendor's examples are the vendor's examples — but the first worthwhile test isn't a waterfall, it's a 12-image faculty batch graded to one sentence.

### Ignition Works — Studio Up North for Kirklees Council
**Link:** https://www.designweek.co.uk/studio-up-north-creates-place-brand-for-kirklees-councils-ignition-works/ · published **13 November 2026** · Design Week · studio: https://www.sun-design.co.uk/ · site: https://businesskirklees.com/ignition-works/
**Type:** Idea (a brand system worth studying, not a tool)
**What it is:** A strategic naming, positioning and identity system for a **seven-acre** regeneration site on Gasworks Street in Huddersfield, positioned for health research and advanced manufacturing inside the West Yorkshire Investment Zone. The organising idea is a spark — "the spark that once powered the ambitions of an industrial town" as a metaphor for discovery and commercial impact — carried as the line "One spark. Endless possibilities," with creative direction by Jamie Kelly.

**What's good about it:** The system is genuinely systematic, and it shows in the application list rather than the logo. One motif — a thin circular lens, sometimes concentric rings, sometimes a dot-matrix field — scales from a security access card to a bus livery to a sheet of postage stamps to window graphics on a red-brick building, and it does so without becoming wallpaper because the scale and density change with the substrate. The best move in the whole set is what sits **inside** the letterforms and the lens: histology slides, cobalt cellular spheres, molecular webs, golden technical illustrations of robotic arms, dot-matrix silhouettes of a scientist at a microscope. That is the difference between an identity built on real material and one built on a gradient — the science imagery is the client's own subject matter, not decoration borrowed from a stock library. The two-division split is also properly designed rather than merely labelled: **royal blue for Advanced Manufacturing, amber for Life Sciences**, same system, one colour axis. And the ID card is a lovely piece of thinking — "JOSEPH KAYE / ADVANCED MANUFACTURING, Staff Number: 005," a historical figure given a modern staff card, which makes the past feel like an employee rather than a plaque.
**Pros:**
- One motif, fifteen-plus surfaces, no loss of coherence — access cards, ID cards and lanyards, a stamp sheet, a bus wrap, a tote, a bucket hat, a café tee ("EVEN INNOVATORS NEED FUEL" with a photographed doughnut), visitor leaflets in two palettes, a brochure with proper editorial spreads, event walls, bus-shelter posters, window graphics.
- The imagery is **the subject's own material** — microscopy, cell render, histology — set inside type rather than dropped beside it. That is art direction, and it is the exact opposite of the abstract-blob default.
- Real typographic and layout craft in the brochure: multi-column editorial copy on black, circular image callouts, an actual executive summary spread rather than a deck printed sideways.
- A place brand for a health-research campus is directly adjacent to Shareef's problem — institutional, science-literate, needs to work in print, environment and screen.
**Cons:**
- "One spark. Endless possibilities" is the slogan the concept writes for itself, and it fails the swap test: substitute any innovation district, tech park or enterprise zone and it still works. The metaphor is doing all the work and the words are interchangeable.
- The volume reads as portfolio breadth — a bucket hat and a stamp sheet for a site that hasn't opened — and breadth is not the same as restraint.
- A research-campus identity that leans on glow, particle fields and luminous cellular orbs is standing close to the visual vocabulary of AI-generated imagery, which is a strange place for a brand whose whole claim is that it's real.
- The case study is written by the studio's own account of its own strategic brilliance; there is no client voice, no measurement, and no evidence the system survives a developer's PowerPoint.

**Should we...**
- ✅ **Adopt it** — use it now; it raises our design ceiling
**Why:** Not the look — the move. The transferable rule is *one motif, deliberately scaled, filled with the client's own material, and split by division rather than by whim*, and it maps onto the Sub-I welcome materials almost line for line. Those materials currently have to do the same job this brand does: make an institutional programme feel like a place with a point of view, work in print and on a phone, and hold together across a dozen pieces. The specific technique to steal is the circular lens with the subject inside it — a ring that frames a microscopy image, a faculty portrait, a procedure still — because it gives every piece a common container without repeating a layout. The two-palette split is the second steal: residency track versus research track, one system, one axis of difference. What to refuse is the slogan, and the honesty test is Antislop's step 4 — if "one spark, endless possibilities" works for the cardiology fellowship too, it isn't a line, it's a template.

### Springetts — "How Brands Live Forever"
**Link:** https://www.creativeboom.com/insight/what-50-years-in-business-has-taught-springetts-about-building-brands-that-thrive/ · published **12 November 2026** · Creative Boom · guide at https://springetts.co.uk/ · agency founded 1976
**Type:** Idea (a published framework)
**What it is:** A UK brand and packaging agency marking fifty years — Weetabix, Walkers, Ryvita, Kettle Chips, McVitie's, Rubicon, and brands it coined itself (Frubes, Frijj, The Happy Egg Co., Nuii), 24 Pentawards and 19 Design Effectiveness Awards — by publishing a field guide called *How Brands Live Forever*. The framework is four life stages: **Launch** (make an entrance), **Grow** (build momentum), **Extend** (gain new ground), **Renew** (reclaim relevance). Launch breaks down into four principles: **start with a point of view, build memory blocks, bend category codes, design for becoming, not just being.**

**What's good about it:** "Bend category codes" is the most useful two words in anything I read this week. The distinction it draws — bend, not break — is the whole craft of recognition: break the code and you're a stranger; obey it and you're invisible; bend it and you're new and legible at once. That is a design principle with a testable edge, and it is the exact opposite of what AI generation does by default, because a model trained on a category reproduces the category's centre, not its edge. "Design for becoming, not just being" is the second: a launch identity needs room to grow into channels, products and audiences that don't exist yet, which is a systems requirement rather than an aesthetic one. "Build memory blocks" turns brand consistency into an inventory problem — what are the three or four units a person actually remembers — instead of a style-guide problem. And the framing sentence is the correct one for 2026: *design for longevity rather than novelty*, which "involves, according to the agency, a level of restraint." Sophie Broderick's line on renewal also earns its place: "The danger for legacy brands is not age, it's invisibility."
**Pros:**
- "Bend category codes" is immediately usable as a critique question on any draft: which code are we obeying, and where are we bending it — or are we just obeying?
- "Design for becoming" is a systems-thinking principle with a practical consequence (leave the identity room it will need in eighteen months).
- It's a food-and-drink packaging discipline applied over fifty years, which is the hardest possible school for longevity — packaging has to work at 200mm and at 2 metres, in print and on shelf.
- The evidence is unusually concrete for this genre: 24 Pentawards and 19 Design Effectiveness Awards, and named brands a reader can look at.
**Cons:**
- It is a **relaunch marketing asset**. The framework arrives alongside a new positioning and a refreshed identity, so it should be read as a pitch with a bibliography, not a piece of research.
- Four stages with four principles each is a consulting diagram. The metaphor is doing more work than the evidence — the article cites "85% of new brands fail" with no source, and no part of the framework is tested against a brand that followed it and lost.
- It's written by the people it flatters. The piece is largely the agency describing its own culture ("fiercely designer-led... without the usual layers of account management"), which is interesting but is not a method.
- The horizon is fifty years. A department's recruitment cycle is eighteen months and a fellowship's is four years; the lifespan rules don't transfer cleanly, and neither does the shelf logic of packaging.
**Should we...**
- 👀 **Watch it** — promising, not proven yet
**Why:** Worth having because two of its four launch principles are diagnostic questions Shareef can ask this week, and not worth adopting wholesale because it's an agency's anniversary present to itself. The residency programme is, in this framework's terms, permanently stuck in **Launch** — a new cohort every year, all of them needing to be "instantly understood, emotionally felt and easy to remember" — and the failure mode of a programme that relaunches annually is novelty-chasing, which is exactly the slop pressure the whole brief is about. "Bend the category code" is the corrective: every urology residency in the country uses the same blue-gradient banner, the same smiling-resident-in-a-corridor photo, the same "training the future of..." headline. Bending that code — one deliberate violation, held consistently — is a marketing decision Shareef can actually make, and it costs nothing.

### Mitch Bernstein's first-week field notes on AI in design
**Link:** https://x.com/MitchBernstein/status/2120898673638862990 · posted **12 November 2026** · 43 likes, 2,766 views, 8 replies · reply worth reading: @TheoKode_, 13 November (https://x.com/TheoKode_/status/2121331174593474777)
**Type:** Idea (practitioner field notes)
**What it is:** A designer one week into a role at a native-AI company (@owner) writing down what he thinks AI is actually doing to design work — not a manifesto, in his own framing, but "thoughts that will leak from my head and into X." The framing he borrows from a colleague: with AI, **engineers get something like 100x, PMs and managers maybe 10x, and designers more like 5x** — and the struggle now is scaling designers, not engineers.

**What's good about it:** The best sentence written about the human-AI design loop this month is in here: **"AI gets you to 80% fast. The last 20% is the hardest part, and right now it feels like doing it over a walkie-talkie: you describe, it guesses, you correct, it guesses again."** That is the whole experience of art-directing a generated screen, described without a single buzzword, and it explains why the percentage of AI-assisted design that ships is so much lower than the percentage that demos. The diagnosis that follows is the right one and it is not the usual one: engineers got faster because **their output is functionality**, and models are extremely good at functionality; designers' output isn't, and the answer is **systems thinking** — a design system plus a coded component library lets you "replicate the utilitarian parts of a product really fast. What that doesn't give you is the human part... experiences and taste." Hence the conclusion that matters: designers "have to pause and iterate on the specific areas that need humanity, and that has to be baked into the process, not tacked on at the end." The reply that improves on the post is from @TheoKode_ on the 13th: what shrinks the walkie-talkie loop is **correcting by pointing instead of describing** — reference the coded component, or drop a marked-up screenshot. That is a technique, it's free, and it works today.
**Pros:**
- The 80/20 walkie-talkie line is a precise, portable description of the failure mode — useful language for a team that keeps asking why the generated screen "looks fine" but isn't shippable.
- It separates where AI is genuinely strong (exploration: functional prototypes in front of users before implementation, many variations at once so you're "choosing between paths instead of betting on one") from where it's weak, instead of arguing about the whole category.
- The reframing is useful for a manager: the question moves from "can we build it and how long will it take" to "what should we build, and why" — but **far earlier**, which changes what a design review is for.
- The reply thread adds the practical fix (point at the component, don't describe it), which is more actionable than most standalone articles.
**Cons:**
- It is week-one orientation, not a method: "had to abstract the artifacts cause IP and traded secrets," so there is nothing to look at, only assertions.
- The 100x / 10x / 5x numbers are secondhand from a colleague, round, and unfalsifiable. They are a useful organising metaphor and should never be quoted as data.
- 43 likes is not a stress test — this is one designer's honest impression, and the thread's own replies are mostly congratulations.
- "Protect the parts that should feel human" is the correct instinct and gives no rule for deciding which parts those are, which is the only hard question.
**Should we...**
- 👀 **Watch it** — promising, not proven yet
**Why:** Nothing to install, but one thing to take immediately, and it's in the reply rather than the post: when a generated screen comes back wrong, stop writing a paragraph about what's wrong and point at the artifact — the component in the coded library, or a screenshot with the offending spacing circled. That single change cuts the guess-correct loop, and it's a workflow Shareef's team can adopt without buying anything. The rest of the post is the correct diagnosis of why the Unified Platform can't be handed to an agent wholesale: the utilitarian parts (tables, forms, settings, states) are exactly what a design system plus a coded component library already solves, and the parts that need a human — the hierarchy of a schedule screen at 7am on a phone, the tone of a call-out confirmation — are the parts worth a designer's afternoon. Watch it as language to use in a review, not as a strategy.

### IDEO on when AI makes a product worse
**Link:** https://spectrum.ieee.org/human-centered-ai-product-design · published **12 November 2026** · IEEE Spectrum · interview with **Savannah Kunovsky**, who leads industrial product design at IDEO
**Type:** Idea (an interview)
**What it is:** A conversation about the pressure to put AI into every physical product, when that integration makes things worse, and how IDEO decides. The centrepiece anecdote: a client wanted a cooktop with **no knobs** — you tell the AI what you're cooking and it manages the heat. In testing the pan started smoking, the user reached for a knob that wasn't there, said "turn it down" twice, and pulled the pan off the burner — all before the AI responded. They kept the knobs.

**What's good about it:** The cooktop story is a genuinely excellent piece of evidence, and it generalises cleanly: "anything you need to do instantly, by feel, or under stress is usually best kept physical — a volume knob, a light switch, a stove burner, an emergency stop." The gimmick test is also stated without hedging — "does it help someone do what they're trying to do?" — and the Humane AI Pin gets an honest post-mortem as a product that "asked people to give up an interface they knew well for one that was slower and less reliable." The most transferable passage for interface work is about non-determinism: with generative AI "you can't specify every output, so you define the space the product should operate in," and therefore **evals and constitutions have become a new form of product definition** — "getting that right is now core design work, not just an engineering step at the end." The rules on override are the ones I'd underline for any AI feature in a clinical workflow: manual pathways should be "obvious, fast, and available in the moment you need them, not buried in a settings menu."
**Pros:**
- One concrete, memorable failure case (the smoking pan) that makes the abstract argument stick in a stakeholder meeting.
- The non-determinism passage reframes evals as a **design** artifact rather than a QA artifact, which is a genuinely useful organisational argument.
- Euro NCAP now rewarding physical controls for cars is a good external validation of the "keep it physical" rule.
- The override principle — obvious, fast, available in the moment, not in settings — is written as a constraint a team can design against.
**Cons:**
- It is **industrial product design** — knobs, cooktops, cars, hearing aids — and the transfer to software interfaces is assumed, not demonstrated. A settings menu is not a burner.
- IDEO is answering as a consultancy that sells exactly this discipline; there is no failed engagement, no cost, no measurement anywhere in the piece.
- Nothing is quantified: no before-and-after, no failure rates, no test protocol. "We tested it" is as specific as the evidence gets.
- The strongest lines are ones any senior designer already believes. That makes it good confirmation and thin intelligence — the brief's job is to move the ceiling, and this doesn't.
**Should we...**
- ❌ **Skip it** — slop-adjacent, hype, or redundant
**Why:** Not because it's wrong — because it's already the house position. Shareef's platform is deliberately conventional where convention serves the user: a call-out is a form with a submit button, not a conversation with an assistant, and that decision has already been made correctly. The one thing worth carrying out of this interview is the evals-as-product-definition line, and it belongs in a design review of the call-out flow rather than in an intel brief. Quoting the cooktop in a meeting about a dashboard would be a category error, and the brief's job is to keep that line clean.

## Slop Radar

- **Plate 2 is now the slop.** The cream page, the editorial serif, the single red accent, the dot-separated fact line — that is what you get when you tell a model to avoid the purple gradient. Antislop's own first "good" example became the second bad archetype in its own field guide. If your anti-AI look is a look, it's a template too.
- **The design-skill pack is the new template farm.** Eight days produced antislop, Wensity, OpenDesigner, Brandrail, Cards and ai-ready-design-system, alongside the existing impeccable, Taste Skill and Hallmark — and each one's README argues the others are unnecessary while shipping the same spaced-caps eyebrow and 4px scale. Installing a pack is not the same as having a point of view; the packs that will matter are the ones that publish a measurement, and so far that's one.
- **Convergence, not "the AI look."** The tell has moved. From r/graphic_design, in a thread I could retrieve the body of but not the date: *"Claude has this thing where all of its branding and UI looks the same. Not in a 'hmm I can tell this is AI' way. More in a, Montserrat, same corner radius, same button design and text styling for everything."* The detection method is no longer "does this look generated" but "have I seen this exact component decision twice this week" — and the second question is the one that catches work you're about to ship. (Pattern only; not judged as an item because the thread carries no retrievable date.)
- **Slogan symmetry on institutional brands.** "One spark. Endless possibilities." Swap in any innovation district, hospital campus or fellowship programme and it still works. Antislop's step 4, run on real work: if the line survives a change of subject, the subject was never in it.

## Overall Assessment

The market has stopped selling generation and started selling **constraint**, and this week it sold a lot of it: token generators, brand kits, contrast gates, brand-score checkers, six design-skill packs in eight days. That is the correct direction and mostly the wrong implementation, because almost every one of these products encodes its rules as **prose** — a `DESIGN.md`, a brand description, a set of instructions the model is asked to follow — and the Supernova test published on the 12th settled what prose is worth: colours and components travelled from a token file into generated UI, and spacing did not, because spacing arrives as a judgement the model still feels entitled to make by eye. The products that will survive the next quarter are the ones that fail a build, and there is exactly one live example in this brief. For Shareef the highest-leverage move this week is therefore not a purchase, it's an afternoon: take the Unified Platform's spacing scale, write it down as a list of permitted values, and put a check in front of it that rejects 2, 10, 12, 18 and 20px — the five off-scale numbers Supernova counted. That single gate retires the most visible slop tell in app UI, it costs nothing, and unlike every skill pack in this brief it cannot be talked out of by a confident model. Everything else can wait for a release tag.
