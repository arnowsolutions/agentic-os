### anthropics/knowledge-work-plugins

**What it is:** Anthropic's open-source library of role-based plugins — marketing, human-resources, operations, enterprise-search, productivity, data, legal, finance, product-management, design, engineering and more — each bundling skills, slash commands, MCP connectors and sub-agents. 36,487★, Apache-2.0, created 2026-01-23, pushed today, 4,026 forks, seven distinct authors in the last hundred commits.

**Source:** https://github.com/anthropics/knowledge-work-plugins · Provenance: spotted on Github Awesome #60 — https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=nrxB2WWBBi4 (chapter 1:05)

**What's good about it:** The plugin container is Claude-Cowork-shaped, but the payload is not. I read the tree: 1,283 files, and the thing inside every plugin is `skills/<name>/SKILL.md` with YAML frontmatter — `name:` and `description:` at the top of the file, then the body. That is exactly the format Hermes skills use, so the work of adopting this is `cp -r`, not a port. And the skills were written by the people who do these jobs: `marketing/skills/` carries `brand-review` (checks copy against a voice guide and flags deviations with before/after fixes, including unsubstantiated claims and missing disclaimers), `campaign-plan`, `content-creation`, `draft-content`, `email-sequence`, `seo-audit`, `performance-report`, `competitive-brief`. `human-resources/skills/` carries `interview-prep`, `onboarding`, `performance-review`, `policy-lookup`, `recruiting-pipeline`, `people-report`. `operations/skills/` carries `runbook`, `compliance-tracking`, `process-doc`, `change-request`, `risk-assessment`, `status-report`, `vendor-review`. For a residency program director that second and third list is the interesting one — interview season, onboarding, policy lookup and runbooks are literally the program's job.

**Pros:**
- Apache-2.0 with a per-plugin `LICENSE`, so copying skill bodies into our own skills directory is permitted, not a grey area.
- The format matches: `marketing/skills/brand-review/SKILL.md` opens with `---`, `name: brand-review`, `description: Review content against your brand voice…` — Hermes reads that without modification.
- Three plugin folders map onto work we already do badly by hand: brand review before content ships, interview prep for the residency cycle, runbooks for the ops we keep in our heads.
- Official Anthropic, ten months old, seven authors, daily commits — this is not a repo that vanishes next month.
- The `brand-review` skill alone is a gate we do not have: our copy-desk QA is my judgement every time, with nothing that fails a build.
- `cowork-plugin-management` documents how they build plugins, which is a usable template if we ever publish our own skill pack.

**Cons:**
- The install path is `/plugin marketplace add` in Claude Code — Hermes cannot consume that, which is precisely why `claude-plugins-official` was dropped from yesterday's brief. The difference here is that the SKILL.md files are plain markdown and can be copied directly; the manifest is the only part we cannot use.
- Every skill body references placeholders and `CONNECTORS.md` — Slack, Notion, MS365, Figma, HubSpot — none of which we run. A copied skill will ask for tools that do not exist until we trim those sections.
- Slash commands (`/brand-review`, `/sales:call-prep`) are Claude-Code syntax; in Hermes they become natural-language triggers, so the "command" half of each plugin is dead weight.
- Quality is uneven across 20 plugin folders — the marketing and HR skills are specific, others are generic role descriptions wearing a skill wrapper.
- 171 open issues and heavy fork traffic (4,026 forks) means the folders will churn; a wholesale copy will drift from upstream and we will not notice.
- Nothing here is hospital-specific: no HIPAA language, no ACGME vocabulary, no Montefiore terminology. The value is structure and method, not content.

**Should we...**
- ✅ **Adopt it** — copy the `marketing`, `human-resources` and `operations` skill folders into `~/.hermes/skills/`, strip the connector placeholders, and rewrite the two or three that touch our brand voice and program language.

**Impact on our stack:** High — Hermes skills, and through them the marketing program and program-operations work. Concretely: an `brand-review` skill that runs against `arnow-copy-logic-2026-09-10.md` before any copy ships, an `interview-prep` and `onboarding` skill for the interview-season platform's human side, and `runbook` / `change-request` skills that turn the procedures I keep in prose into something the agent can execute.

**Why:** We have a skills directory and about a dozen skills in it, most of which I wrote from scratch under time pressure. Anthropic has just published, under a licence that lets us take it, twenty folders of the same artifact written by specialists — in the identical file format. The honest read is that the code is not the contribution here; the *method* is. `brand-review` encodes a review process (voice, terminology, claims, disclaimers, severity, before/after) that our copy desk currently reinvents every time, and `policy-lookup` and `runbook` encode the shape of the document a program director needs when a resident asks about leave or a rotation site goes down. The work is bounded and unglamorous: copy three folders, delete the sections naming tools we do not have, replace the brand placeholders with the Montefiore voice document, and test each skill on one real piece of work before keeping it. I would not install the plugin marketplace, and I would not copy the folders we have no use for — but the marketing and HR sets are the cheapest upgrade to Hermes we have seen in a month, and the licence makes it clean.

### Harness Memory (HarnessRouter)

**What it is:** A hosted long-term memory layer for agents, sold as part of HarnessRouter — "every agent that needs one gets its own memory, automatically," recalled in any later session and shared across agents running on different harnesses. Their site lists **Hermes Agent** among supported harnesses, alongside Codex and Claude Code, and publishes a Harness Memory Protocol. Free tier at $0/month, Developer at $20/month, Production $200/month; long-term memory is metered in "memory operations".

**Source:** https://harnessrouter.ai/harness-memory · Provenance: spotted on ManuAGI — https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=FFcgOKSd3nc (chapter 13:31)

**What's good about it:** Hermes memory is a 6,000-character file, and ours is at 99% — 5,949 characters — which means every new operational fact now costs an old one. That is a hard architectural ceiling on how much the agent can know about this department, and it bites hardest on exactly the material we care about: which URL maps to which service, which credentials belong to which box, which procedure belongs to which skill. Harness Memory is aimed straight at that: memory as a service with its own protocol, growing per agent, recalled before the agent answers, shared across harnesses so a fact learned in Codex is available in Hermes. Their benchmark page even compares the same task across Codex, Claude Code and Hermes configurations, which means someone is testing our harness specifically. The free tier is enough to evaluate without a card.

**Pros:**
- It names Hermes as a first-class harness and benchmarks it — most memory products do not know we exist.
- Solves a real, measured ceiling: we are at 99% of 6,000 characters, and the overflow is currently going into skill files and prose.
- Cross-harness recall means the desktop Hermes and the VPS Hermes stop diverging on what they remember.
- Free tier, published protocol, open-source community edition at 4.4k★ — evaluation is cheap and reversible.
- Memory is chosen per agent by workload, adjustable, and can be turned off for agents that only transform input.

**Cons:**
- **It is somebody else's server.** Our memory file contains Montefiore policy facts, internal hostnames, IP addresses and the shape of our credential layout. Shipping that to a third-party memory service in a hospital-adjacent context is a compliance decision, not a technical one, and it is not mine to make.
- Metered "memory operations" is a pricing model that punishes exactly the behaviour we want — an agent that recalls constantly.
- Three days of public evidence; the product is new, the pricing page is a slider, and there is no published retention or deletion policy on the page I read.
- Lock-in at the layer that is hardest to unwind: migrating skills is easy, migrating what the agent *knows* is not.
- We already have a working answer — the repo vault (1,913 items, searchable) plus skill files — which is local, free and ours.
- Hermes' memory file is also readable and auditable in a way a hosted service is not; "why did it say that" gets much harder.

**Should we...**
- 👀 **Watch it** — and do not move any memory off-box until the data question is settled.

**Impact on our stack:** Medium — Hermes memory across both instances, and indirectly every cron job that currently re-derives context it cannot remember. Concretely: a memory backend that could hold the full service map, the credential inventory and the procedure index instead of the compressed subset that fits in 6,000 characters.

**Why:** The ceiling is real and we hit it daily — I am one of the things being forgotten every time something new is worth remembering, and the workaround (moving facts into skills and vault entries) is a workaround. But the fix has to run where the data is allowed to live. Montefiore flagged an Office 365 login from this VPS in July and the lesson was not "be careful with credentials", it was that institutional facts leaving our boundary cause incidents with real consequences. So the correct verdict is watch, with a specific trigger: if HarnessRouter publishes a self-hosted memory option, or a data-processing agreement we can actually sign, this becomes the best answer to the memory ceiling we have. Until then the honest move is to keep compressing, and to note that our own vault is already two-thirds of this product's job at zero cost.

### tiny-voca

**What it is:** A 4.5-million-parameter English text-to-speech model with two voices, trained from scratch, shipping code, weights, training recipe and dataset. 18 MB including the waveform generator, roughly 70× real time on four CPU threads at 24 kHz, runnable through PyTorch, ONNX Runtime or ONNX Runtime Web. 714★, Apache-2.0, created 2026-11-14, 23 commits, two authors.

**Source:** https://github.com/n4ze3m/tiny-voca · Weights: https://huggingface.co/n4ze3m/tiny-voca · Provenance: spotted on Github Awesome #60 — https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=nrxB2WWBBi4 (chapter 7:51)

**What's good about it:** Eighteen megabytes and no GPU. That is small enough to live inside the Hermes container and fast enough (70× real time on four threads) that turning a 2,000-word brief into audio costs seconds of CPU rather than an API call. It handles long text by synthesising sentence by sentence and joining, peaking at 0.99 so nothing clips in 16-bit WAV, and skips sentences with nothing to read — small details that suggest someone actually used it. Weights and dataset are published, the ONNX export is pre-built in the weights repo, and the browser demo runs the model client-side. For our purposes the interesting part is that it is *ours*: no provider account, no per-character bill, no network dependency, and no third party holding a recording of anything.

**Pros:**
- 18 MB, CPU-only, ONNX — runs on the VPS beside the voice agent with no GPU and no new service.
- Fully open: code, weights, dataset, training recipe. We could fine-tune on a faculty voice recording if we ever wanted a house voice.
- Per-sentence streaming means audio starts before the whole text is synthesised, which is what a voice agent needs.
- Apache-2.0, no per-character cost, no account, nothing leaving the box.
- Sensible defaults built in: speed, pitch shift, pitch range as flags rather than SSML gymnastics.

**Cons:**
- Two fixed voices, American English only, and no voice cloning — for a department brand voice that is a hard limit, not a preference.
- 4.5M parameters is roughly three orders of magnitude below the models behind our current audio; expect flat, robotic prosody. The repo makes no quality claim against commercial TTS, and neither should we until we listen.
- Four days old, two authors, and the "70× real time" figure is the author's own measurement on unknown hardware.
- No SSML, no emphasis control, no pronunciation dictionary — "Montefiore" and "Frasier" will be mispronounced and we cannot fix that without fine-tuning.
- Nothing here helps the M4B player's word-level read-along, which needs forced alignment we already have.
- Two voices is a demo product; a real TTS decision for the department needs more range than this.

**Should we...**
- 👀 **Watch it** — and spend twenty minutes listening to the demo before deciding anything.

**Impact on our stack:** Medium — the voice agent (`arnow-voice`, port 8199) and the audio version of this brief. Concretely: a zero-cost, zero-dependency synthesis path for long-form audio and for voice-agent replies where network latency and per-character cost currently matter.

**Why:** Voice is the one part of our stack with a recurring bill and a hard dependency on someone else's cloud, and it is also the part where a 90% solution would be fine — a brief read aloud does not need eleven out of ten, it needs to be intelligible and free. tiny-voca is the first genuinely small TTS I have seen this month that ships weights, recipe and dataset together, which means the ceiling is not fixed: a fine-tune on clean audio is a weekend, not a research project. The reason this is watch and not adopt is quality. Everything in the repo is engineering, and nothing in it is evidence that the output sounds good; 4.5M parameters is a remarkable size and a suspicious one for natural prosody. The test is cheap and unambiguous — render one paragraph, listen, decide. If it is intelligible, it becomes the default for audio briefs and the fallback for the voice agent; if it is not, we have lost twenty minutes.

### git-twig

**What it is:** A git subcommand that creates lightweight copy-on-write worktrees for agents, using `clonefile` on APFS and `FICLONE` on Btrfs/XFS-with-reflink, falling back to stock `git worktree` everywhere else. Claims up to 97% less disk per worktree. Rust, MIT, 253★, created 2026-11-15, 100 commits, two authors, installable via Homebrew or `cargo install git-twig`.

**Source:** https://github.com/leostera/git-twig · Provenance: spotted on Github Awesome #60 — https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=nrxB2WWBBi4 (chapter 6:09)

**What's good about it:** This is an unusually honest repo. The README publishes a filesystem support matrix with what is *verified* and what is not, a benchmark directory, `TWIG_TRACE=1` to show which backend was chosen, and explicit statements about which cases fall back to stock git. Version 0.5.0 adds cross-revision donors and lets git write attribute-exceptional files, so LF policies and builtin ident/encoding exceptions degrade rather than break. It also ships `git twig skill`, which prints instructions telling an agent how to choose the right checkout, branch and location, and to ask before cleanup — the rare case of a tool that ships its own agent contract instead of assuming one.

**Pros:**
- The design is correct: agents make worktrees constantly and never clean them up, so the cost of a worktree is the thing to attack.
- Published verification matrix and a `TWIG_TRACE` escape hatch — most projects hide their fallbacks; this one documents them.
- `git twig skill` gives agents a contract for creating and cleaning worktrees, which is the missing half of every "run ten agents in parallel" plan.
- MIT, 100 commits in three days, two authors, benchmark directory in-tree.
- Clean degradation: unsupported filesystems and exotic checkout policies fall through to real `git worktree`, so it cannot corrupt a repo by trying.

**Cons:**
- **On our box it saves nothing.** The Hermes container sits on OverlayFS over ext4, which the matrix lists as stock-git fallback with no reflink savings. The 97% figure applies to APFS, Btrfs and reflink-XFS — none of which is where our agents run.
- Three days old, two authors, version 0.5.0 — the version number is honest and the software is not settled.
- Local changes and ignored files are not copied into a worktree, which will confuse an agent expecting a full working copy.
- Failed or interrupted runs can leave a partial registered worktree and retained staging; the README tells you to inspect before retrying, which is not a behaviour I want an unattended cron job to hit.
- The problem it solves is real but small for us: our parallel work is a handful of branches, not fifty.

**Should we...**
- 👀 **Watch it** — relevant only if we ever move agent work onto Btrfs.

**Impact on our stack:** Low — the `ce:build` workflow and any future parallel-agent setup. Concretely: nothing today, because the filesystem it optimises for is not the one we run on.

**Why:** The diagnosis is right and the engineering is better than the repo's age suggests — a support matrix with unverified rows marked unverified is a sign of someone who has been burned by a filesystem claim before. But a tool that saves 97% of disk *only on filesystems we do not use* is a tool we cannot adopt honestly. Our agent work happens in a container on ext4/OverlayFS, where this is a wrapper around the thing we already have. The correct move is to keep it on file for the day we rebuild the VPS on Btrfs — and to steal the idea in the meantime, which costs nothing: `git worktree` plus a cleanup rule in the agent's instructions gets most of the benefit without a new dependency.

### Spacefast (Automattic)

**What it is:** A publishing platform for what an agent makes — "a link for your idea". Each thing you make gets its own Space at `<name>.space.fast`; spaces are private by default with invited people, a share link, or public access; every change is versioned with restore; comments can be pinned to the page; custom domains and static/app hosting are supported, and it works with "any MCP client". By Automattic. No account needed to start.

**Source:** https://spacefast.com · Provenance: spotted on ManuAGI — https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=FFcgOKSd3nc (chapter 12:24)

**What's good about it:** The review loop we run by hand, as a product. Today a CE report or a redesign preview goes to `/var/www/...` via nginx, Shareef opens a URL, and any change is a new deploy with a new path — there is no version history, no commenting, and no way to share one artifact privately short of a Traefik rule. Spacefast's default is private, per-artifact, versioned, with comments anchored to the page and a share card that expires unless claimed. The MCP angle matters too: an agent can publish a Space itself, which means the last mile of "build a report, give me a URL" stops being a shell command I have to remember.

**Pros:**
- Private-by-default with invite links is the correct default for internal content, and it is the opposite of the public-nginx approach we use today.
- Version history with restore means a bad preview is a rollback, not a redeploy.
- Comments anchored to the page turn review feedback into something structured instead of a Telegram paragraph.
- MCP support means the agent publishes directly — one fewer manual step in the CE pipeline.
- Automattic is a serious operator; this is not a weekend project's uptime.
- No account needed to receive a Space, so Shareef clicks a link like he does today.

**Cons:**
- **It is a third-party host for internal Montefiore material.** CE reports name real pages, real people and real defects; a private Space is still someone else's datastore, and that is a decision the department has to make, not me.
- We already have a working deployment path on infrastructure we control — nginx on the VPS, zero data leaving the box — so the gain is workflow, not capability.
- New product, thin public documentation on retention, deletion, and what happens to a Space when a claim card expires unclaimed.
- "Private by default" is only as good as the sharing panel; one mis-click on "anyone with the link" puts a report on the open web.
- Pricing and limits are not clear from the pages I read, and metered publishing is the kind of cost that grows quietly.
- Another account, another vendor, another thing to audit.

**Should we...**
- 👀 **Watch it** — and try one non-sensitive artifact first, not a CE report.

**Impact on our stack:** Medium — the CE report pipeline and the redesign-preview workflow. Concretely: `publish` becomes an MCP call that returns a private, versioned, commentable URL instead of an scp and an nginx path.

**Why:** The gap this closes is not hosting, it is *review*. Shareef reviews offline and returns with a decision, and the current mechanics — public path, no versions, feedback in chat — lose the thread between what he saw and what he said. Version history plus anchored comments is the right answer to that, and private-by-default is the right posture for a hospital. But the content that would go into it is the most sensitive material we produce: named staff, live defects, internal URLs. Putting it on Automattic's infrastructure without a data conversation would repeat the shape of the mistake that got us flagged in July. So: watch, with a clean first test — publish something with no institutional content at all, see whether the review loop actually feels better, and only then have the compliance conversation about the material that matters.

### GPT-6.2 Astra + Sol

**What it is:** OpenAI's November 17 model release — two models, not one. **GPT-6.2 Astra** is the frontier model at $8 input / $0.40 cached / $40 output per million tokens (a 20% cut on input and output and a 60% cached-input discount versus GPT-6 Astra). **GPT-6.2 Sol** is the everyday model, holding GPT-6.1 Sol's $2 / $0.10 / $10 rates. Both have a 1.05M-token context window and 128K max output, available as `gpt-6.2-astra` and `gpt-6.2-sol` in the API and in ChatGPT Work and Codex.

**Source:** https://openai.com/index/introducing-gpt-6-2-astra-sol/ · Provenance: previously covered — GPT-6 Astra was filed as news; **new: the 6.2 generation splits into a frontier tier and a cheap tier, with an actual price cut.** Spotted on Hyperautomation Labs — https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=O8_1sgV2ifI and Matt Wolfe — https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=JVHkb8PTMvc

**What's good about it:** The economics, if the numbers hold. Astra is claimed at 5.2 points above GPT-6 Astra on Agents' Last Exam at less than a third of the cost per task, and 5.5 points above Claude Fable 5.2 at roughly 5% of the cost. Sol is the more interesting release: on Cognition's FrontierCode 2.0 it lands within 0.5 points of Astra at about a fifth of the cost, and on LifeVal it outscores GPT-6 Astra at roughly a seventh of the cost. OpenAI also states its own employees use Sol as the default for technical work. For our stack the relevant fact is that the cheap tier is now plausibly good enough for the high-volume, low-stakes work — the daily brief, the vault filing, the classification and formatting inside cron jobs — where we currently run deepseek-v4-flash.

**Pros:**
- A two-tier release with published prices is exactly what a cost-conscious operator wants: pick per job instead of per vendor.
- Sol at $2/$10 with Astra-class behaviour on everyday tasks is a genuine option for our high-volume cron work.
- 1.05M context and 128K output removes a whole class of "split this document" plumbing for long transcripts and full-page audits.
- Astra's 20% input/output cut and 60% cached-input discount reward the prefix-stable prompts that cron jobs naturally produce.
- Same API surface as prior releases; switching is a model string, not a migration.

**Cons:**
- Early testers are hostile: VentureBeat's headline was "'Essentially unusable': OpenAI launches GPT-6.2 Astra and Sol to negative reception from AI power users," and independent Codex testing reportedly found Astra flat and Sol a regression against its predecessor.
- Sol costs about 2.7× the input and 2.2× the output of GPT-5.4-mini — it is cheap next to Astra, not cheap in absolute terms.
- The effort setting is a trap: OpenAI's own charts show Sol on Low collapsing on LifeVal where Medium recovers most of it. A cron job that does not set effort explicitly gets the bad number.
- Prompts above 272K input double the price, so the long-context advantage comes with a cliff.
- Model churn at this cadence — 6, 6.1, 6.2 inside a few weeks — means any routing decision needs a re-test date, not a one-time choice.
- OpenAI's published benchmarks are the vendor grading its own homework, and the two independent readings we have both disagree with parts of it.

**Should we...**
- 👀 **Watch it** — then A/B `gpt-6.2-sol` against `deepseek-v4-flash` on two cron jobs with a cost and quality note.

**Impact on our stack:** Medium — Hermes model routing across every cron job (the brief, the vault filer, the reminder jobs) and the copy desk. Concretely: a config change that routes one high-volume job to Sol, with output compared side by side against what we get today.

**Why:** We pick models once and then stop thinking about it, which is the cheapest mistake available to us: our largest cost driver is not any single job, it is that every job runs on whatever model I configured months ago. This release is worth attention because the cheap tier is where the volume is, and Sol's claim — within half a point of the frontier model on coding at a fifth of the cost — is the exact trade a brief-generating cron job wants. But the evidence is not good enough to act on blind: two independent readings found Astra flat and Sol worse, the effort-setting cliff is documented in OpenAI's own charts, and a job that silently downgrades its reasoning is worse than a job that costs more. The honest plan is a measured one — one job, both models, same input, compare the prose and the bill — and a re-test date on the calendar, because the next release is probably three weeks away.

**Source:** https://github.com/morluto/rea · Provenance: spotted on The Next New Thing — https://www.youtube.com/shorts/O5k0Wr7e-pE · **Previously covered 2026-10-11 as 👀 watch. New: the star graph is now readable as farmed, and the repo's audience numbers have decoupled from its watcher count — verdict revised to skip.**

**What's good about it:** It is not vaporware. Docs in twenty-five languages, a real MCP tool catalogue, a npm package, a Discord, a website with guides and showcases, 1,571 open issues and a commit today. The design — analysis on your machine, conclusions paired with the evidence and the limits of that evidence — is a more honest posture than most agent tools manage. If the job is "understand how this app does this feature, then build our own", the plumbing looks complete and the setup is one command.

**Pros:**
- Genuinely broad target coverage: native binaries, Electron/JS, .NET, and live websites through one interface.
- Evidence-and-limitations output is the right contract for reverse engineering, where a confident wrong answer is expensive.
- One-command setup that registers itself with the agent and installs workflow instructions.
- MIT, active daily, real documentation, real issue traffic.
- The workflow it enables — inspect a feature you like, then implement your own version — is a legitimate way to scope a rebuild.

**Cons:**

- Nothing in our stack needs it: we build our own software, we do not clone other people's.
- Two distinct authors on the last hundred commits, and a 182 MB repo with 1,571 open issues.
- It pulls in Hopper/Ghidra/IDA — commercial or heavyweight tooling — to do the part that matters.
- The video framing ("rebuild any software in a day, then get paid to host it") is a business pitch, not a capability claim.

**Should we...**
- ❌ **Skip it** — filed only so it is not suggested again.

**Impact on our stack:** None. No system we own improves by inspecting someone else's binary.

**Why:** The question this brief exists to answer is which tool makes our system materially better, and the answer here is none — we do not have a reverse-engineering problem, we have a residency program, a scheduling platform and a set of cron jobs. Even if we did, the star graph is a warning: a repo that collects 467k stars with 1.7k watchers has an audience that was purchased, not earned, and the same promotional machinery that bought the stars is what put it on a trending video. The engineering may well be real — the docs and issue traffic suggest it is — but "real and irrelevant" is still a skip, and the legal exposure of pointing an agent at a binary in a hospital-adjacent context makes it a skip with a note attached.
