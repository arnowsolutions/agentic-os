### Adobe MAX 2026 — MCP in Creative Cloud, and Convert to Text Layers
**Link:** https://www.creativeboom.com/news/adobe-max-2026-all-the-big-announcements/ · reported live from Adobe MAX, Miami Beach, **10 November 2026** · Creative Boom
**Type:** Tool
**What it is:** Two announcements from the same keynote, and the second matters more than the first. Adobe is adding **native MCP (Model Context Protocol) support to Photoshop, Illustrator, Premiere and After Effects**, so an outside agent — Claude, ChatGPT, Gemini, or one you build — can work on native project files while "layers, vectors, bins, sequences, compositions all stay editable," per VP of product marketing Deepa Subramaniam; it lands in private beta in **early 2027**. Separately, and available now in beta: **Convert to Text Layers**. Give Photoshop a flat JPEG or PNG, and it analyses the typography, matches it against the **35,000 fonts in Adobe Fonts**, and rebuilds the lettering as live, editable vector text. Alongside those: Relight with up to three movable light sources in 3D space (GA this week), Generative Restyle applied as its own layer with a strength slider or typed percentage, a Remove tool that catches related elements like a shadow, Quick Compare, and Premiere 27.0 with Color Mode out of beta.

**What's good about it:** The MCP half is the strategic headline and it is genuinely a reversal — a company that spent forty years making its tools so complete you never needed anything else is now inviting other people's agents inside its file formats. But the craft story is Convert to Text Layers, because it directly addresses the single most destructive habit in AI-assisted design: **text baked into a picture**. Every brief in this series has said the same thing — never let a model set your words. The reason that rule exists is that generated lettering is unrecoverable: it cannot be edited, kerned, translated or licensed, and it is always *almost* a typeface. A tool that reads the lettering back out of a flattened image and rebuilds it as vector text, matched against a real library, converts a dead picture into an editable asset. Note the quote, because it is the whole thesis in seven words: Photoshop's director of product management Joel Baer says generated text is "frozen" — *"What I want is vector text."* That is a product manager at Adobe independently arriving at this brief's position, and it is the most encouraging sentence in the week's coverage. Relight is the second useful one, and its value is the same shape: three lights placed in 3D space, updating in near real time with **no prompt involved** — a control, not a slot machine, which is the difference between art direction and re-rolling.

**Pros:**
- Convert to Text Layers is a direct answer to the flattened-flyer problem: it makes the words editable again, which is the precondition for fixing hierarchy, contrast or a wrong font without a rebuild from zero.
- Matching against 35,000 named fonts rather than inventing shapes means the output can be licensed, spec'd and handed to a printer — the difference between a typeface and a resemblance.
- MCP with editable layers preserved is the correct architecture for agents in professional work: the agent operates inside the file's structure instead of emitting a picture of the result.
- Generative Restyle as a layer with a percentage is a *graduated* control. Most AI styling is binary; a slider that goes to 15% is how a designer would actually use it.

**Cons:**
- MCP is a **private beta in early 2027** — nine months of press-release time, and the demo is a demo. Nothing in that half is adoptable now.
- Convert to Text Layers is a beta in one app, and font matching is exactly the kind of task that fails loudly on condensed faces, tight tracking, low resolution, and any lettering that was never a typeface to begin with. It will be confidently wrong.
- The whole thing is a subscription argument dressed as a feature release. Adobe's Q3 story this month is AI credits and margin, and every one of these tools meters.
- Nothing here decides anything. It removes the labour of reconstruction; it does not tell you the kerning is wrong, the crop is boring, or the photograph is the wrong photograph.

**Should we...**
- ✅ **Adopt it** — use it now; it raises our design ceiling
**Why:** Adopt the beta this week, on exactly one artefact, and adopt the principle immediately. The department is sitting on years of flattened output — flyers exported as JPEGs, deck slides pasted in as images, logos traced off screenshots, campaign art whose only copy of the words is inside a picture. Every one of those is a thing Shareef cannot edit, cannot correct, and in the strict sense does not own. Convert to Text Layers is the first tool that offers to reverse that without a rebuild, and even a 60% success rate turns a two-hour recreation into a fifteen-minute correction. Do it on the Sub-I welcome packet first, because that is the artefact where a wrong typeface is most visible and where a reprint is most expensive. What to ignore for now is the MCP announcement: an agent editing native layers is the right destination and it is not shipping until 2027, and pointing an agent at a design file before there is a review gate is how a system drifts. The design-director point is narrower and more useful — the industry has just publicly conceded that the picture was the wrong unit all along, and the winning habit is to never let a finished thing become a flat file in the first place. Keep the layered original. Archive the .psd, not the .jpg.

### Koto × Giffgaff — the arrow that does a job
**Link:** https://www.creativeboom.com/news/koto-points-giffgaff-towards-its-next-chapter-with-an-arrow-led-rebrand/ · published **11 November 2026** · Creative Boom · identity by Koto; creative director Joe Ling; founder/CEO James Greenfield
**Type:** Idea
**What it is:** A full rebrand of the UK mobile operator — 4 million members, part of Virgin Media O2 since 2021, a certified B Corp, and still positioned as the challenger — built on a single organising device. The final **'f' in the wordmark ends in a pointed tip, turning it into an arrow**, and that arrow then recurs across the system: pointing at prices, indicating offers, guiding people through the app. Koto also drew a custom typeface, **Giffsans** — tall, condensed, punchy, with a **'gg' ligature** that ties back to the original logo — kept the black-and-white palette but added **acid green**, and replaced glossy stock photography with candid, flash-lit, phone-camera material.

**What's good about it:** The mechanism is the thing to study, and it is not the logo. **The arrow is a device with a job.** It is a letterform, a wayfinding cue, a bullet, and a directional prompt, and because it does all four it earns the right to appear everywhere without becoming wallpaper. That is the distinction most AI-assisted identity work never gets near: a generated mark is a shape that looks like a logo; this is a shape that *functions*, and function is what makes repetition feel like a system instead of a template. Second, the strategy is stated plainly and the design follows it — Giffgaff's proposition is that you can change plan whenever your life changes, so the identity is about "what's next," which is why the arrow exists at all. Third, and most practically: **the digital system was "built on Giffgaff's existing design tokens and ready for development from day one, so it can be rolled out rather than sitting in a deck."** That single sentence describes the difference between a rebrand that ships and a rebrand that gets admired in a PDF and quietly ignored. Fourth, the photography brief is a genuine art-direction decision with a failure mode named: Koto "avoided the trap of glossy stock shots" — sunburnt older men in swim shorts, a couple laughing on a night out. It reads as real because it is the register of the audience, not the register of the category.

**Pros:**
- One device doing four jobs: letterform, wayfinding, punctuation and CTA. That is systems thinking that survives contact with a 320px screen.
- The custom face is drawn for the slogans it has to set ("Great tech. No traps."), and the 'gg' ligature carries the old logo forward — heritage handled by one glyph rather than a nostalgic badge.
- Built on existing tokens, dev-ready from day one. This is the single most copyable sentence in the week's coverage.
- The off-screen execution — green packing tape printed "Your Next Move" across brown cardboard — is a reminder that identity lives in the least-designed moments, and that is where slop is most obvious.

**Cons:**
- Acid green on black-and-white is a strong, fashionable move and it is also the move half the challenger brands in Europe are making this year. It will date faster than the arrow will.
- The piece is a launch notice written with the agency in the room. No recall, no conversion, no member response — and Black Friday is described as "a big test" precisely because the result is unknown.
- "Avoid stock photography" is easy advice and hard practice. A department with no photo budget reads that line and substitutes worse stock, not better candour.
- The arrow is a small, elegant idea that requires discipline to keep small. The moment a second device appears, the system becomes decoration — and nothing in the release says who enforces that.

**Should we...**
- ✅ **Adopt it** — use it now; it raises our design ceiling
**Why:** Adopt the method, and adopt it to the department's actual problem. The Unified Platform has a strong visual system and no wayfinding idea: it has colour, type, spacing and a radius scale, and every screen still has to explain itself with words. Giffgaff's arrow is a *repeated functional mark* — cheap to draw, easy to place, and legible at 16px — and the residency program needs exactly one of these, whether it reads as a directional cue, a stethoscope-line, a route, or the letterform of the program's own name. Build it once, define what job it does (point at the next step), and then let it do that job in the application form, the interview invite, the Sub-I packet and the call-out instructions. The second transferable asset is the operating rule behind the digital system: **design against the tokens that already exist and ship something the developers can build**. That is the line that separates the platform's best pages from its weakest — the weak ones were designed as pictures and handed over as pictures. Koto's restraint on heritage is the third: when you need continuity, change one glyph, not the whole logo. What to skip is the colour. Acid green is Giffgaff's, not ours; the platform already has its navy, and a second loud accent would spend the equity the system has built.

### Woven by Essential Viewing — a mark made of its own subject
**Link:** https://www.creativeboom.com/news/this-new-identity-for-a-womens-sports-community-swaps-cheerleading-for-real-talk/ · published **11 November 2026** · Creative Boom · naming, strategy, identity, voice, merch and campaign by Essential Viewing (New Zealand)
**Type:** Idea
**What it is:** The Ironman group wanted more women in triathlon and had a community called Women For Tri since 2014. Essential Viewing rebuilt it as **Woven** — a name, strategy, identity, tone of voice, merch line and launch campaign — unveiled at the Ironman World Championship in Kailua-Kona in October and now rolled out across **45 territories**. The mark is **three interlocking chevrons**, one per discipline, stacked and overlapped until they also read as a subtle **'W'**; the studio calls it a "braid of resilience," anchored by "Strength in every thread."

**What's good about it:** The mark is derived, not decorated — three sports, three strokes, one braid, and a letterform falls out of the geometry for free. That is the difference between an identity and a logo: you can explain *why* every part of it is there without reaching for a metaphor afterwards. The second decision is the gradients, and it is the most instructive thing in the piece: the hazy, grainy orange-pink-lilac-blue fields look like a 90s UK garage flyer rather than a sports brand, and — the part that makes it a system — **each gradient is assigned to a mood**: cool blues for support and care, hot oranges for power and energy, pinks for race-day excitement. A palette with semantic roles, not a mood board. Third, the type does two jobs in parallel: a wide confident sans for the statements ("Progress Over Perfection", "Born For This, Made For This") and **small monospaced type running across images like a ticker**, which is how you get editorial texture without a second display face. Fourth, the voice deliberately refuses the category's cliché — the insight is that the only entry requirement is the will to try, which produces "**I Am Not One Thing / Neither Is My Sport**" instead of "You go, girl." That is positioning you can hear.

**Pros:**
- A mark whose construction is its argument: swim, bike, run, braided, and the W is a by-product rather than a contrivance.
- Gradients with assigned meaning — the single most copyable idea here, and the direct opposite of "make it feel premium."
- Photography brief that names the failure it is avoiding: close-ups on freckled, sunburnt faces, motion-blurred swimmers, grainy tarmac. Gritty, not airbrushed.
- The mark flexes into product without redrawing: repeated down a jacket spine it becomes a pattern; as a metal charm on a lace it reads as jewellery; as a temporary tattoo it becomes a badge. One shape, four artefacts.

**Cons:**
- It is a sports community with a merch line and a Strava presence. Almost none of the execution transfers to a hospital's recruitment materials at any realistic budget.
- The "90s garage" reference is load-bearing and unverified — the studio says the community embraced it; there is no independent evidence in the piece.
- The gradient system is the kind of idea that degrades silently. Assigned meanings only survive if they are written down and enforced; otherwise the pink gets used for a sponsorship logo in month three.
- Three interlocking chevrons is a crowded shape-space — arrows, chevrons and woven knots are the three most-generated "dynamic" marks in every AI logo tool. The logic is original; the silhouette is not defensible on its own.

**Should we...**
- ✅ **Adopt it** — use it now; it raises our design ceiling
**Why:** Adopt the derivation discipline, because it is the exact antidote to prompting. When you ask a model for "a modern medical logo," you get the average of every medical logo it has seen — a shield, a cross, a swoosh, or a blue gradient blob — and the reason is structural: the model has no access to *why*. Woven works because the form comes from the subject: three disciplines, three strokes. The department has the same raw material sitting unused — a residency is four years, a call schedule is a rotation, a urology service is clinic plus OR plus consult, a Sub-I is a single week inside all of it. Any of those can generate a mark with a reason attached, and a mark with a reason survives every "can we try it a bit more modern?" meeting because the answer is a sentence, not a preference. The second adoption is the palette-with-jobs rule: the Unified Platform already restricts itself to one accent, and this is the argument for going one step further and writing down what each colour is *for* — data, action, alert, identity — so that a new screen cannot invent a fifth use. The third is the ticker. Small monospaced type running across an image is the cheapest way to make a photograph look art-directed, it is one line of CSS, and it will do more for the social creative than any generated visual in this brief.

### Sourmilk vs Cupful — AI branding that reproduced a competitor
**Link:** https://www.thefashionlaw.com/ai-makes-it-easy-to-launch-a-brand-and-copy-existing-ones/ · published **9 November 2026** · The Fashion Law (reporting an incident that surfaced publicly on 5 November)
**Type:** Idea
**What it is:** A New York yogurt company, **Sourmilk**, discovered a rival called **Cupful** whose entire brand — arched white lettering on deep blue, similar illustrations, similar product imagery — closely resembled its own. Sourmilk's founders had commissioned designers, artists and photographers for their identity; Cupful's founder, a Harvard graduate student, told *Inc.* she had used AI because she lacked the budget and expertise, and said she had never heard of Sourmilk until social media users pointed out the resemblance. She has since shut the accounts and site and says she will rebrand. The article's value is the legal analysis underneath: **a lack of intent to copy is not a defence to trademark infringement** if confusion is likely; copyright similarly does not require intent, though independent creation can defeat a copying claim — which makes the provenance of AI-generated illustrations a live question. And AI adds a new problem to clearance, because the model's training data and influences are opaque even to the person who directed it.

**What's good about it:** It is the first documented, dated case in this brief's run where the slop failure mode produced a *legal* exposure rather than an aesthetic complaint — and the mechanism is exactly what the brief has argued for weeks. A model asked for "premium yogurt branding, deep blue, arched type" does not invent; it returns the nearest plausible point in the space of yogurt branding it has seen. If the brand it lands on happens to be real and recent, the output is a copy that nobody intended, produced by a process with no memory and no clearance step. The legal analysis is the useful part: the standard for infringement is consumer confusion, not bad faith, so "I didn't know" is worth nothing, and the opacity of the model makes the clearance question *harder* than it was for a human designer, because a human at least knows what they looked at. It also quietly indicts the whole "generate 40 options and pick one" workflow: at that volume, nobody checks any of them, and the one that looks most professional is the one most likely to be a near-copy of a brand that hired an agency.

**Pros:**
- A real, dated, attributable case with a named companies and a public timeline — not a hypothetical about AI and originality.
- States the doctrine plainly: intent is irrelevant to confusion-based infringement, and AI provenance does not insulate anyone.
- Names the operational gap most brand teams have: no clearance step between "the model made it" and "we printed 5,000 of it."
- Inadvertently proves the premium argument from the other direction — the copied brand was the one that had commissioned real designers, artists and photographers.

**Cons:**
- It is a single anecdote from one category, resolved without litigation, so the legal stakes are inferred rather than adjudicated. No court has ruled on any of this.
- The piece is written by a legal publication for brand owners and counsel; the practical design-side protocol is implied, never specified.
- Nothing in it addresses the far more common outcome — not a copy, just an average — which is the actual daily cost of prompted branding.
- Cupful's account is second-hand through *Inc.*, and the founder's claim of never having heard of the brand is reported, not tested.

**Should we...**
- ✅ **Adopt it** — use it now; it raises our design ceiling
**Why:** Adopt the protocol, not the fear. The department is a public health institution with a licensed marks portfolio, and "we generated it, we didn't know" is the weakest possible position if a campaign borrows a competitor's or another health system's look — and branding in academic medicine is a small, dense visual space where a navy-plus-arched-type composition is genuinely at risk of landing on something. The protocol is one page and it costs nothing: anything generated for external use gets a **reverse image search and a name check before it ships**, and anything that survives goes into the archive with a note on what was prompted. That is the clearance step the article says nobody has. The second, larger adoption is procedural and it addresses the root cause: never brief a model with a competitor's name, a rival's URL, or "in the style of" language, because that is literally the instruction to find the nearest existing brand and stand on it. Brief with the department's own material — the twenty-eight interviews, the real OR, the real Bronx — and the output has nowhere to copy from except us. This is the week's clearest evidence that craft and compliance point the same way: the reason to art-direct instead of prompt is not taste, it is that a directed composition is checkable and a generated one is not.

### Cards — a design system that emits its own AI prompt
**Link:** https://github.com/oliur/cards · live studio: https://cards.uidesigncourse.com · created **5 November 2026** · MIT · 18 stars, 0 releases · by Oliur (UI Design Course)
**Type:** Tool
**What it is:** A design-system studio in the browser. Tune colour, neutrals, fonts, radius, density and shadows live, then take the result away in **three forms from one source**: as installable **shadcn/ui** components (`npx shadcn@latest add .../r/cards.json`), as a **copyable AI prompt** describing the whole system in words, or as an **agent skill** (`npx skills add oliur/cards`). It is built on shadcn/ui conventions — Radix primitives, `cva`, `cn()`, `data-slot` — with its own semantic token layer (`bg-surface`, `text-fg`, `ring-line`, `text-ui-sm`-style type), a light and dark ramp, a radius scale and shadows.

**What's good about it:** The architecture is correct and almost nobody gets it right: **the SKILL.md is generated from `src/design-system/`, not written by hand**, the site and the prompt "read from the same source files, so they can't drift apart," and `pnpm lint` **fails if the committed copy is out of date**. That is the whole problem with the current crop of "taste skills" solved — the ones floating around X this month are hand-written prose about taste that goes stale the moment the tokens change, whereas here the instruction to the agent is an *artifact of the design system*, versioned and enforced. There is also a second, quieter piece of rigour: the lint runs **a 4px spacing-grid check and token checks**, so the system polices itself rather than relying on review. And the three-output model is the right shape for a small team: the same decisions become components for the build, a prompt for the agent, and a skill for the coding tool, and none of the three can silently disagree with the others.

**Pros:**
- One source of truth emitting three artifacts, with a lint that fails on divergence — the only mechanism in this week's scan that actually prevents design-system drift.
- Semantic token names (`bg-surface`, `text-fg`, `ring-line`) instead of raw hex, which is what makes a theme changeable without a search-and-replace.
- The skill-install path (`npx skills add`) means an agent picks the system up automatically when working on UI — the system constrains generation rather than being bypassed by it.
- MIT, static-hostable, no database, no environment variables. Deployable by someone who is not a developer, which matters here.

**Cons:**
- **Five days old, 18 stars, zero releases**, one author. It is a course companion before it is a product, and the bus factor is one.
- The customisation lives in a TypeScript file (`guidelines.ts`) that you edit to make the system yours — which is a code change, not a design change, and it excludes the person who actually owns the brand.
- It is shadcn/Tailwind-shaped by construction. The Unified Platform is React with its own component layer, so adopting it means adopting a stack, not adding a tool.
- The aesthetic is fixed to whatever the studio ships as presets; you can tune colour, type and radius, but the *composition* ideas are still the system's, not yours.

**Should we...**
- 👀 **Watch it** — promising, not proven yet
**Why:** Watch the tool, steal the mechanism this week. The Unified Platform's real problem is not that its pages look bad — most now look good — it is that the design system lives in three places that can disagree: the tokens in code, the Figma file, and whatever instructions get pasted into an agent. Cards' answer is the one to copy: **generate the instruction from the tokens, and fail the build when they diverge.** Concretely, that means the platform's design rules should be a generated artifact — a `DESIGN.md` emitted from the token source, checked in CI, with the lint failing if the doc and the tokens disagree. That is a weekend of work and it converts "our agent sometimes gets the radius wrong" into a build error. Watch rather than adopt because the repo is five days old with no releases and a single maintainer, and because adopting the tool means adopting shadcn, Tailwind and a preset aesthetic — the thing worth taking is the pipeline, not the library. And note what it does not do: it will not tell you which typeface. A studio that lets you tune Inter and DM Mono is a studio that has already decided, and that decision is the one that matters most.

### Burg Vector by Charis Tsevis — KPT for Illustrator, in editable paths
**Link:** https://github.com/tsevis/burg-vector · created **6 November 2026** · v0.3.1 · MIT · by Charis Tsevis · three releases
**Type:** Tool
**What it is:** An Illustrator panel with **thirteen vector effects** — extrude, warp, fracture, inset, sketch, glow and more — built as a deliberate homage to **KPT Vector Effects**, Kai Krause's 1990s plug-ins that turned Illustrator into a playground. You select artwork, the panel previews live, you drag across controls, move a light around its sphere, let **Explore** breed variations, and press ✓ to commit. The design decision that matters: **nothing it produces is a live effect or a raster.** The output is a group of ordinary paths you can ungroup, recolour and keep editing. Targets Illustrator 2026 (30.x) via Adobe's CEP panel runtime; tested end to end on macOS in Illustrator 30.8.2 — every effect written into a real document and compared anchor by anchor, handle by handle — with a Windows installer built but not yet verified against Illustrator on Windows.

**What's good about it:** The constraint is the craft. Every commercial "3D-ify your logo" tool outputs either a live effect you cannot flatten sensibly or a raster you cannot edit; Tsevis's entire premise is that the result must be **plain editable geometry**, which is why the README can talk about a 3,600-point extrusion being ungroupable into side walls you can recolour individually. That is a designer building the tool he wanted rather than the demo that sells. The second thing worth respecting is the historical reference: KPT Vector Effects is a genuinely forgotten piece of design history, from an era when plug-ins were authored by artists and experimenters rather than shipped as subscription features, and reviving that posture — small, weird, generous, MIT — is a real position. Third, the process is documented like engineering: a `tools/check.mjs` that creates every effect in a temporary document and reads the paths back, a versioned .zxp, an honest platform status table distinguishing "installed and tested" from "installer exercised with simulated responses." Almost no one in the AI-tool economy is this candid about what has been verified.

**Pros:**
- Output is editable paths, always — no rasters, no live effects, no flattened result. The artefact stays workable, which is the definition of an asset.
- "Explore" breeds variations from a parameter set, which is the *good* version of generation: controlled, seeded, repeatable, and each result is yours to keep editing.
- A real designer's tool with a real designer's reference library — KPT and Kai Krause — instead of a UI built on the current aesthetic.
- Verification is documented: thirteen effects checked anchor by anchor in a real Illustrator document, plus a geometry test harness you can run yourself.

**Cons:**
- **Zero stars.** It has 3 releases and no audience, which for a one-person CEP extension means no bug reports and no support when Illustrator 31 ships.
- Windows is unbuilt in practice and the certificate is self-signed, so every install involves a security warning — a non-starter for a department laptop with IT policy.
- These are exactly the effects that read as 1990s pastiche: extrude, bevel, glow. Used without judgment they produce the kitsch that AI image tools have now made ubiquitous in a *newer* dialect.
- It is an Illustrator-only, desktop-only, macOS-verified tool in a workflow that increasingly happens in code.

**Should we...**
- 👀 **Watch it** — promising, not proven yet
**Why:** Watch the tool, adopt the argument. The interesting thing here is not extrusion; it is that a designer responded to a generation-obsessed market by shipping **deterministic, editable, repeatable geometry controls** and calling it a homage to 1996. That is the correct posture for craft software, and it is the same one this brief keeps landing on: the tool should produce something you can keep working on. For the residency creative, the practical use is narrow but real — the department's posters and social templates have no dimensional or typographic tension at all, and a controlled extrude on a *word* (not a logo, not a generated image) is a cheap way to get a poster that looks art-directed rather than assembled. The rule that keeps it from becoming kitsch is the same rule as always: one effect, one element, once per piece. Watch rather than adopt because it is a zero-star, self-signed, macOS-verified extension that IT will not install, and because the honest value for Shareef this week is the principle — **prefer the tool that leaves the paths editable** — which he can apply to every tool decision without installing anything.

### Accidental Collage by Dr.Me — six years of torn posters
**Link:** https://www.creativereview.co.uk/accidental-collage-book-drme-manchester/ · published **10 November 2026** · Creative Review · book by Dr.Me (Mark Edwards and Ryan Doyle), published by Waiting Room press
**Type:** Idea
**What it is:** A book collecting **six years of photographs of decaying outdoor advertising** — posters ripped down, billboards weathered, old campaigns emerging from beneath newer ones — taken by Manchester studio Dr.Me and a circle of collaborators. It began in February 2020 as Mark Edwards's personal archive while travelling for design events, then became a bicycle survey of Manchester's neglected hoardings during the pandemic. The best example is a Nick Cave & the Bad Seeds billboard peeling away to reveal an old McDonald's campaign beneath: Edwards calls the result "**NickDonald's and the bad seeds**." Contributors include Hisham Akira Bharoocha, Sarah Boris, Nick Deakin, and drummer Jeremy Gustin, who photographed found compositions while touring with David Byrne, Joan as Police Woman and Iggy Pop.

**What's good about it:** It is a method disguised as a hobby. The compositions in the book were authored by nobody — by the person who pasted the poster, the weather, and the passer-by who tore it — and the only authorial act left is **noticing**, which is a trainable skill and the one thing a model cannot do for you. Boris's essay frames it as "a collective endeavour, shaped by countless hands, moments and colours," which is a genuinely interesting answer to the authorship question everyone in AI design is currently arguing about: authorship can be the act of *selection and framing* rather than the act of making. The second lesson is the archive discipline. Six years, one subject, photographed consistently, and it eventually became a book — and Edwards is explicit that he did **not** repurpose the images into his own design work, he documented them "more like an archaeologist documenting a find," while the practice was influenced instead (he started experimenting with torn typography and adhesive tape). That is the healthiest relationship between reference and output I have read this month. Third, the piece carries a real observation about the medium: with digital outdoor advertising, these accidental surfaces are getting rarer and surviving in less well-heeled areas — an accidental record of a vanishing physical condition.

**Pros:**
- A concrete, copyable practice: one subject, shot consistently, for years — the cheapest way to build a visual vocabulary that no model has.
- Selection-as-authorship is a strong, defensible position for anyone whose competitors are generating; the value is in the eye, not the render.
- Documents a genuinely disappearing thing — layered, weathered, physically deteriorating advertising — which gives the book a documentary reason to exist beyond aesthetics.
- The "NickDonald's" example is a working demonstration of juxtaposition as a design tool: two unrelated messages colliding into a third meaning, which is what collage has always been for.

**Cons:**
- It is a book about photographs of other people's design. The transferable output is a habit, not an artefact, and habits are the hardest thing to hand to a team.
- Torn-poster collage is one of the most imitated looks in graphic design, and it is the exact aesthetic that AI image tools produce when asked for "grunge collage texture" — the original is documentary, the copies are costume.
- "Notice more" is not a brief. A department that buys the book and not the practice has acquired a coffee-table object and a new visual cliché.
- Nothing here touches typography, systems or digital surfaces, which is where most of Shareef's work actually lives.

**Should we...**
- 👀 **Watch it** — promising, not proven yet
**Why:** Watch the book, start the archive. The reason this belongs in a brief about AI design is that it is the clearest available answer to "what does a designer do that a model cannot?" — notice a composition that already exists in the world and frame it. Every photograph in that book is a decision about cropping, timing and attention, and none of it required generating anything. The department has an unusually rich version of this asset and is not using it: twenty-eight faculty interviews, real operating rooms, real corridors, a real building in the Bronx, four seasons of a residency. A single shared folder with a rule — shoot the same six subjects, consistently, for a year — would produce a body of material no competitor and no model can reproduce, and it would do more for how the program reads than any campaign. The second transferable idea is the discipline of **separating reference from output**: Edwards collected torn posters for six years and did not paste them into his work; he let them change how he drew type. That is the right relationship with a mood board, and it is the opposite of prompting an image to look like the reference. Watch rather than adopt because a book cannot change a workflow; the practice can, and the practice costs nothing but consistency.

## Slop Radar
- **The client-supplied AI comp as the brief.** Six r/graphic_design threads in this week's fallback scan describe the same scene: a client runs the designer's work (or a ChatGPT output) through an image model and sends it back saying "make it look like this." The tell that the thread's veterans keep naming is that the AI comp cannot be honoured — it has no layers, no named typeface, no consistent scale, and often a tabloid's worth of copy on an 8.5×11 page. Treat every AI comp as a *reference to be interrogated* ("what across these five do you actually like?"), never as direction, and price the reconstruction honestly.
- **Type is the new tell, and clients can now feel it.** The most commercially interesting thread of the week is the Figma State of Design 2026 discussion: designers report clients who used to approve anything "clean" now arriving with strong opinions about fonts — because AI output has a recognisable typographic signature (the same two or three sans-serifs, the same weights, the same tracking). That is good news for craft and bad news for anyone still choosing type by default. It also raises the bar: "not Inter" is now table stakes, not a point of view.
- **Star-farmed repos have learned to write design copy.** The `fujc683/gpt-image-2-6-*` family is now **ten repositories** at 100–103 stars each with zero forks — `pricing`, `mcp-server`, `text-rendering`, `commercial-use`, `alternatives` — and the `rpocket`/`resocket` cluster now includes "visual QA for AI-built frontends" and "a check Claude can run against your Figma design" at 53–59 stars. The descriptions are fluent and design-literate; the repos are one reseller's SEO surface. Open the commit history before you believe the README.
- **"Free" generation, paid reconstruction.** The r/graphic_design threads and the Cupful case converge on the same arithmetic: the AI comp costs nothing to make and everything to rebuild, because converting a flat image back into editable, licensed, consistent artwork is the expensive part — and the department usually pays for it twice. Adobe's beta is an admission of the same thing. Budget the reconstruction, or refuse the flattened input.

## Overall Assessment
The week's centre is **recoverable structure**, and it is the healthiest theme this brief has had. Adobe's keynote — an MCP that keeps layers editable, and a Photoshop beta that turns flat JPEG lettering back into vector text — is a platform owner conceding that the picture was the wrong unit. Cards makes the point at the level of the design system: one token source emitting components, a prompt and an agent skill, with a lint that fails when they disagree. Burg Vector insists that every effect come out as editable paths. Giffgaff was built on the client's existing tokens so it could ship rather than be admired. And the Sourmilk case shows what happens at the other end: a brand generated without structure was the average of its category, and the average turned out to be somebody's actual identity — with no intent required, and no defence in having no intent. Put together, the argument is no longer aesthetic. Work that cannot be edited, named, cleared and re-used is not an asset, it is a photograph of one, and an organisation that keeps producing them is renting its own brand. **The single highest-leverage move for Shareef this week:** pick the three most-used flattened artefacts in the department — almost certainly the Sub-I welcome packet, the recruitment flyer, and the interview-season one-pager — and rebuild each as a live original with named typefaces, a layered source file and a one-page spec, then run a reverse-image and name clearance check on everything already in circulation that was generated. Three artefacts, one afternoon each. That is the difference between owning the material and licensing the look of it.
