### florilegium — procedurally grown Delft-blue page margins, painted as watercolour, seeded per page
**Link:** https://github.com/kevinraymond/florilegium (MIT, Lua + LuaLaTeX; origin: a [r/LaTeX thread](https://www.reddit.com/r/LaTeX/comments/1xixznw/how_do_you_make_these_flowers_in_latex/) asking how such flowers were made)
**Type:** Tool
**What it is:** A LuaLaTeX package by Kevin Raymond, created **30 October**, pushed **1 November**, **1,333 stars and 34 forks in three days**, MIT. Every petal, leaf, stem, butterfly, bee, snail and occasional bird is a **parametric curve computed in Lua while the page is being written**, painted in the order a watercolourist works — flat wash, darker blotches multiplied over it, pigment pooling at the edges, streaks lifted out in white, a few fine veins, "and almost no outlines" — then written straight into the PDF as path operators. No images, no imported paths, no external assets. Two compositions ship: **authored** (stems, leaves and flowers placed by hand) and **grown** (a greedy space-filling loop that takes the largest free disc within reach of a stem, chooses a motif that fits it, and grows a branch to it — borrowed explicitly from Wong, Zongker & Salesin's 1998 SIGGRAPH paper *Computer-Generated Floral Ornament*). Options include initials (**`\florilegiuminitial[5]{E}`** reads the letter's outline from an OpenType font and paints it like a petal, with a vine passing over and under its strokes and flowers landing in its counters — the letter is also typeset invisibly so the paragraph still copies as text), flowers at headings painted in copper "the way headings were once picked out in red", a season system that ages the garden from spring buds to autumn seed heads across the document, and a `geometric` theme that cuts the same plant from flat rhombi and low-poly butterflies. The whole palette derives from **one** declared colour (`ink`) and is interpolated in **OKLCH** so the light end never turns grey. There is also a pure-PostScript port, self-contained, that a printer re-runs per job — so every physical print is a different garden.
**What's good about it:** This is the most *design-literate* piece of generative tooling I have read all month, and it is literate in exactly the place the rest of the field is weak: **the seed is derived from the page number, so page n always gets garden n.** That one decision is the difference between a decoration and a typesetting system — it makes the output stable enough to set a real document in, reviewable, diffable, and printable twice to the same result. Everything else follows from it. Second, the ornament is genuinely *drawn* rather than generated-from-nothing: the primitives are hand-authored and the randomness only samples their parameters, which is precisely the split that separates craft from slot-machine luck. The author says so himself and refuses to overclaim: "I don't know how the original was made, and this project doesn't try to reproduce it. It is one possible way, and the way I did it." Third, the **space-filling loop is old knowledge applied properly** — the 1998 paper is cited, the mechanism (largest free disc first, big holes slowly, small ones fast) is described accurately, and it produces a layout that *reads* the page: it measures `\pagetotal` so a short final page grows branches into the space below the text, in one compile. Fourth, the **one-colour palette with an OKLCH ramp** is the single most transferable technique here — a document declares one ink and gets an entire coherent tonal family that never greys out, which is the same discipline our platform's single-accent rule already enforces in another medium. Fifth, the craft honesty: `theme=geometric` ships as a tenth the file size and the README says so rather than pretending both are equal.

**Pros:**
- Deterministic by construction, which almost no generative ornament is: same seed, same garden, forever. That is what makes it usable in a document that gets proofed, revised and reprinted.
- The ornament system is *semantically* hooked to the text, not pasted onto it — flowers land beside specific headings and specific lines, and initials are painted from the actual letterform's outline.
- Real craft vocabulary in the implementation, not in the marketing: tapered strokes are "two offset Béziers, not sampled ribbons"; pigments pool; veins are drawn; outlines are nearly absent. Those are decisions a watercolourist and a printmaker recognise.
- Genuinely MIT, single author, no commercial gravity, no API key, and it runs in Docker if you have no TeX — the low-friction path is documented.
- The PostScript port is a serious piece of work in its own right (it names the four things that don't map across: layers, transparency, single-precision randomness, and text) and it produces something *printable* that varies per impression.

**Cons:**
- **It is LaTeX-only.** LuaLaTeX, plus an optional PostScript path. Shareef's surfaces are a React app, a web player, and social/print creative made in modern tools. Nothing in our current pipeline speaks `.tex`, so adoption means porting the *method*, not using the package.
- A 1,333-star rep in three days is real attention but it is a single-author art project with **two releases** and no users yet beyond the Reddit thread that prompted it. There is no evidence it survives contact with a 200-page document or an unusual page geometry.
- The aesthetic is very specific — Delft blue, forget-me-not cymes, lobed leaves, a snail. As a *system* it is disciplined; as a *look* it is a costume. Applied to institutional material it would read as decorative nostalgia, which is a different failure from slop but still a failure.
- Documentation quality is uneven: the README is superb on how the drawings are built and thin on the tuning story — what `density=0.4` actually does to a page, what breaks on a two-column layout, what happens at A5 or US Letter.
- The origin is a Reddit ask, so the project's whole reason for existing is "somebody wanted this look in LaTeX" — that is a satisfying origin and a narrow demand.

**Should we...**
- ✅ **Adopt it**
**Why:** Adopt the three techniques, not the package. The first is **seeded determinism as a design decision**: derive the variation from the object's own identity — page number, record id, issue number — and the generative output stops being a slot machine and becomes something you can proof. That is directly applicable to the social creative pipeline: if each residency announcement's background or accent treatment were derived from a stable key rather than re-rolled, the whole series would look related *and* each piece would be reproducible when someone asks for "the version we ran in March." The second is **one colour in, a tonal family out, interpolated in OKLCH** — we already argue for a single accent and quiet chrome; this is the technical spine under that argument, and it is the correct way to build an accent ramp that doesn't turn grey at the light end, which is the exact defect visible in a lot of AI-generated "premium" palettes right now. The third is the **1998-ornament-paper move itself**: the modern answer to "make it feel hand-made" is increasingly to reach for a documented generative-craft tradition rather than a diffusion model, and this repo is the clearest example on record of doing that and getting something a design director would sign. Where it does *not* go: the Unified Platform (a dark React UI has no business growing watercolour margins), and the Chapter Player. Where it *could* land beautifully: **Sub-I welcome materials and fellowship/graduation print collateral** — a seeded margin motif in Montefiore navy, generated deterministically per recipient, is the kind of detail that makes a welcome packet feel commissioned instead of mailed, and it is precisely the print surface where "AI-generated" currently reads as cheap. The honest reason this is Adopt rather than Watch is that the craft is finished and the ideas are portable; the reason to be careful is that the finished article is an art project for TeX users, and we are not TeX users.

### logo-design-skill — a disciplined identity workflow that stops at a checkpoint and tests at 16 pixels
**Link:** https://github.com/kaankiziltug/logo-design-skill (MIT; author Kaan Kızıltuğ; created 26 September, last push **20 October**, now **5,775 stars and 456 forks**, six releases)
**Type:** Tool
**What it is:** An open Agent Skill (folder + `SKILL.md`, plus dependency-free Python tools) that turns Claude, Codex, Gemini CLI, Cursor or Copilot into a *disciplined* identity designer rather than a mark generator. It carries written references on **twelve principles, discovery and briefs, word mapping, mark-type selection (wordmark → combination, with pros/cons/decision guide), geometric construction, optical corrections (overshoot, bone effect, irradiation), colour, typography, lockups, testing, presentation, delivery, redesign and identity systems**. It ships a **library of 1,400+ real SVG logos (~1,200 brands)**, each classified by mark type, technique, geometry, subject, typography, mood and industry — searchable from the CLI, browsable as a local gallery — explicitly "to study construction, map category conventions and avoid look-alikes (**never to copy**)". And it ships the tools that matter: `svg_audit.py` (structure, colours, complexity, near-miss angles, tiny details, centring), `concept_sheet.py` (all concepts as one image with a recommendation), `preview_sheet.py` (a test sheet with **16 px pixel test, one-colour, reversed, squint, mirror, contexts and a competitor shelf test**), `presentation_board.py` (six industry-specific mockups per concept), `render_png.py`, `export_variants.py` (mono, square, favicon, app-icon, full web-icon set). The central behavioural rule: **the skill always stops at the checkpoint** — it shows concepts as one image with a recommendation and produces nothing further until a direction is chosen.
**What's good about it:** The **checkpoint** is the most important thing in this repo and the thing almost no AI design tool has the discipline to build. A model asked for a logo will happily produce forty finished lockups and forty sets of guidelines; this skill refuses, because "the kit is most of the work and only makes sense for an approved idea." That is a working studio's cost structure encoded as a rule, and it is the direct antidote to the thing that makes AI creative output feel cheap — the flood of finished-looking options nobody chose. Second, the **test battery is the right battery**: a mark that survives 16 px, one colour, reversal, a squint, a mirror, the contexts it will actually live in, and a shelf of competitors is a mark that will survive procurement and a hospital sign shop. Those are the exact tests institutional identities die on, and here they are automated and shown *before* the client is asked to approve. Third, the **library is used correctly** — 1,400 classified logos is a research corpus for construction and category-convention mapping with an explicit never-copy rule, which is the opposite of the "train on the cream-and-terracotta default" behaviour Shareef is trying to escape. The classifier statistics are themselves editorial: "median 2 colours, 75% use ≤ 3, gradients in 19%" is an argument about restraint made with data rather than adjectives. Fourth, the **example set is honest about failure**: 28 fictional briefs across sectors, and it reports that "in four of them the client picked a different concept at the checkpoint than the one the skill recommended, and the skill refined that choice before colouring it: exactly what the checkpoint is for." A vendor publishing the cases where its recommendation lost is a vendor worth reading.
**Pros:**
- The category-convention work is real and specific — the newest batch deliberately takes on crowded categories with explicit bans ("SaaS: no chat bubbles, charts or padlocks; finance: no coins, piggy banks or bank blue; health: no crosses, pills or heartbeat lines"). That is the same negative-space discipline this brief has been arguing for, aimed at logos.
- Optical corrections named and handled — overshoot, bone effect, irradiation — are the difference between a drawn mark and a traced one, and they are almost never in an AI tool's vocabulary.
- Portability is unusually good: plain Markdown instructions, Python 3.8+ standard library only, works across six different agent CLIs, and PNG export falls back through cairosvg / rsvg / Inkscape / Chromium / Quick Look.
- It has actual tests in CI and six releases — evidence of maintenance rather than a screenshot-driven launch.
- The one-colour and 3:1 contrast checks being applied even to the deliberately "vivid, saturated" batches is the correct order of operations: expressiveness inside a constraint.

**Cons:**
- **The library's licence boundary is a real hazard if anyone is careless.** The skill, scripts and templates are MIT; the 1,400 SVG files are trademarks of their owners, "included for reference and education only and **not** covered by the MIT license." A tool that puts a folder of real trademarks on a machine next to a generative model is one sloppy prompt away from a look-alike, and the README's never-copy rule is a policy, not a control.
- The output is **still a model's idea of a mark**, filtered through a checklist. The twelve principles and the audit can certify that a mark is well-constructed; they cannot make it interesting. A skill that passes every test can produce something technically correct and entirely forgettable — and for a department, forgettable is the actual risk, not badly drawn.
- It is an **identity-only** tool. It has nothing to say about the layouts, decks, cards, signage and clinical collateral that carry Shareef's marks in the real world, which is where most institutional identity work actually fails.
- The example brands are firmly small-business-to-mid-market SaaS and DTC in register — the mockup industries are coaching, apps, festivals, gyms. Nothing in the shipped examples resembles a hospital, a university department or a professional-services institution.
- 5,775 stars in five weeks is a genuinely popular tool, which means the moment it matters the "avoid look-alikes" pressure reverses: a well-known skill produces a well-known set of solutions.

**Should we...**
- ✅ **Adopt it**
**Why:** Adopt the **test battery and the checkpoint**, and treat the twelve principles as a reading list. The battery is immediately and cheaply useful: Montefiore Urology already has marks and a navy, and the question for every future application — a fellowship lockup, a resident-alumni sub-brand, a conference identity — is "does this survive 16 px, one colour, reversal, a squint, and a shelf of competitor identities?" Running that as a checklist before anything goes to a stakeholder is the single cheapest quality upgrade available on the identity surface, and `preview_sheet.py` builds it as one HTML page. The checkpoint applies to our whole creative review as a policy rather than a tool: *concepts first, one image, a recommendation, and no production until a direction is chosen.* That is how to stop the residency programme drowning in fourteen half-finished options per announcement, and it costs nothing to adopt as a rule of process this week. The classification statistics are worth quoting to anyone who proposes a gradient-and-three-colour departmental mark: "median 2 colours, 75% use ≤ 3." Where it fits and where it doesn't: this is a **brand-identity** tool for new/satellite marks and for auditing existing ones, not a UI, marketing or platform tool — the Unified Platform's SVG icon set and the Chapter Player's light theme are governed by systems already in place and should not be touched by it. The one firm caution: because the repo bundles real trademarks beside a generative agent, if we ever put it on a shared machine the first thing we do is quarantine the library folder, and we treat "never to copy" as a control we enforce, not a rule we hope holds.

### genai-mesh-gradient — controllable gradient construction, with its own verification state published
**Link:** https://github.com/9929y/genai-mesh-gradient (MIT; in the GitHub index still resolvable at https://github.com/9929y/mesh-gradient-generation — both return HTTP 200, the repo was renamed after creation; created **24 October**, pushed **31 October**, **102 stars, 7 forks**)
**Type:** Tool
**What it is:** A bilingual (EN/中文) agent skill for making mesh gradients that are *decisions* rather than dice rolls: given a use case, an aspect ratio, brand colours and a reserved text-safe area, it first settles **palette roles, where the main colour masses sit, how edges merge, and what must remain editable later**, then builds natively in Figma via Gaussian-mask groups with separate colour fills (returning node IDs so individual layers can be recoloured later). Six named native constructions ship with their actual parameters published: **Sky** (broad diagonal current, 3 curved planes + 2 light fields, 125–155 px blur), **Dreamlike** (offset light and shadow, 2 planes + 4 fields), **Flower** (magnified cropped petals, 4 translucent planes at **56–85% opacity**, 125–160 px blur), **Diffuse**, **Heightmap** (a warped multi-peak field with **40 contour levels** extracted by marching squares, 24–38 px blur) and **Multipoint** (eleven Gaussian alpha-mask groups). Four browser-based palette studies (monochromatic, analogous, apricot/cobalt, cool-neutral) share one composition so palettes can be compared without changing anything else. It also ships a stdlib Python SVG renderer with a `--seed`, and motion guidance.
**What's good about it:** The headline is the **honesty**, and it is rare enough to be the whole reason this item is here: the README states plainly that the six-study builder and its Gaussian-mask helper "have now been executed in live Figma and their actual renders inspected," while the browser layout specs "still need live-target calibration," and it repeats that distinction in both languages, per-artefact, in the guide. A tool that publishes which of its own claims are verified and which are not is doing the thing this brief asks of everything else — and it is the first design tool in weeks to do it about itself. Second, the *method* is art direction rather than generation: it asks for the **reserved text-safe area** up front, compares palettes **holding composition constant**, and insists on layers that stay editable. Those three constraints are what separate a background from a screenshot of a generated image, and they map directly onto the actual defect in most AI deck and hero backgrounds — a beautiful field that destroys the headline sitting on it. Third, the **named, parameterised constructions** are a real vocabulary with real numbers: "four translucent planes at 56–85% opacity, 125–160 px blur" is a recipe another designer can execute and correct, not an adjective. The Heightmap construction — extracting 40 contour levels from a warped height field — is the most interesting one, because contour extraction from a field is a *design-history* move (it is how topographic and engraved ornament has been drawn for two centuries) arriving inside a gradient tool.
**Pros:**
- Its verification state is published per feature, in both languages, including the parts that failed — that is falsifiable documentation, and it should become the bar we apply to every tooling claim we read.
- The palette-comparison-on-a-fixed-composition workflow is the correct way to make a colour decision, and it is executed here rather than merely advised.
- Real, editable Figma structure (Gaussian-mask groups + separate fills + node IDs) rather than a flattened export, so a designer can go back and correct one mass without rebuilding.
- Text-safe-area and desktop/mobile crop logic are built into the brief rather than left as an afterthought, which is the actual production problem with gradient backgrounds.
- MIT, no vendor gravity, and the sample palette (blue with apricot and warm white) is a disciplined, non-default pairing rather than the purple-blue everyone is trying to escape.

**Cons:**
- **102 stars, one author, no external users.** The samples are attractive; the claim that this produces *repeatable* results across a team is untested, and the browser path is self-declared uncalibrated.
- It requires a **live Figma write connection** for the native path — it explicitly does not supply the connector or sign in. That is honest, and it also means the good half of the tool is unavailable to anyone not already deep in Figma with an MCP-style bridge wired up.
- Mesh gradients are themselves **slop-adjacent vocabulary right now**: blurred colour blobs are the defining visual of the last two years of AI brand work. A precise tool for making them more carefully is still a tool for making the thing everyone is tired of.
- The recipe library is six constructions. Six is a catalogue, not a system, and once they are shared they become six recognisable defaults — the same convergence problem Hallmark's fifty themes have, at smaller scale.
- Documentation is bilingual by hand, which doubles maintenance on a project with one contributor and no releases.

**Should we...**
- 👀 **Watch it**
**Why:** Watch, and steal the verification-state discipline today. The reason this is a Watch and not an Adopt is a design-director judgement rather than a quality one: the craft is real, but the artefact it produces is a blurred colour field, and blurred colour fields are the single most over-used signal of AI-mediated design in 2026. Sharing a more precise way to make the most exhausted form is not obviously progress. What *is* progress, and worth taking this week, is the constraint set it encodes: **name the palette's roles before you pick colours; reserve the text-safe area before you compose; hold the composition fixed while you compare palettes; keep every mass editable.** Those four rules would improve the residency announcement cards and any Sub-I cover immediately, they cost nothing, and the third one alone would end the common review pathology where someone changes the palette, the layout and the crop at once and nobody can say what made it worse. Where it could genuinely land: **presentation decks and the Sub-I welcome cover**, where a controlled background that protects a headline is a real production need and the tool's brief shape matches the task. Where it must not go: the **Unified Platform**, whose dark chrome deliberately builds depth from surface steps and hairlines with no gradient fields at all — introducing mesh gradients there would be a direct violation of the system, not an enhancement. Watch for external users and for the browser-path calibration to actually happen; either would raise this to Adopt for the deck surface specifically.

### "Writing on the Wall?" — Sixth Tone on what AI actually does to typography
**Link:** https://www.sixthtone.com/news/1019200 (Sixth Tone, Voices & Opinion, **2 November 2026**)
**Type:** Idea
**What it is:** A dated essay making the structural argument that the AI threat to typography is not "a model draws letters" but something more precise: **the typeface as a unit is dissolving.** The setup — that OpenAI's Image 2.0 "was able to produce beautifully designed documents featuring long passages of text in multiple languages, while maintaining a high degree of accuracy," and that this removed the last comfort of type designers — gives way to the real analysis. Most typographic practice descends from movable type: a few dozen Latin letters, reused constantly, so Gutenberg's whole process of carving, reproducing and reusing individual letterforms made economic sense. Chinese characters invert every assumption of that: 3,500 cover 99.5% of usage, but the industry sets 7,000 as a working minimum and some projects run to 27,000 or 87,000 — because a character outside the common set that simply cannot be rendered is an unacceptable failure. That is why Chinese printers who invented movable type *earlier* than Gutenberg nonetheless carved whole pages onto woodblocks for centuries. The essay then shows where AI actually helps: foundries have used internal models for years to extend a few hundred directional characters into thousands, and open-source tools let independent designers do it alone. And then the sharp part — models like Qwen, Seedream, ChatGPT and DALL·E are moving toward a system that **bypasses typefaces entirely**: text is drawn directly onto posters, video and social covers in whatever visual style is prompted or hand-demonstrated. The model doesn't select a font. It invents one, per artefact.
**What's good about it:** It gets the mechanism right where almost all AI-and-design commentary gets it wrong. The mainstream story is "AI will replace designers"; the accurate story, which this essay tells clearly, is that **AI attacks the unit of reuse.** A typeface is an asset precisely because it is made once and applied ten thousand times. A model that draws the page does the applying without ever making the asset — which means every artefact is a one-off, the system has no continuity, and the brand cannot be enforced. That reframing is more useful to Shareef than any tool this week, because it names *why* AI-generated marketing looks like AI-generated marketing: not that the letterforms are bad, but that they belong to no family. Second, the design-history literacy is real and load-bearing rather than decorative — the woodblock-vs-movable-type comparison is the right analogy and it is used to explain economics, not to sound cultured. Third, the essay's closing argument is unusually good for an industry-defence piece: professionals "will no longer monopolize the creation of stylistic Chinese characters," and the role that remains is "gatekeepers of taste and judgment," with decades of training continuing to "shape a philosophy that pioneers future designs, regardless of what technology is involved." That is a claim a design director can stand behind without pretending nothing has changed. Fourth, it is honest about the uncomfortable near-term: for a layperson making a poster or brochure, Image 2.0's output "was just fine."
**Pros:**
- It supplies the correct vocabulary for the problem we actually have: the loss isn't craft, it's **continuity** — type drawn per artefact cannot be a system, and a brand without a type system is a mood.
- The economics are explained with numbers (3,500 / 7,000 / 27,000 / 87,000 characters) rather than slogans, which makes the labour argument concrete and quotable in a stakeholder conversation.
- It documents the hybrid that already works — human-designed directional character set, model-extended coverage, human adjustment — which is the realistic model for AI in production generally, not just in type.
- The argument for what professionals keep is specific and defensible: judgment and taste as gatekeeping, not production as monopoly.
- Dated today and free; a genuinely current read rather than a think-piece resurfacing from months ago.

**Cons:**
- **Almost no visual evidence.** It is an essay with header images; it never shows you a generated font beside a designed one, or a model-drawn poster set beside a typeset one. You have to take the quality claims on trust, and the central claim — that model-rendered text is now visually adequate — is exactly the claim that needs evidence.
- Every named example and datum is Chinese-language. The Latin-script translation of the argument is left as an exercise, and the situation genuinely differs: with 26 letters, model-rendered text is much less of a threat to the *typeface* and much more of a threat to the *layout*.
- It is an opinion piece in a "Voices & Opinion" section, authored by a practitioner with an interest in the conclusion. The sympathetic framing is not wrong, but it is not reported either.
- No cost, licence or licensing-framework discussion, which is where the actual institutional risk sits — a hospital department cannot use a font that was "invented per artefact" and has no foundry behind it.
- It arrives at "keep experimenting" as the prescription, which is the least actionable sentence in it.

**Should we...**
- ✅ **Adopt it**
**Why:** Adopt the argument, which is a correction to how we've been talking about all of this. The briefs this past week have been full of tools that make generated output *survive* a design system — Hallmark's themes, ai-design-check's inventory, unslop's direction step. This essay explains why that work exists at all: a model that draws text onto a poster instead of setting a poster in a font has removed the reusable unit, and the entire anti-slop industry is, in this reading, a set of prosthetics for a missing type system. Where it lands for Shareef: the residency programme and the Sub-I materials run on a system whose continuity is Inter plus a navy — which is *already* the right answer and worth stating as a principle rather than accepting as a default. The concrete move it justifies is small and permanent: **whenever AI touches a marketing artefact, the type must be set, not drawn** — no generated lettering, no model-improvised logotypes, no per-post type treatments. That single rule does more for perceived premium quality than any tool in this brief, because it preserves the one thing that makes a series of announcements read as a programme rather than as fourteen unrelated posts. On the platforms, the argument is already institutional policy and this essay is good ammunition for anyone who proposes letting a model generate UI type. The caution: it is an essay about Chinese typography, and the transferable part is the *mechanism*, not the specifics — do not quote the character-count economics at anyone as though they applied to Latin.

### Prada "Drawing Prada" — a luxury house hired back the people who drew the classics
**Link:** https://www.creativebloq.com/news/pradas-hand-drawn-holiday-campaign-is-an-utter-joy-and-a-sharp-lesson-for-coca-cola (Tom May, **29 October 2026**; the campaign itself: https://youtu.be/jTiRRC-I1So)
**Type:** Idea
**What it is:** Prada's Holiday 2026 campaign places its celebrity ambassadors — Sadie Sink, Maya Hawke, Chen Haoyu, Kelvin Harrison Jr, Luther Ford — in cartoon worlds **drawn entirely by hand**, directed by **John Pomeroy** (started at Disney in the 1970s, co-founded the Don Bluth studio behind *The Secret of NIMH*, returned for *Pocahontas*) with lead animator **Tom Bancroft** (*Beauty and the Beast*, *Aladdin*, *The Lion King*, *Mulan*). A sardonic fox trails Maya through the snow; a goofy lynx peers from behind a tree at Kelvin; three mice make off with Sadie's spotty loafers; a bat carries off Luther's shoulder bag. The behind-the-scenes reel is the actual content: "What's great is that John called the band back together. He called a bunch of us that had been working at other studios for many years doing traditional hand drawn animation. It's great to be back and doing this for Prada." Creative Bloq's framing sets it directly against Coca-Cola, which has spent the last two Christmases inserting AI-generated effects into its "Holidays are coming" ads and boasting about it: "a company valued at $400 billion? Do me a favour."
**What's good about it:** The strategic move is more interesting than the execution, and it is this: Prada did not *reject* AI in a press release. It **visibly re-employed a specific, named, ageing craft** and put the craftspeople in front of the audience as the deliverable. "John called the band back together" is a better brand statement than any tagline in this year's luxury advertising, because it is a fact rather than a claim, and it is un-copyable by a competitor that has not spent forty years employing those people. Second, the assignment of craft to *medium* is precisely right: hand-drawn animation is the one technique whose value is inseparable from its labour, so it cannot be replicated at any price by a model — and unlike Prada's iconic print and product work, there is no stock-photo substitute for a fox drawn by a *Mulan* animator. Third, the campaign is built on celebrity, which usually flattens character, and instead uses the cartoons to give each ambassador a different *comic* situation — the celebrity is the straight man and the drawing is the wit. Fourth, it demonstrates a hierarchy that most institutional work gets wrong: the drawings are disciplined, mid-century, Disney-school construction, not a stylistic pastiche of "hand-drawn" as a texture filter. Every one of those characters can be animated, and was.
**Pros:**
- A clean model of *provenance as the product*: the value is the named people and visible craft process, not the finished frame — which is the same argument Apple Music's rebrand made in October, but human-labour rather than workshop-labour.
- It proves the counter-position is a budget choice, not a moral stance — and it publishes that choice as a behind-the-scenes asset, which is the cheapest part.
- The construction quality is a real standard: cartoon characters designed with Disney-school line and appeal are a discipline our AI-generated "illustrative" attempts do not approach.
- It gives Shareef an argument with teeth in the internal AI debate: a $400bn company chose hand-drawn for its highest-stakes campaign of the year.

**Cons:**
- **Unreachable as production for us.** Pomeroy and Bancroft — this is a roster that exists in perhaps a few dozen living animators. There is no small version of "call the band back together" for a urology department.
- It is a *fashion holiday* campaign, which means the emotional register (whimsy, snowfall, a fox stealing a handbag) is the opposite of what institutional medicine needs; the technique transfers as a principle, not a mood.
- The write-up is admiring rather than evaluative — no cost, no discussion of how the campaign holds up at feed size or in a bad reproduction, and no admission that a luxury house can afford a craft position that a department cannot.
- "Hand-drawn good, AI bad" is by now the design press's default frame, and it flatters the reader. The essay never engages the harder question — whether a mid-budget institutional campaign using AI-assisted *layout and retouching* with hand-drawn *hero illustration* is the realistic middle.
- The campaign has no obvious measurement story. It is delightful, which is not the same as effective, and Creative Bloq's affection is doing the analytical work.

**Should we...**
- 👀 **Watch it**
**Why:** Watch, and take one thing: **hire the drawing, not the drawing tool.** The realistic version of this for Shareef is not animation — it is the observation that when we have 28 faculty interview clips, real clinical rooms and a limited budget, the highest-leverage craft purchase is a small number of *commissioned* visual assets (a hand-drawn motif, a custom letterform, a single illustrated device that recurs) rather than more generated imagery. That is precisely the White & Case "give photography a rule" idea from 1 November arriving from a different direction, and it is affordable at our scale in a way that Pomeroy is not. The reason this stays a Watch is that the campaign itself is a statement about *luxury's* relationship to labour, and copying the register would be both expensive and wrong for a hospital — whimsical snow-forest drawings in a Sub-I packet would read as unserious. What we genuinely cannot copy is the budget; what we can copy is the discipline of putting the *maker's name and process* in front of the audience, which for us means crediting our photographers, illustrators and faculty rather than anonymising them behind a house style. Watch whether Prada publishes any effect data and whether the "band back together" model gets picked up by mid-market brands at workable prices — if a real commissioning market for named traditional illustrators develops at institutional rates, that changes what residency creative can be.

## Slop Radar
- **The "Design Director" persona as prompt.** Today's X results are full of it again, in a new costume: not "act as my design director" this time, but bios — "Design Director at Meta Superintelligence Lab. Prev: Linear / Substack," "Design Director + Creative Partner, let's make something beautiful together" — surfacing as *search results for design authority* with no design content attached. The title is being used as a credential to borrow; ask for the constraint document, not the job history.
- **Blurred colour fields as the universal premium signal.** Everyone has converged on a soft mesh gradient behind everything, and now there are precise tools for making them more carefully. Careful execution of an exhausted form is still an exhausted form — the giveaway is a hero where the background was designed and the headline was not.
- **Model-invented lettering in marketing copy.** The image models now draw text well enough that nobody notices it was drawn. That is the actual danger in the Sixth Tone essay: a poster whose type belongs to no family will look fine alone and look like nothing in a series. Institutional rule: type is set, never drawn.
- **The "AI tools are UX assistants, not creative directors" consensus piece.** SDxCentral published one today and it is entirely reasonable and entirely generic — the dishwasher-next-to-the-sink analogy, "design smell," "AI smell," "quantity is not a substitute for quality." This register now appears weekly with no craft detail. When every vendor and outlet agrees that AI is an assistant, the sentence has stopped carrying information.
- **Anti-AI positioning as a launch strategy.** Maxon teased another Cinema 4D AI feature this week and got the predictable wall — "a slop generator, cool," "you lost your mind and don't even know your customers" — which is the same reaction Maxon Digital Twin drew in January. A teaser with no detail producing a verdict is a tell about the audience, not the tool. Neither side of that fight is teaching us anything about craft.

## Overall Assessment
The strongest signal today is a correction, and it comes from the least expected place. Every brief this fortnight has been tracking tools that make AI output survive a design system — themes, rule checkers, directors, floors, gates. Sixth Tone's essay explains the reason that whole cottage industry exists, and it is not that models draw badly: it is that a model which draws text onto a poster instead of setting a poster in a typeface has destroyed the unit of reuse, and continuity — the thing that makes a series of artefacts read as one programme — is the first casualty. That reframes the tooling: florilegium's crowning virtue is that it is *seeded and therefore reproducible*; logo-design-skill's crowning virtue is that it *stops* and refuses to ship options nobody chose; even the mesh-gradient tool's most valuable feature is that it publishes which of its own claims have been verified. The through-line across all three is not generation quality. It is **accountability**: reproducibility, refusal, and honest documentation of what was checked. Meanwhile the craft counter-position hardened — Prada re-employed the specific people who drew the Disney classics and put them on camera, which is a move no prompt can imitate and no mid-market budget can fully reach.

**The single highest-leverage move for Shareef this week:** write the **type rule** and put it where the tools can't argue with it. One sentence — *in any AI-touched artefact, type is set in Inter (or the dedicated display face), never drawn by a model; no generated lettering, no model-improvised logotypes, no per-post type treatments* — and then enforce it at the two places drift enters: the residency/social creative brief, and the review checklist. It is the cheap, permanent version of the argument Sixth Tone makes, it protects the one thing that makes fourteen announcements read as one programme, and unlike every tool in this brief it requires no adoption at all. Then spend the afternoon it saves running logo-design-skill's test battery against the existing Montefiore Urology mark — 16 px, one colour, reversed, squint, shelf — because that battery is the cheapest quality check on the identity surface available, and the answer will either confirm the mark is sound or tell us something nobody wants to hear at a conference deadline. Reproducibility, refusal, and an honest test. Three things, one afternoon, no new stack.
