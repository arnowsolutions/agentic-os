### Obel Mono / Obel Mono Masked
**Link:** https://typographic.systems/journal/obel-mono-1-000 · the masking write-up: https://typographic.systems/journal/a-typeface-that-keeps-a-secret · published **4 November 2026** (both posts, verified in page metadata) · free, with a plain-words licence · by Cody Collier, Typographic Systems
**Type:** Tool
**What it is:** A monospace typeface — 1,618 characters, nine weights, true italic — drawn specifically for reading code an agent wrote rather than code you typed. It ships in two builds: *Obel Mono*, which has the key-masking behaviour as an optional OpenType feature (`ss01`), and *Obel Mono Masked*, which has it always on for applications with no font settings at all. The masking recognises 205 key prefixes from 82 issuers plus 691 context patterns, and draws the secret as dots in the font itself — no plugin, no extension, nothing for the app to know about.

**What's good about it:** Three decisions carry the whole thing, and each is a rule rather than a preference. **First, it was drawn at 12px on a Retina screen** — the size code actually sits at — with every letter judged there first, inverting the normal workflow of drawing large and scaling down. The author's point is that "there's nowhere to hide at that size": straight strokes line up with the pixel grid, counters stay open, and `0 O` and `1 l I` stay apart. **Second, every character is exactly one cell wide at every weight**, so bold never shoves a line sideways and the lines of an ASCII box actually meet — the most common failure in terminal typography, solved as a constraint rather than a tweak. **Third, it draws the marks agents make** — ⏺ ✢ ✳ ✶ ✻ ⎿ ❯ ✓ ✗ ○ ◐ ▸ █ ░ ⠿ — on the same grid as the letters, instead of letting the system borrow them from another font at another weight and leaving a visible seam. The masking is the showpiece and it is genuinely well engineered: OpenType chained contextual substitutions with a **backtrack-only context**, so every rule looks only backwards and a character, once drawn, is final. Nothing on screen changes, which means a screen share never shows the key even for one frame. To make a shaper with no memory know it is inside a key, it swaps the underscore in `ghp_` for a second glyph with an identical drawing that the rules can tell apart — a note left hidden in plain sight. And the restraint is the professional part: "Knowing when not to" leaves `ghp_import`, `MONKEY=banana`, `SORT_KEY=modified_date` and public keys alone, and a mask ends at the first character that could not be in a key, so it never swallows the rest of the line. Tested across HarfBuzz, CoreText and DirectWrite on thousands of keys and near-misses.

**Pros:**
- The design method is copyable independent of the typeface: draw at the final size, in the real medium, and let the constraint (one cell, always) generate the decisions. That is a discipline, not a style.
- The masking is honest about its own limits in public — it hides pixels from screen shares, recordings and agents reading the screen; it does not hide copy-paste, the file on disk, shell history, or a wrapped line. A tool that states its failure modes is one a professional can deploy.
- Free for anything you make, selling included, with a single restriction: you cannot sell the font itself. No seat count, no subscription, no attribution theatre.
- It is genuinely original design engineering — the kind of thing that exists only because someone had a specific irritation and enough craft to answer it at the glyph level.

**Cons:**
- It is a *terminal* typeface. Alacritty does not shape text at all, so the rules cannot run there; Terminal.app and Warp are untested. None of it transfers to Figma, the web, a PDF or a poster, which is most of what Shareef's surfaces are.
- The security value is narrow and easy to oversell. It is a shoulder-surfing defence, not a secrets-hygiene one — the article itself says to rotate any key already seen, and "no font can take that back."
- A free typeface from one designer is a continuity risk in a way a licensed family is not. Obel Sans is announced but unshipped, and there is no foundry behind this.
- 1,618 characters across nine weights plus a true italic is an extraordinary amount of work to give away, which raises the question of how it is sustained — and the licence answers it only by implication.

**Should we...**
- ✅ **Adopt it** — use it now; it raises our design ceiling
**Why:** Adopt two things: the typeface, and more importantly the method behind it. The typeface is a five-minute install that makes every terminal session in this pipeline more legible, and the masking is a genuinely good idea that costs nothing to try — if Shareef ever screen-shares Supabase keys or deploy logs, this is the difference between a plausible risk and none. But the transferable lesson is the one that applies to his actual design surfaces: **Obel Mono was drawn at the size it would be read at, and every decision followed from that.** The Sub-I welcome packet and the platform's dense data tables have the same property and have never been treated that way — the Sub-I PDF is laid out at 100% zoom on a 27-inch monitor and then printed at A4, where the 11px captions become 8pt mush, and the platform's 13px table rows are judged on a retina laptop and read at arm's length on a ward desktop. Draw at final size first, then argue about everything else. The second transferable idea is structural and it sets up the rest of this brief: a font is a small program, and if a typeface can enforce a rule about what may appear on screen, then a tokens file, a component or a template can too. That is the whole argument against "we'll just be careful" as a design strategy.

### The Vignellis Are Back — *Design Is One* re-released
**Link:** https://www.printmag.com/daily-heller/the-daily-heller-the-vignellis-are-back/ · published **6 November 2026** · PRINT, The Daily Heller by Steven Heller
**Type:** Idea
**What it is:** The 2012 documentary *Design Is One: Lella & Massimo Vignelli*, produced and directed by Kathy Brew and Roberto Guerra, has been re-released in 2026 after years without distribution — now on streaming and broadcast with a two-year Netflix contract. Heller's column is the announcement plus the argument for why it matters now.

**What's good about it:** The doctrine is named in the piece and it is the precise counter-position to everything in this brief's Slop Radar: *"Their insistence on clarity, timelessness, responsibility and designing for everyday life feels remarkably relevant today."* Those four words are a design brief. Clarity is a hierarchy decision; timelessness is a refusal of trend; responsibility is an argument against producing anything you would not sign; and designing for everyday life is the reason a hospital's schedule page matters more than its campaign. Heller also notes what the passage of time has done to the film: with Lella and Massimo both gone, the documentary has become an intimate record of them speaking in their own voices about the principles that guided half a century of work. That converts it from an artefact into a primary source. And the practical fact is that it is now easy to watch — the distribution problem that kept it invisible is solved.

**Pros:**
- It is the standard that predates the slop. Everything in the AI-design conversation is a reaction to a technology that is two years old; this is a position developed across fifty years of practice that does not move when the tools change.
- An 80-minute film can be watched in one review session, which makes it a *shared* reference rather than a citation nobody has read — the difference between a team that has a standard and a team that has heard of one.
- The Vignelli answer to "what should we remove?" is unusually concrete for something so canonical: two typefaces, a grid, one accent, no decoration. It is directly comparable to what the platform already does.
- Heller's framing puts *responsibility* in the sentence, which is the word missing from almost every AI-design manifesto written this year.

**Cons:**
- It is a re-release, not new thinking. Nothing in it addresses generative tools, so every connection to 2026 has to be made by the viewer.
- The Vignelli canon is also the reason a certain generation of corporate design looks identical — Helvetica, a red accent, a grid, done — which is the same failure mode as AI slop wearing better manners. The film does not interrogate that.
- Heller's column is short and mostly a distribution note; the four-word doctrine is quoted, not argued.
- Nostalgia is a real risk with this material. Watching it can produce reverence rather than decisions, and reverence is not a design method.

**Should we...**
- ✅ **Adopt it** — use it now; it raises our design ceiling
**Why:** Adopt the standard, and use the film to install it. The most useful thing a design director can do against slop is hold a position older than the technology producing it, because that position cannot be dismissed as a taste preference. Vignelli's rules — a couple of typefaces used relentlessly, a real grid, one accent, nothing decorative — are the same rules Obel Mono is obeying at the glyph level and the same rules the Unified Platform already follows by instinct; watching the film together makes them explicit and gives Shareef a citation that is not his own opinion. Then run the one question it licenses on every residency and Sub-I surface: **what would we remove?** Not what should we add, not how do we make it pop — what is on this page that is not doing work. That pass costs an afternoon and would do more for how the department's creative reads than any tool in this brief. Keep the critique close, though: the Vignelli answer is not "Helvetica and red," it is "the decisions were made." A page that copies the answers without the decisions is a template, which is where we came in.

### TemPad Dev 1.0 — native, editable Figma layers for coding agents
**Link:** https://github.com/ecomfe/tempad-dev · 1.0 announced **4 November 2026** on X by the maintainer (@_justineo); repository live since 2024, 655 stars, MIT · Chrome Web Store: https://chromewebstore.google.com/detail/tempad-dev/lgoeakbaikpkihoiphamaeopmliaimpc
**Type:** Tool
**What it is:** An open-source bridge between Figma and a coding agent, shipped as a Chrome extension plus an MCP server. It works in both directions: it lets you inspect a design in the browser (CSS, variables, deep select, measure-to-selection), and it lets an agent **read designs, create and edit native Figma content, and implement the UI in your project**. The agent edits through real components, variables and styles rather than redrawing what it sees, and a bundled `design-in-figma` skill walks it through inspecting resources, making the change and checking the rendered result.

**What's good about it:** The claim that matters is *native, editable layers* — and the README is unusually specific about it, which is how you can tell it is real rather than aspirational. An agent that produces a picture of a settings screen has produced a reference; an agent that produces a settings screen built from your existing components, wired to your existing variables, has produced work. The direction of travel is also correct: most Figma-to-code tools stop at reading (generate HTML from a frame), and most generation tools stop at writing an image. This is the only one in this week's scan that closes the loop in both directions with the same object. Using the project's own components and styles as the vocabulary is the difference between "AI made a screen" and "AI made a screen that belongs to this system" — the distinction the entire premium/slop argument rests on. And it runs on the agent you already use, so there is no new model to trust, only a new pipe.

**Pros:**
- Editable native layers, not a flattened export — the output can be art-directed afterwards, which is the only kind of generated artefact worth keeping.
- Reuses the file's components, variables and styles, so the system constrains the output rather than being bypassed by it.
- MIT, open source, works with any MCP-capable agent (Codex, Claude and others) rather than locking you to one vendor's assistant.
- The inspect half is useful with no agent at all — CSS, variables, deep select and measure in the browser is a decent handoff tool on its own.

**Cons:**
- It needs Node 22+ and edit access to the Figma file, and it lives in a Chrome extension — a chain of dependencies that will break at least quarterly, in a department with no one to maintain it.
- 655 stars after two and a half years is a modest, single-team project; the 1.0 is a milestone, not an adoption signal. Bus factor is the honest concern.
- An agent with write access to a design file can make forty changes you did not ask for, and there is no described review gate — you are trusting the skill prompt and the model's restraint.
- Nothing here improves taste. It makes it cheaper to produce more screens in the system's clothes, which is the same trap as every generation tool: volume is not the goal.

**Should we...**
- 👀 **Watch it** — promising, not proven yet
**Why:** The mechanism is the right one and it is worth understanding now, even if the install is not worth it yet. Everything in the AI-design conversation has been stuck on the wrong unit: pictures of interfaces, which cannot be edited, cannot be reviewed and cannot be maintained. TemPad's unit is the layer — the same object a designer works in — and that is why it is the most interesting thing in this week's GitHub sweep despite being the least hyped. For Shareef the immediate value is as a test, not a tool: the Unified Platform's design system is the asset, and the question worth asking this quarter is whether an agent can be pointed at it and produce one page that is indistinguishable from a hand-built one. If the answer is yes, the next ten pages are cheap. If the answer is no, the reason will be that the system was never written down precisely enough — which is a finding about the system, not about the agent. Watch rather than adopt because a hospital department cannot carry a Node-version-dependent Chrome extension with 655 stars on the critical path, and because granting an agent write access to the design file before there is a review gate is exactly the kind of shortcut that produces the drift this brief exists to prevent.

### Figma's one-click object isolate
**Link:** https://x.com/figma/status/2118751599312937096 · announced **6 November 2026** · available now in the macOS desktop app
**Type:** Tool
**What it is:** A small update to Figma's AI image tools: select an object in an image and isolate it or erase it in one click. It replaces the previous lasso workflow from the December 2025 release, where you drew around an object before choosing Erase or Isolate; the new version drops the lasso for a single selection and is, per Figma, only possible at that precision in the desktop app.

**What's good about it:** Not the feature — the pattern. Every AI release that crossed this brief's scan this week was doing **cleanup**, not composition: a font that masks a key, an agent that edits layers, a tool that isolates an object. That is the correct seat for a model in a professional workflow, and it is worth naming because it is the opposite of what the last two years promised. A designer's tedious twenty percent is retouching, separating, extending, removing — labour with a known correct answer and no judgement in it — and that is precisely what these tools now do well. The moment the same model is asked to compose, it produces the average of its training set, which is the slop. Figma shipping the small, boring, useful thing rather than the impressive one is a signal about where the product organisation thinks the value is, and the answer is the same as this brief's: the instrument gets faster, the decisions stay human.

**Pros:**
- Removes a genuinely tedious step from real work — separating a subject from a background is the single most repeated operation in marketing creative, and one click instead of a lasso is a real saving.
- One-click is reachable by non-designers, which matters for a department where the person who needs the cut-out is often not the person who can use a pen tool.
- It is confined to the macOS desktop app, which is an honest limitation rather than a half-shipped cross-platform promise.
- Nothing about it changes your files' structure or invents content — it operates on material you already have, which is the safest category of AI feature.

**Cons:**
- It is a feature note, not a design idea. There is no craft here to learn from and nothing that raises a ceiling — it lowers a cost.
- Precision on hair, glass, semi-transparency and soft edges is where every isolate tool fails, and Figma has published no comparison; the demo will pick an easy object.
- It is macOS-only, so on a Windows or browser-only workflow the answer is "not for you," and Figma has given no date.
- The strategic reading — "AI is now cleanup, not composition" — is my inference from one week of releases, not a claim anyone at Figma made. It could as easily be that the impressive features are being held for a paid tier.

**Should we...**
- 👀 **Watch it** — promising, not proven yet
**Why:** The honest reason to note this is that it inverts the expectation of what an AI feature should be, and that inversion is the most useful idea of the week. For two years the pitch was that a model would design for you; the releases that are actually landing are the ones that take the boring half of the job. If that holds, the practical consequence for Shareef's program is a reallocation: the time saved on retouching is time that has to go into the decision — which photograph, which crop, which face, which frame of the 28 clips — because that is the part no click removes. The second consequence is a warning. One-click isolate is also how a flattened, unattributed, generated image gets separated into plausible-looking "assets," and plausible-looking assets are how a folder fills with material nobody can source or license. Watch rather than adopt because it is one feature in one app on one platform, and the design-director value is in the reading, not the button.

## Slop Radar

- **The brush-stroke display face** (named 5 November, still the most reliable tell): aggressive strokes, slight upward slant, shouty. It is not a font the model chose and cannot be excluded by name — it is lettering the model drew from posters it memorised. Ban generated lettering in imagery, not a typeface.
- **"We added AI" as the feature announcement.** This week's releases were all AI doing cleanup — isolate, erase, mask, edit — and none were AI making a composition decision. That is the right division of labour and it is worth saying plainly: a tool that removes a keystroke has not raised a ceiling. Judge tools on whether they change a *decision*.
- **Prompt-library accounts as design authority.** The X scan was dominated by accounts selling multi-paragraph "Swiss editorial / brutalist typography / premium magazine" prompts as a product. The prompts are competent and the accounts have no portfolio, which is the precise combination that produces work which is technically art-directed and entirely anonymous. If a brief can be pasted from a thread, it is not a brief.
- **Star-farmed repo families are still running, and one has grown.** The `fujc683/gpt-image-2-6-*` family now stands at **eight repositories**, all created 5 November, all at exactly 100–103 stars with zero forks: `pricing`, `free-tier`, `text-rendering`, `mcp-server`, `api-gateway`, `api-examples`, `commercial-use`, `openai-compatible`. One reseller, eight SEO landing pages. The `resocket`/`rpocket` cluster is also active and has diversified past FPS tweakers into design-adjacent bait — `claude-resocket-plugin` ("a check Claude can run against your Figma design") and `resocket-pixeljury` ("visual QA CLI for AI-built frontends"). The suffix is the tell; open the tree before you believe the description.

## Overall Assessment

The tools are converging on **the editable object**, and that is the week's real signal. Obel Mono is a typeface that behaves like a program — rules that decide, at draw time, what may appear. TemPad's 1.0 produces native Figma layers built from your own components rather than a picture of a screen. Figma's biggest AI addition of the week isolates an object so it can be worked on rather than generating something new. Even Vignelli's doctrine is, structurally, a rule set: two typefaces, a grid, one accent, nothing decorative — executable by a person instead of a shaper. Against that, the generation side of the market has stalled into the same poster with better kerning, which is why the most interesting releases this week were the small, boring, useful ones. The practical consequence is a change in what to ask for. Stop asking whether a tool can make the thing and start asking whether the thing it makes can be edited, governed and maintained — because that property, not the quality of the first render, is what separates an asset from a picture of one. **The single highest-leverage move for Shareef this week:** audit the department's live creative for anything that cannot be edited, and replace each one with a native original. The flattened flyer with the headline baked into the JPEG, the logo traced off a screenshot, the deck slide pasted in as an image, the poster whose only copy is inside a generated picture — every one of those is a thing the department does not actually own, and each is a five-minute fix now and an unfixable one in six months. If you cannot change the words, you do not control the message; and if you cannot change the type, you are not art-directing, you are choosing between pictures.
