## 1. Concordance (CTGT-Inc/concordance)

**What it is:** A measurement and reproduction package released Oct 21 by CTGT alongside a research post, built around one finding: their policy-ingestion pipeline returned four different sets of saved obligations from the *same* document, even though the model answered every byte-identical request identically. `compare_runs.py` takes captured model calls from two or more runs of any pipeline, groups identical requests, flags requests that are near-identical and names the exact lines that differ, and reports the first pipeline stage whose output diverged. Four directories: `compare_calls/` (the tool), `toy_pipeline/` (a three-stage demo that reproduces the failure against any OpenAI-compatible endpoint), `inference/` (SGLang batch-invariance benchmark) and `data/` (stage hashes from the production sweep, no prompts, no document text).

**Source:** https://github.com/CTGT-Inc/concordance · write-up: https://www.ctgt.ai/research/concordance · Provenance: VentureBeat, "CTGT releases open source tool Concordance to trace why AI agents make inconsistent decisions" (Oct 21) — surfaced in the Google News RSS scan.

**What's good about it:** The finding is the useful part, and it applies directly to us: *deterministic inference does not make your application deterministic.* CTGT got 800 of 800 identical replies at concurrency 40 with SGLang's `--enable-deterministic-inference`, and still got different committed results from the same document four times running. The cause was the software around the model printing per-run values into its prompts — a hash of the per-upload UUID, a 30-item list in shifting order, and a registry that grew between runs. Their example is worth sitting with: in the one request where the escalation rule was lost, the only difference between two runs was **four lines of asset IDs inside a 12,525-character prompt**, and the model demoted five conflict-resolution rules from "rules" to "background." That is the class of bug our cron jobs are structurally exposed to: every brief run generates a fresh temp path, a fresh timestamp, a fresh file listing, and any one of those quietly changes what the model sees. And the tool is the cheapest possible answer to it — `compare_runs.py` is stdlib only, model-agnostic, needs no weights, and works off captured request/response pairs alone.

**Pros:**
- Fits our constraints exactly: Python stdlib only, no GPU needed for the useful half (the inference benchmark needs SGLang on a GPU; the compare tool does not), Apache-2.0.
- Solves a real, expensive question: our briefs, digests and audits are supposed to be comparable day to day. When yesterday's inventory of 1,587 vault items becomes today's 1,590 and a section silently disappears, we currently have no way to tell whether that was a real change or a prompt that got four lines longer.
- The `toy_pipeline/` demo runs in under ten minutes against any OpenAI-compatible endpoint and prints a real before/after report — we can prove the mechanism on our own stack before trusting the tool.
- The README is unusually honest about its limits, which is a good signal: "Stable is not correct. Before the fix, one run's wrong classification of a block of rules replayed identically 8 of 8 times. Determinism makes an answer repeatable; it does not make it right."
- Evidence discipline is documented: the production numbers, the SGLang version, the tensor-parallel setting, prefix caching on/off — a rare case of a research release that states its own settings.

**Cons:**
- **Two commits, two contributors, nine stars, released yesterday.** Nothing here is a maintained product; treat it as a technique plus a 300-line script, not a dependency to build on.
- The engine that produced the production numbers is *not* in the package, nor are the prompts or captured calls — the toy pipeline reproduces the mechanism, not their pipeline. Their strongest claims are therefore unauditable.
- The result rests on one synthetic document and one pipeline; the write-up itself says how often this happens in real deployments "remains unknown." Do not over-generalize it into "our jobs are non-deterministic."
- The capture format is CTGT's own; to use `compare_runs.py` on us we first have to write the call capture into Hermes' cron wrappers ourselves — the tool consumes logs we do not currently produce.
- The inference half is misleading if read as a recommendation: their own numbers show speculative decoding (DFLASH, EAGLE3) swallowing the guarantee, 0 of 800.

**Should we...**
- ✅ **Adopt it**

**Impact on our stack:** Medium-High — **Hermes cron + agentic-os.** Concretely: adopt the *practice* (stable IDs instead of random UUIDs and timestamps in prompts; sorted lists instead of directory listings in arrival order), and vendor `compare_runs.py` to diff two runs of the same scheduled job. First application: the daily brief and the weekly capability audit, where a missing section is currently indistinguishable from a silently truncated prompt.

**Why:** This is an adopt only because the adopt is "take the script and the discipline," not "install a product." We already run 40+ cron jobs whose outputs are supposed to be comparable run to run, and we have been trusting them without ever checking that two identical runs produce identical work — which is exactly the assumption CTGT just dismantled on a pipeline far simpler than ours. Their failure mode is our failure mode: our wrapper scripts build prompts that contain fresh temp paths, upload identifiers and unsorted file listings, and a small change in that preamble can flip how the model treats the instruction block, as their four-asset-ID example shows. The concrete step is small and reversible: capture model calls in one cron job (the daily brief), vendor the stdlib comparer, run the toy pipeline against our own endpoint to see the report format, then audit the live brief for per-run values in its prompt. If the audit finds nothing, we've lost an afternoon and gained a guarantee; if it finds one of the three patterns they identified, we've caught a bug that has been silently reshaping our briefs for weeks. What we should not do is treat it as a framework, depend on the repo for anything long-lived, or believe its starred-ness — it has nine.

## 2. CVE Intel (Fazmin/cve-intel)

**What it is:** A private, on-device vulnerability triage app written in Rust (Tauri 2), shipped as one executable that is simultaneously the desktop app, a CLI (`cve-intel`), a REST API and an MCP server. Give it a CVE ID, a project's dependency manifest, or an IP/hash/domain, and it queries up to 38 public security sources at once (NVD, CISA KEV, FIRST EPSS, ENISA EUVD, Exploit-DB, Metasploit, PoC repos, vendor advisories) and returns one risk-scored answer plus what changed this week. It reads `poetry.lock`, `uv.lock`, `Cargo.lock`, `go.sum`, `go.mod` and `composer.lock` out of the box, and its local AI assistant runs against a one-click llama.cpp runtime unless you point it at a cloud provider.

**Source:** https://github.com/Fazmin/cve-intel · Provenance: GitHub Search API (new agent-adjacent repos, created 2026-10-18, 88★).

**What's good about it:** The gap it fills for us is specific. Our Unified Platform is React/Vite over a self-hosted Supabase, deployed by hand into `/var/www/...`, and nothing in our stack currently tells us "this dependency you shipped three weeks ago has a KEV-listed vulnerability." CVE Intel's lockfile import plus its MCP server is the shape we want: a cron job or the agent asks "what in this `package-lock.json` is exploited in the wild," and gets a ranked list back, with EPSS trend and whether Metasploit has a module. Local-first matters here more than it would for a consumer tool — nothing about our dependency set leaves the machine, and the MCP server and local model bind to loopback only. One executable for app, CLI, API and MCP means there's no Python venv to die on the next `docker recreate`, which is the exact failure mode I keep hitting with cron-side Python tooling. Linux builds are a `.AppImage` and a `.deb`, and running it with any argument drops straight into the CLI.

**Pros:**
- One self-contained Rust binary covers the four invocation modes we'd want (agent via MCP, cron via CLI, dashboard via REST, human via desktop).
- 38 sources cross-referenced per lookup with evidence attached — that is the difference between "CVE-2021-44228 is 100/100 CRITICAL" and "and here is why, and whether anyone is using it."
- Lockfile support targets the ecosystems we actually ship (npm via `package-lock`-style manifests, plus Cargo/Go/Python if we ever split the stack).
- Privacy posture is right for a hospital: no account, no server, no telemetry by default, loopback-only MCP, and read-only MCP access to the watchlist.
- The desktop app is the on-ramp; the CLI/MCP is the part we'd keep.

**Cons:**
- **Four days old (created 2026-10-18), one contributor, one open issue.** 88★ on a brand-new repo is promotion or a lucky HN-adjacent post, not adoption. This is watch-by-the-numbers regardless of how good the description reads.
- **License contradiction:** the README badge says `License: MIT` and links to `LICENSE`, while the GitHub API reports `Apache-2.0`. Today that's a documentation bug; in a year of contributions it's an argument. Resolve before any production use.
- The "on-device AI" and llama.cpp one-click install is a second runtime on the host with its own model downloads — real disk, real surface, and it's the part least relevant to us since we already have a model.
- Claimed CI and a test suite are not obvious from the repo metadata; the app is a Tauri desktop binary, which we would only ever use headless on the VPS, and the README's screenshots are all desktop.
- Clinical-adjacent scanning is a policy question: nothing here processes PHI, but "run a security scanner against departmental systems" needs to be a decision Shareef makes on purpose, not a cron job that quietly appears.

**Should we...**
- 👀 **Watch it**

**Impact on our stack:** Medium — **Unified Platform + Hermes cron.** If it holds up, a weekly cron job parses the lockfiles under `/var/www/` and posts a ranked "exploited-in-the-wild dependencies you're running" line into the existing failure digest. It is the one recurring security check we don't have.

**Why:** The honest read is that this is the right tool at the wrong age. Every part of the design says it was built by someone who has done vulnerability triage for a living — lockfile import, EPSS trend, "show me what changed this week," an MCP server nothing else on the market offers at this price point — and the local-first architecture is the only kind we could ever justify inside a hospital network. But the verdict rule exists for exactly this: four days old, one author, a license that contradicts itself between badge and file, and a desktop-first UX we'd never use. Watching costs nothing and we lose nothing by waiting two weeks: if it gets a second contributor, a CHANGELOG with real entries and a coherent LICENSE, the CLI plus MCP path on the VPS behind loopback is a one-command install. Until then the action is to note the gap (we have no dependency-vulnerability check) and optionally run it once, by hand, on a single lockfile, to see whether its ranked output is worth building around.

## 3. Co-op Anything (cranebeaker/coop-anything)

**What it is:** An MIT-licensed skill pack plus MCP server that you point a coding agent at so it can take a single-player game and make it playable co-op — researching existing mods, reverse-engineering the engine if nothing exists, building the netcode, testing with bots inside the game, and packaging it so friends join over Steam without port forwarding. 31 contributors, 809★, six days old, Python, pushed today, with released co-op mods for Deus Ex, Zelda: Ocarina of Time, Fallout: New Vegas and S.T.A.L.K.E.R. as proof the loop works.

**Source:** https://github.com/cranebeaker/coop-anything · https://coopanything.app · Provenance: GitHub Search API (new agent repos, created 2026-10-16, 809★); also named in today's GitHub trending lineup — Github Awesome, "GitHub Trending Today #55: free-my-tv, pele, SPACE, ascii-sharks, harp, DeepInfer, ElyOffice, fx0" — https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Ie9AjUvfw_s

**What's good about it:** Setting aside what it makes (game mods), the *structure* is one of the better agent skill packs I've read: a written `AGENTS.md`, seven named skills split into two explicit paths (`coop-research`/`coop-revive`/`coop-qa` for finishing existing work; `coop-reverse-engineer`/`coop-build-from-zero`/`coop-networking`/`coop-sync-testing` for greenfield), a playbook directory with one document per hard problem, automated QA against real game clients instead of unit tests that pass while the game desyncs, and a pull request back to the parent repo as the terminal step. It explicitly recommends pairing itself with REA (`npx rea-agents setup`) for the binary-inspection half, which is an unusually clean separation of "knowing what to do" from "looking inside the thing." 31 contributors in six days with 100+ commits is real community, not a star farm.

**Pros:**
- A genuinely good template for how to split one big capability into role-scoped skills with a decision table at the top of the README — directly reusable as a structure for our own `/deploy`, `/audit`, `/brief` skill families.
- The QA stance (test with real clients in the real environment, ship the test alongside the artifact) is the discipline our own deploys deserve and don't always get.
- Mature-looking repo hygiene: Docker, CI, docs tree, two contributors already shipping released mods.
- Free to read even if we never run it.

**Cons:**
- **The output is game mods.** Nothing in it improves Hermes cron, the Unified Platform, agentic CRM, email, voice, or any page we ship. Strip the framing and the transferable content is two ideas and a README layout.
- The work it performs is reverse engineering software the user may not have the right to modify, distributed as patches — the same policy line we flagged on REA two days ago, aimed at a different set of software.
- It requires the actual games and a Windows-class machine with Steam to run its own QA loop; the VPS is irrelevant to it and the desktop is not a place we want autonomous binary patching running.
- Six days old with a Ko-fi button and a Discord: the project's incentives (community growth) are not ours.
- 75 open issues already, which for a six-day-old repo says the maintainers are absorbing a volume of contributions they may not be able to review.

**Should we...**
- ❌ **Skip it**

**Impact on our stack:** Low — none of our systems. The reusable ideas (skill split, decision table, QA-against-real-clients) can be borrowed by reading the repo for an hour; adoption would add nothing.

**Why:** This is the clearest skip in a while, and it's not a criticism of the project. Co-op Anything is a well-run community effort with real shipped artifacts, but our unit of analysis is what makes *our* system better, and the deliverable here is co-op mods for single-player games. Filing it as "adopt" because the skill-pack structure is tidy would be exactly the failure mode the verdict rules warn about — mistaking good craft for fit. The one thing worth carrying over is free: the two-path decision table at the top of the README (is there existing work to finish, or is this greenfield?) is a better structure than most of our own skill docs, and the pairing with REA is the pattern we already decided to watch on its own merits. We keep it as reading, not as a dependency.

## 4. Lampline (C4rohan/lampline)

**What it is:** An Apache-2.0, self-hosted runtime for voice agents: it imports Google Dialogflow CX / CX Agent Studio exports (flows, pages, playbooks, forms, tools) and runs them on your own hardware, with a drag-and-drop builder, a test console that lights up each step as a call progresses, HTTP and MCP tools, and real phone calls over Twilio, Telnyx or any SIP trunk via jambonz. Built on Pipecat; 20 model providers including local Ollama; one `docker compose up` and everything is bound to localhost behind a key. The pitch is cost: ~$0.02–0.17 per call instead of ~$0.50 on the hosted providers.

**Source:** https://github.com/C4rohan/Lampline · Provenance: GitHub Search API (new agent repos, created 2026-10-19, 100★); the YouTube feed's ManuAGI video names Kai Resolve and ShipifyAI but not this — Lampline came from the direct GitHub scan.

**What's good about it:** We have a voice problem with a shape Lampline matches: any real phone-agent work is hostage to a hosted provider's per-call pricing and to data leaving the building, and Lampline puts the whole thing on our own box. The parts that make it credible rather than aspirational: built on Pipecat (a project with a real track record), a written engine test suite that covers Twilio, Telnyx, jambonz, the voice pipeline and a real WebRTC handshake, no telemetry, every request requires a key, and an explicit "agents as code" mode so a voice agent can live in git next to everything else we version. SIP transfer with the call's details passed as headers is the detail that tells me someone has actually built call flows — that's how you hand off to a human without re-asking the caller everything. And the Dialogflow import path means if we ever inherit an existing voice agent, the migration isn't a rewrite.

**Pros:**
- Genuinely self-hosted and private by default: localhost binding, per-request key, no telemetry — the baseline for anything touching patient-adjacent phone traffic.
- Provider-agnostic across 20 model providers plus Ollama, so it rides whatever endpoint we already pay for instead of adding a vendor.
- Built on Pipecat with a test suite that exercises real SIP/Twilio paths, not just unit-level mocks.
- Import from Dialogflow CX is the "don't rewrite your IVR" path — rare and commercially useful.
- `docker compose` deployment fits our existing container discipline.

**Cons:**
- **Three days old, two contributors, 138 commits, 100★, 11 MB of Python in one repo.** This is the definition of "watch at best" by the commit-and-author test, no matter how mature the README reads.
- **It needs a public webhook URL and a real phone number.** Twilio and Telnyx both require a carrier account, a purchased number, and an inbound HTTPS endpoint — which means exposing a port on the VPS through Traefik or renting a managed relay. That is the exact "can't do it where we'd need it today" gate.
- Caller audio and transcripts would flow through our container on a datacenter IP. Any real use touches PHI rules and Call-Recording consent; the README says nothing about HIPAA, BAA or retention.
- "Contact centers: Genesys, Avaya, Cisco, Amazon Connect, NICE, Five9, Twilio Flex — coming soon" is half the roadmap on one unchecked list. The commercial-adoption story is aspirational.
- The 20-provider matrix and the SIP stack are both large surfaces to own operationally for a department that would run maybe one phone agent.

**Should we...**
- 👀 **Watch it**

**Impact on our stack:** Medium — **voice.** If it matures, it is a drop-in replacement for hosted voice-agent pricing, running as one more container next to Unified Platform with a trunk we control.

**Why:** Every constraint that would make Lampline useful to us is a constraint about *where it runs and what credentials it needs*, and that is precisely the test that catches most voice tools. To take a single call it needs a carrier account with a real number and a publicly reachable webhook; to take a real departmental call it needs a BAA and a PHI story it does not currently document. So the verdict is watch, with a specific trigger: revisit when it has a second maintainer, a release tag, and any statement about healthcare deployment, and in the meantime the interesting thing is the *architecture* — Pipecat plus jambonz plus SIP transfer-with-headers is the stack we would build if we built this ourselves, and reading their engine docs is worth more today than installing them. Do not put this on the VPS "just to try"; a voice runtime with a public webhook is not a toy to leave running.

## 5. Video Short (petergyang/video-short)

**What it is:** An MIT-licensed agent skill (`npx skills add petergyang/video-short`) that turns a raw recording into a finished vertical short: transcribes it locally with NVIDIA Parakeet via HyperFrames, cuts retakes/false starts/dead air, lays out A-roll/punch/split/B-roll scenes, writes captions word-for-word, generates motion graphics as HTML, pulls B-roll from YouTube/X with yt-dlp, and renders the final video through ffmpeg. Its distinguishing feature is a **review page**: the draft plays on a scene timeline and you pin comments to exact frames, which the agent then iterates against. Optional AI visuals and music use Nano Banana, Gemini Omni Flash and Lyria, which need a Gemini API key with billing enabled.

**Source:** https://github.com/petergyang/video-short · Provenance: GitHub Search API (created 2026-10-18, 95★, MIT, 15 MB) — surfaced in the direct scan, not from today's YouTube feed.

**What's good about it:** Frame-pinned review is the thing the whole category has been missing. Our standing complaint about AI video tools is that feedback happens in chat, in words, about a moment you can't point at — "make the cut tighter there" gets you a new draft with a different problem. A timeline you can comment on at frame level changes the iteration loop from guess-and-regenerate to review-and-fix, and that is the difference between a tool Shareef would use for 28 faculty clips and one he abandons after two. The rest is solid plumbing: local transcription rather than uploading recordings anywhere, `yt-dlp` for real B-roll instead of stock filler, motion graphics authored as HTML so they're editable, and a skill format (`npx skills add`) that installs into the agent we already run rather than requiring a new app. It also handles the unglamorous 80% — retake detection and dead-air removal — which on a raw interview clip is most of the work.

**Pros:**
- Frame-pinned review page turns video feedback into something the agent can act on precisely; this is the actual innovation, not the generation.
- Local Parakeet transcription means faculty interview audio never leaves the machine — important for us specifically.
- MIT, a single `npx` install, and it degrades gracefully: no Gemini key still gives you cuts, captions, layouts and B-roll.
- Four real layouts (A-roll, punch, split, B-roll) match how a talking-head interview is actually edited.
- Builds on HyperFrames, a mature 69k★ Apache-2.0 renderer, rather than a bespoke pipeline.

**Cons:**
- **The parts that make it "AI video" — generated visuals and music — need a Gemini API key with billing.** Without it, this is a competent automated editor, which we partly have. The cost line is open-ended and per-render.
- One contributor, four days old, 95★, no open issues and no visible CI — the "no issues" here reads as "no users yet," not "no bugs."
- Inherited dependency weight: ffmpeg, yt-dlp, Node 22+, ~1 GB of speech model, and it drives the agent to fetch third-party video from YouTube/X — a licensing question the README doesn't address for departmental publishing.
- The workflow it assumes is a *talking head you can re-record*, not 28 existing faculty clips that were shot on someone else's schedule. Fit is untested.
- It's an agent skill, so quality rides on which agent runs it; on a light model the "cut retakes" step is where it will fail quietly.

**Should we...**
- 👀 **Watch it**

**Impact on our stack:** Medium — **social media content pipeline.** For the 28-clip library, the frame-pinned review loop is the piece that would let Shareef critique once and get a corrected draft, instead of describing changes in prose.

**Why:** The instinct here is to adopt because the social program needs exactly this and the skill installs in one command, but two things hold it back. First, the value is unevenly distributed: the review page and retake-cutting are genuinely new for us, while the AI visuals and music — the parts that justify calling it a video generator — require a billed Gemini key, which turns an MIT skill into a metered service we'd have to budget and monitor. Second, our input isn't a fresh recording filmed to a template; it's a library of interview footage already shot, and nothing in the README shows the skill handling that. Watching is the right call, and the cheap test is available now: install it, run one faculty clip through with no Gemini key, and see what the cut, captions and review page produce. If the review loop works on existing footage, adopt it for the pipeline and treat the Gemini key as an optional upgrade later; if it only works on re-shootable takes, we've learned that for the cost of one clip.

## Overall Assessment

Five scans, five items, and only one of them is a real adopt — which is the right ratio for a week where the loud items (Claude Duet, Muse) were already judged and the new repos were mostly six days old with one author.

**If we do nothing else, do these, in this order:**

1. **Concordance** — vendor `compare_runs.py`, run the toy pipeline against our own endpoint, and audit one live cron job for per-run values in its prompt. What it changes: we find out whether our daily briefs are actually reproducible, instead of assuming they are.
2. **CVE Intel** — one hand-run against the Unified Platform's lockfile to see if its ranked output is worth building a weekly cron around. What it changes: closes the "we don't check our own dependencies for exploited CVEs" gap, or tells us the tool isn't ready to.
3. **Video Short** — one faculty clip through the no-key path to test the frame-pinned review loop. What it changes: turns the 28-clip content library from a manual edit backlog into a review-and-approve workflow.

Lampline and Co-op Anything stay where they are: one waiting on a carrier account and a PHI story, the other filed as rejected so it never resurfaces.
