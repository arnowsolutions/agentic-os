### Andy Clarke — *Standout Designs for the Web*, and the six questions that precede Figma
**Link:** https://css-tricks.com/andy-clarke-standout-designs-for-the-web-interview/ · book https://css-tricks.com/product/standout-web-design-for-the-web/ · interview by Geoff Graham, **published 20 November 2026** · Clarke is founder of Stuff & Nonsense
**Type:** Idea / Work (a craft manual, and the procedure inside it)

**What it is:** An e-book from a designer with thirty years in, arguing that the problem with the web is not a lack of tools but a surplus of defaults. He names the condition **"commodity design"** — *"perfectly fine when all you want is fine, but it does little to make a company recognisable or its website memorable"* — and refuses the easy version of the argument: he is not against conventions, he wants designers to decide *when* to use them. The demonstration is a fictional company, **Wheelman**, a getaway-driver outfit whose characters and questionable business activities are used to show that personality can survive all the way down to a donation form, an e-commerce flow and a transactional page.

**What's good about it:** Two things, and neither is a style. **First, the procedure.** Clarke's method is not moodboarding, it is conversation: *"I spend a lot of time talking to clients because I'm looking for what makes them different. Sometimes, something they mention almost in passing becomes the inspiration for an entire design. The trick is recognising which details are worth developing and turning them into something visual."* That is a locatable, repeatable act, and it is the exact opposite of prompting — the material arrives from a person, not from a latent space. **Second, the grid argument**, which is the most useful technical position in the piece: *"I think twelve-column grids have become the default setting for web design. They're familiar and useful, but they're only one way to organise content. When designers begin every project with twelve columns, they're already limiting their possibilities before they've considered what the content needs."* He then goes through column, compound and modular grids, ratios, and symmetry against asymmetry. A designer who cannot name a second grid cannot choose the first one; he is asking for literacy, not rebellion.

**Pros:**
- **The closing instruction is a filter we can run on any brief:** *"Before opening Figma or Sketch, choosing a typeface, or deciding on a layout, I'd like them to spend more time discovering what makes the company they're designing for interesting. Then question their first design decisions. Does this layout suit the content, or is it just familiar? Does this typography express the company's personality?"*
- **He puts the AI question where it belongs — inside a craft question, not a technology one:** *"Can an illustration communicate something more effectively than an AI-generated image or a stock photo?"* Not "is AI allowed," but "does this asset say anything only this organisation could say." That is the only version of the question that has ever produced good work.
- **The Wheelman device is a serious teaching move.** By inventing a client, he can show the same page set twice — once generic, once with character — which is the before/after this whole brief keeps asking for and almost nobody publishes.
- **The honesty about scope:** *"I don't expect every website to be extraordinary, and I certainly don't think every design needs to break conventions."* A craft argument that admits most work should be ordinary is a craft argument you can trust.
- It is a book about layout, type and imagery written by someone who has shipped for three decades, in a week when the industry's loudest design content was a leaderboard.

**Cons:**
- **It is a sales page.** The interview exists to move a $ product in the CSS-Tricks store, and every answer lands within reach of the shop link. That does not make the thinking wrong; it does mean no failure is discussed and no example is shown at length here.
- **The AI comparison is a rhetorical question, not tested.** "Can an illustration communicate more effectively than an AI-generated image" is answered by assertion. There is no side-by-side, no budget, no case where the generated image won — and sometimes it will.
- **Twelve-column grids are not the villain they were in 2015.** Modern CSS grid, container queries and component-driven layout already broke the fixed twelve; the argument is true for page layout and slightly dated as a diagnosis of *why* interfaces look alike now.
- **"Commodity design" is a good coinage that explains less than it claims** — the sameness in AI-assisted work comes from a shared training distribution, not from designers being comfortable. Clarke's remedy treats the symptom he can see.
- No index of the book's exercises, no sample chapter, no page count in the interview — you cannot evaluate the artifact, only the argument.

**Should we...**
- ✅ **Adopt it** — run the procedure on the residency programme before the next campaign.
**Why:** The department has a brand document that says navy, Inter, real photographs, and no teal; it has no document that says what Montefiore Urology is *for* in a sentence a designer could build from. Clarke's method is the cheapest possible way to get one: an hour of questions, with the material coming from people rather than a prompt. The Sub-I welcome packet is the perfect first subject, because the answers already exist and nobody has ever written them down — what does an applicant hold in the first week, what did this department do that no other department did, what is the one story a chief resident tells at the dinner. That material is what makes a page unrecognisable as anyone else's, and it is precisely what no model can supply, because the model was not in the room. The grid half is the technical follow-through and it lands directly on the marketing creative: every social frame in this department currently starts from a twelve-column habit — a rectangle with a logo top-left, a headline in the upper third, a photograph below — and the sameness of our output is a grid decision that was never made. Name a second grid, use it once a quarter, and the series stops looking like stock.

### TaishoDS Core — set the contrast first, then compute the colour
**Link:** https://taisho.design/en/blog/accessible-color-palette-from-one-color · **published 20 November 2026** by Pablo Di Filippo · code https://github.com/taishoDS/core · MIT · `@taishods/core` on npm
**Type:** Technique (a palette method, and the failure that produced it)

**What it is:** A ten-step colour scale, 100 to 1000, generated from a single input colour — built on an inversion of the usual order. Instead of spacing lightness evenly and hoping contrast works out, **every step gets a target contrast ratio against the page background first** (on white: 1.08, 1.22, 1.5, 2.1, 3.6, 6, 8.5, 12, 16, 19), the starting colour is placed *untouched* on whichever step its measured ratio belongs to, and the other nine steps are found by searching OKLCH lightness for the ratio they must hit. Hue holds, chroma never exceeds the source colour's and eases off at both ends. Dark mode is computed again against the dark ground — *"It isn't light mode flipped."*

**What's good about it:** **He publishes the version of his own idea that failed, with the measurement.** The first engine did the obvious thing — keep hue and chroma, throw away lightness, lay out ten fixed OKLCH values from 0.97 down to 0.12 — *"It looked even, and I took it for granted that contrast came out right on its own. That was only an assumption, picked up from what I'd been seeing in the tools around me."* Then he measured it: 648 colours around the hue wheel, and **for 45 of them (about one in fourteen) at least one step had text below AA, the worst pair at 4.10**, and *"the brand color also never showed up in its own scale: pass in `#3b82f6` and you got ten blues, none of them `#3b82f6`."* That is a complete, reproducible critique of the palette method almost everyone uses, including every design system that ships an evenly-spaced ramp. Second: **the Tailwind comparison is done with numbers rather than taste**, and the numbers are embarrassing in exactly the right way — Green 500 at 2.25:1 on white against TaishoDS's 3.60, Yellow 500 at **1.93** against 3.60. Blue and Red land where everyone expects (3.71/3.70, 3.82/3.82), which is the tell: a hand-tuned palette is fine for blue and red and quietly broken for green and yellow, and nobody notices because most systems are blue.

**Pros:**
- **The stated principle is the one this brief keeps arguing for, in six words:** *"quality has to be enforced by the system, not by discipline."* A contrast floor that depends on a designer remembering to check it is not a floor.
- **The result is checked at scale, not asserted:** 4,096 starting colours × light and dark = 81,920 steps, minimum text contrast **4.632**, and it reports the same minimum computed in 8 bits (4.588) — the number that actually ships.
- **The step number becomes portable across hues.** After the rebuild, *"a 500 stands about as far from the background whether it's blue or yellow, and a 600 holds light text on top in both"* — which is what a token system needs and what a per-colour hand-tune never gives you.
- **The engine is 350 lines in one file** (`src/lib/theme/palette.ts`), MIT, with a theme editor on the homepage where you type a hex and the whole page repaints with each step's contrast shown. You can audit it in an afternoon.
- **It asks for its own counterexamples** — "if a color breaks it, open an issue with the hex and the mode" — which is rarer in a colour tool than it should be.

**Cons:**
- **Two stars, one author, ten days old.** No independent review, no accessibility audit of the tool itself, and the 4.63 figure is the author's own measurement of his own algorithm.
- **Optimising one axis flattens the others.** A scale where every step is pinned to a contrast target has no room for the step that exists purely to be quiet — and quiet greys, dividers and disabled states are most of what a dark product UI is made of.
- **The "same number means the same thing in any hue" claim is a real trade, not a pure win.** It is what makes tokens portable and it is also why generated ramps tend to look machine-made: the interpolation has no opinion about whether yellow at 600 should be a different *kind* of colour from blue at 600.
- **The Tailwind comparison is a little unfair.** Tailwind is a fixed, hand-tuned palette optimised for looking right; TaishoDS is a generator optimised for a contrast floor. Comparing them proves the floor works, not that Tailwind's designers were careless.
- **Contrast is not legibility.** A 4.6:1 ratio on 12px Inter is a pass and still hard to read; nothing here touches size, weight, measure or optical spacing, which is where most hospital UI readability problems actually live.
- The repository description and parts of the project are Spanish-language, which will slow adoption across a department.

**Should we...**
- ✅ **Adopt it** — as the method for the next palette revision, not as a dependency.
**Why:** The Unified Platform is a premium dark interface with a single accent, which means almost all of its colour decisions are already made by the background and the one accent — and the tokens that actually matter are the greys: the muted text, the dividers, the disabled states, the surface ladder. Those are exactly the steps that hand-tuned palettes get wrong, because nobody checks a grey against a dark canvas at small size. The inversion is the transferable part and it costs nothing: **write the target contrast for each role before choosing any value.** Muted body text against `#101112` gets a number; the divider gets a number; the disabled label gets a number — and then the value is computed or measured, not picked. Second, the failure story is the argument to keep in the file: if a ramp assembled from evenly-spaced lightness fails one colour in fourteen, then every "we used a 10-step scale, it's accessible" claim in this department is unverified, including ours. Third and most useful for marketing: the Chapter Player's light theme and the campaign collateral both draw on the same navy, and a single generated ramp per hue would end the drift between the UI navy and the marketing navy that nobody has ever reconciled. Run the generator, then throw away the steps that a real screen does not need.

### Floot Design — six directions on a canvas, then build one
**Link:** https://floot.com/docs/getting-started/design-canvas · **launched 17 November 2026** (co-founder @yyjhao) · states: https://floot.com/docs/getting-started/examples · product https://floot.com
**Type:** Tool (a comparison surface, bolted onto a build platform)

**What it is:** Floot is a remote MCP server — connect it once to Claude, ChatGPT, Codex, Cursor or any MCP client and the assistant writes, runs and publishes a full-stack app with database, sign-in, storage, email and hosting. **Floot Design** adds the thing that was missing: a canvas outside your pages where the assistant puts several designs side by side, each in its own frame, as **real React** — buttons, tabs and forms work inside a frame. You compare, double-click into the one worth pursuing, select frames and hit **Build**, and only then does anything reach the app. Canvases are development-only files (`designs/<Name>.tsx`) and never publish. Alongside it, **Examples**: your real page opened at a route with its backend staged, so loading, empty, failed, oversized-upload and long-list states appear on demand without breaking anything or touching the database.

**What's good about it:** **The documentation states the correct comparison discipline, unprompted.** *"A canvas is most useful when every frame varies one thing. Once the layout is right, compare fonts without the rest of the page moving"* — with a worked prompt that says *don't change the layout, colours, images, copy or spacing — only the typography*, and asks for the recommendation and its reasons in each frame's label, for a reason worth stealing: **"It is easier to disagree with a reason than with a picture."** Then the diagnosis of why most canvases are useless: *"Ask for options that are 'completely different' when you are exploring, and for 'only change X' when you are refining. Most weak canvases come from asking for both at once."* Second, and the part that matters more for this department: **Examples is the empty-state problem solved structurally.** Every brief in this file for a month has said that agents ship the happy path because that is the only state they can see; Floot's answer is to let the assistant write a set of example screens — loading, empty, failed, extreme data — where every request is answered from the screen's own staged data, and a screen can hold a request open forever or return an error status so the page's real error handling runs.

**Pros:**
- **The frames are real React, not screenshots**, so "which of these six feels right" is answered by clicking, not by imagining.
- **Nothing on a canvas can reach a user** until it is explicitly built — the exploration/production boundary is a file-system fact, not a promise.
- **One-variable comparison is the correct unit of design review**, and getting the instruction right is most of the battle; the docs write it for you.
- **Examples names the states the department never tests**: loading, empty, failed, oversized upload, long list — including the "sad path" and the extreme-data case.
- **The whole journey can be a canvas** — two alternative routes through a feature, each a row of frames, plus mobile widths, compared before either is built.
- It works inside the assistant the department already pays for, so there is no new editor to learn and no second subscription.

**Cons:**
- **It is a vibe-coding platform, and the canvas is the on-ramp.** Floot owns the database, auth, payments and hosting; adopting the review surface means adopting the build platform, which is the opposite of the vendor-neutral posture that makes the rest of this brief's recommendations safe.
- **Six options is the same volume trap in a nicer container.** The docs mitigate it with advice; nothing in the product enforces "these frames differ in exactly one variable." A canvas of six near-identical heroes is one idea printed six times, at six times the review cost.
- **No published output quality.** The launch is the co-founder's own post and three influencers repeating it; there is no public project, no case study, no before/after from a real repo — and the frames' quality ceiling is still whatever the model writes.
- **It is a hosted service with an unpublished data path.** For a hospital platform, "send the design brief and screenshots to our MCP server" is a compliance question before it is a design question.
- **Examples test *states*, not *content*.** Staged data is not real data; the long-list case that actually breaks a schedule grid is a real term's worth of resident names, not a fixture.
- The docs are excellent and the product is new — which is the combination that most often means the docs describe an intent rather than a shipped behaviour.

**Should we...**
- 👀 **Watch it** — and take the two docs sentences into this week's review.
**Why:** The transferable finding is not the tool, it is the sentence: **one variable per comparison, and label every frame with a reason.** That is a rule the department can apply to hand-made work tomorrow — every time three Sub-I cover options go up, they should differ in *one* thing and each should carry a written reason, so the conversation is about the reasoning rather than about which thumbnail someone likes. The second transferable thing is the state set. The Unified Platform has five surfaces and the ones that break in front of a patient are the empty, error and permission-denied states, and they are never designed because nobody can make them appear on demand. Floot's method — a route with its backend staged, so the failure is a click rather than an incident — is a pattern we can build into the platform's own dev harness for free, without the platform. What to refuse is the build side: a hosted service that owns the database and the deploy is the wrong dependency for anything that ends up holding a resident's name.

### HackerNoon — AI made us faster, so why do designers have less time?
**Link:** https://hackernoon.com/ai-made-us-faster-so-why-do-designers-have-less-time · **published 21 November 2026** · by Rinat Seif · tagged AI-in-design
**Type:** Idea / Technique (an essay with three process rules at the end)

**What it is:** A designer's attempt to explain the gap between the promise and the week. *"A poster that used to take me a day now takes a minute to generate. Last night I built an animated website in one evening without writing a single line of code. By every estimate, designers should have half their lives back. Yet I haven't met a single designer who has more free time."* His three explanations are all about the process rather than the model: **generation got cheap and choosing didn't** (three options became two hundred, and each one still has to be looked at and rejected for a reason); **clients know generation is cheap too**, so "by Friday" became "by tomorrow" and one revision became five; and **AI was dropped into the old process** — brief, references, sketches, approvals all unchanged, with a generate button in the middle.

**What's good about it:** **The METR finding is the most useful number in design this month, and it is about designers in the only way that matters:** sixteen experienced developers, expecting to be 24% faster, were **19% slower**, and afterwards believed they had been **20% faster**. The feeling of speed and the fact of speed have come apart — and the mechanism he names is the one every designer will recognise: *"Generating gives you the sense that work is happening even when it is stuck. For designers, it looks like an hour of rerolling one frame instead of five minutes thinking about what exactly is wrong with it."* Second, the professional observation that lands hardest: *"A component library keeps a UI consistent, but there is no Storybook for light, texture and framing."* That is a precise description of where AI-assisted visual work is weakest in a mature design system — the structural layer is solved and the photographic/atmospheric layer has no vocabulary at all.

**Pros:**
- **The three closing rules are immediately usable and require no tool:** decide light, background, palette and grid *before* the first generation and only vary the object; **if three rerolls don't get the frame right, stop and put into words what is wrong**; and select side by side on one screen rather than one at a time in a chat window.
- **"Show the client less" is the correct and unpopular advice:** three directions with a rationale, not a folder of fifty — *"The more options a client sees, the longer they take to decide and the more revisions they send."*
- **It separates the bubble question from the professional one properly.** Even if the financial bubble pops, the tools stay and the change in the job is already banked — the dot-com comparison is glib but the conclusion is right.
- **The Jevons framing explains a pattern every department has felt this year** and gives it a name: cheap images did not reduce work, they raised appetite.
- It quotes MIT NANDA (52 interviews, 300+ initiatives, 95% of organisations reporting zero return) and Upwork (2,500 workers, 77% saying AI added to their workload), so the argument has receipts beyond one studio.

**Cons:**
- **Every number is second-hand, and none of the studies are about design.** METR is sixteen developers; NANDA is enterprises; Upwork is general knowledge work. The translation to a design floor is plausible and unproven.
- **The three practices are not new.** Decide-before-generate is the "hidden brief" argument from 20 November arrived at from the other direction, and "show three options" is what a good studio has always done. The contribution is the diagnosis, not the cure.
- **No evidence his own workflow produces better work** — no before/after, no artifact, no time log for the claims about the animated site.
- **The bubble section is throat-clearing.** Five and a half trillion dollars of infrastructure spend does not change a single decision about a poster.
- **"Most designers aren't making extra money from it"** sits in the title of a companion piece rather than in this one, so the economic argument the headline promises is thinner here than expected.
- The AI-generated imagery illustrating the article is, by the article's own standards, irrelevant decoration.

**Should we...**
- ✅ **Adopt it** — the reroll rule and the side-by-side rule, this week.
**Why:** The department's actual failure mode is not bad prompts, it is **unbounded iteration**: a poster gets regenerated eleven times at 11pm, the eleventh is chosen because it is the eleventh, and nobody can say what was wrong with the third. The rule — three rerolls and then stop and write down what is wrong — converts that hour into a sentence, and the sentence is reusable. Applied to the Unified Platform's empty states and the campaign creative alike, it is the cheapest quality control available. The side-by-side rule is the other half: the 28 faculty interview clips and the campaign frames are *series* work, and series work cannot be judged one frame at a time, because the defect is always the relationship between frames rather than any single frame. Build one contact sheet per campaign and judge on it. And the "no Storybook for light, texture and framing" line is worth filing as a diagnosis of our own stack: we have tokens for colour and type and nothing at all for how a photograph is lit, cropped and graded — which is exactly why two campaign frames shot in the same room can look like two different departments.

### JimLiu/open-artifact — eight artifact types, plain files, no vendor
**Link:** https://github.com/JimLiu/open-artifact · **created 17 November 2026, pushed 22 November** · Apache-2.0 · **285★, 41 forks, 2 open issues** · formerly `baoyu-design`
**Type:** Tool / Workflow (a local artifact runtime with a comments loop)

**What it is:** A rebuild of an earlier project (`baoyu-design`) as an open, agent-agnostic format: **one local daemon, one page frame, one `/open-artifact` skill, and a plugin per agent app** — Claude Code, Codex and the ChatGPT desktop, Cursor, GitHub Copilot, Antigravity, ChatGPT on the web, DeepSeek Harness. You ask your agent for a deck, a UI design, a design system, a whiteboard, a spreadsheet, a dashboard, a doc or a short animated film, and it writes **a plain folder of files** — `deck.json` plus `slides/*.html`, `canvas.json` plus `.dc.html` artboards, `design-system.json` with a token grammar and CSS compiler. A local page opens it in a real editor where you fix a title, move a sticky note or leave a comment, and **"Send to agent"** routes those comments back into the agent's session through the `Artifact`, `ArtifactData` and `ArtifactComments` MCP tools.

**What's good about it:** **The format is the argument.** Every other artifact product in this genre keeps your work inside a proprietary document that only it can open; this one's output is a folder you can `git diff`, which means a design review produces a *diff* rather than a screenshot and a shrug. Second: **the comments loop closes in the right direction.** The reason design review of generated work is so bad is that feedback travels as prose typed into a chat box, detached from the element it refers to; here the comment is attached to the object and arrives in the session as structured input. Third, and unusually mature for a five-day-old repo: **the design-system type is a first-class citizen** — tokens, colours with contrast checks, type specimens, live component previews — and decks and designs *install* it, which is the same contract idea this brief has been building toward all week, expressed as a file rather than a paragraph.

**Pros:**
- **Local by construction** — *"Everything runs on your machine. No account, no upload, no specific model or vendor."* For a hospital, that is the difference between a tool that can be evaluated and a tool that needs a lawyer.
- **The export matrix is real and unglamorous:** PDF/PPTX/HTML/PNG for decks, XLSX/CSV for sheets, Markdown/Word/PDF for docs, MP4/GIF plus ZIP for motion. Output that a colleague without the repo can open is what makes a review happen.
- **Eight artifact types share one runtime**, so the design system you write for a deck is the same object a UI design installs — no re-keying tokens between formats.
- **The architecture is legible**: daemon / shell / protocol / per-type server, an editor per type, a WASM canvas engine. This is a person who has built document software before, not a weekend wrapper.
- **Apache-2.0 with a NOTICE file and a real release process** — changelog-gated publishing, plugin bundles attached to tags, unit tests, e2e tests that drive the built types.

**Cons:**
- **285 stars and five days old, by one author, as a rewrite of his own earlier project.** The plugin matrix is the warning sign: eight host apps, each with its own install path, and the README's own instruction is to re-extract the bundle and follow each app's update steps. That is a maintenance surface no single maintainer keeps current for a year.
- **Eight artifact types is a marketing decision as much as a capability.** Sheets has a formula engine and motion has MP4 encoding; a person cannot have brought all of that to the same level of finish in five days, and the README does not say which types are mature.
- **The design type is an infinite canvas of HTML artboards** — quality is whatever the agent writes into them. The tool supplies the frame and the comment loop, not the design.
- **Node 22.18+ and a local daemon on a managed hospital laptop** is an IT conversation, and the daemon is the part that has to keep running for comments to route back.
- **No evaluation, no case study, no third-party review** — the repository is the only evidence, and a README is a description of intent.
- The former name (`baoyu-design`) redirects here, so search results and older write-ups describe a different product.

**Should we...**
- 👀 **Watch it** — and copy the two ideas that cost nothing.
**Why:** Two mechanisms here are worth having today without the software. **First, plain files as the artifact.** If every generated deck and mock in this department landed as a folder of JSON and HTML in a repo rather than a screenshot in a Teams thread, we would have version history on design work for the first time — including the ability to answer "what did this look like when it was approved." **Second, comments attached to objects.** Every review in this department currently happens as prose: "the second one, the blue one, can the name be bigger." A comment pinned to the element, delivered back into the session that generated it, is the only form of review feedback that reliably changes the next draft — the same conclusion the 16 November brief reached about deletion lists. What to refuse is installing eight artifact types on a hospital machine to get them. The idea is a specification, and we can write the specification into our own tooling.

### Design Studio for Gutenberg — somebody built the kerning controls back in
**Link:** https://creativebloq.com/inspiration/i-built-a-powerful-new-web-design-tool-that-focuses-on-craft-not-automation-try-a-live-demo-now · **published 16 November 2026** · Creative Bloq, by Lance Evans · demo https://openstudiony.com/
**Type:** Tool / Idea (a counter-positioning piece, written by the maker)

**What it is:** A designer-first plugin for WordPress Gutenberg, $39 with a perpetual licence and per-person unlimited sites, built by a former Type Director at J. Walter Thompson's type arm. His starting complaint is a craft one: print and the 1990s desktop toolbox inherited centuries of typographic practice, and the web threw it away. His workaround, before building the tool, was to set headlines in Illustrator, kern them properly, export SVG and place the result as artwork — for clients including American Express, Merck and Lincoln Center. The plugin adds three blocks: a **Type Director Block** with real kerning, full font and style control not bound to a template, and mixed type inside one container; an **Art Director Block** that imports multilayer Photoshop or Krita files and lets you treat every layer independently — blur, invert, halftone, sepia, full blend modes, then scale, rotate and place it anywhere; and a **Composition Block** with a height slider and free placement so blocks can overlap or bleed off the edge. Plus rulers, guides, grids, per-breakpoint visibility, a blank canvas template, pre-flight checks and a font-management backend.

**What's good about it:** **The argument is the best-written statement of the craft position I have seen this week, and it is four sentences long:** *"Craft is deciding that two letters need to be kerned tighter. That an image needs to move three pixels down. That this composition should deliberately break the grid. That the mobile version shouldn't simply be a smaller copy of the desktop. Those are precisely the kinds of decisions that only professional designers should make."* Then the line the whole brief exists to argue with: **"Craft begins where the default answer ends."** Second: the piece is a clean example of **counter-positioning against automation** rather than against AI — he is not anti-model, he is pro-precision, and the distinction is the strategically useful one: *"Let it remove drudgery, generate first passes, organise assets and automate anything that doesn't benefit from human judgment."* Third, the tool decisions show a designer's priorities rather than an engineer's: the mobile version should not be a shrunken desktop, so visibility is set per breakpoint; art is not uniform, so export settings are per block; and height control replaces the spacer-and-scaffolding habit that has wrecked more web layouts than any font choice.

**Pros:**
- **Kerning in a CMS is a genuinely rare capability** and the proof is that professional workarounds for its absence involved Illustrator round-trips.
- **Layer compositing of PSD and Krita files in the browser** — blur, halftone, blend — is the closest thing to an art director's actual method that a page builder has offered, and it is where collage and photomontage become possible on the web.
- **The per-breakpoint visibility and the blank canvas template** are the two features that make a deliberate composition possible rather than a stack of sections.
- **The pre-flight check concept** — verifying a page before publishing the way a print job is checked before plate — is the right mental model for a department that ships dates and room numbers.
- **$39, perpetual, unlimited sites** is the correct pricing shape for a department, and a live demo means you can evaluate before asking anyone for money.

**Cons:**
- **WordPress only, and WordPress-Gutenberg only.** The Unified Platform is React on a VPS; the residency marketing site may not be WordPress at all. As a tool it is close to useless to us.
- **It is the maker's own promotional article, on a trade site, with no independent review.** Every claim is his, the "testers" are unnamed friends, and the only screenshot evidence is of the tool's own UI.
- **The ChatGPT quote is not evidence:** asking a model whether it can match a competitor's feature set produces agreement, not a measurement.
- **Kerning is a line-level control on a flow medium.** You cannot kern a paragraph, and the place kerning matters is headlines — where the alternative (set it as art, or use a variable font's optical size and tracking) already exists and costs nothing.
- **A one-developer plugin in the WordPress ecosystem is a long-term dependency risk**, and Gutenberg's block API has a history of breaking third-party blocks.
- The piece never shows the thing that would prove it: one real page, built twice, with the craft version and the default version side by side at 100%.

**Should we...**
- 👀 **Watch it** — take the sentence, leave the plugin.
**Why:** The reason to read this is that it names the current moment correctly from the opposite direction to everyone else: while the industry ships agents that decide for you, this person shipped a tool that gives you more decisions to make and fewer excuses not to. That is a position the department should be able to state, because it is the honest answer to "why can't we just generate the flyer." The specific claim worth stealing is the **mobile clause** — *the mobile version shouldn't simply be a smaller copy of the desktop* — because the Unified Platform's schedule grid and the campaign frames both currently do exactly that, and the fix is a composition decision at 390px rather than a scale factor. The second claim worth stealing is **per-breakpoint visibility**: on a phone, the right answer is often fewer objects, not smaller ones, and that is a design choice a token system cannot make for you. And the kerning point, translated to our stack, is simply: **Inter has optical sizing and tracking axes; we set type at one tracking value everywhere.** A headline at 64px in a marketing frame should not carry body tracking. That is a free fix and it is the kind of three-pixel decision that separates a department's work from a template's.

### Figma MCP — design context is intent, not geometry
**Link:** https://dqindia.com/features/ai-code-generation-figma-aditya-muttur-reliability-12826836 · **published 21 November 2026** · Dataquest, interview with **Aditya Muttur**, software engineer at Figma and lead engineer on Figma MCP (previously seven years at Meta and Instagram)
**Type:** Idea (an engineer naming the actual failure mode)

**What it is:** A short interview about why design-to-code still does not work reliably, from the person who built the bridge. Figma's MCP server has spent more than a year being refined so that it retrieves and structures design information well, but the unresolved problem is not the retrieval: **"People's codebases can be different. Whatever they are importing, the code can look different. They render differently. So, how do you make sure that you are able to provide a certain baseline level of speed when you are working with MCP?"** The answer is a two-tier context: heuristics decide which elements in a selection need full detail and which can go as high-level context — *"if you want more details about these pieces, go ask the MCP this other question"* — and **Code Connect** maps design components to their real code components so the agent inherits house practice instead of inventing it.

**What's good about it:** **The most useful sentence is about what travels.** MCP carries *"not only visual properties such as colours, dimensions and corner radii, but also annotations and accessibility requirements that communicate the intent behind a design."* That is the precise distinction the design world keeps collapsing: geometry is what a screenshot has, intent is what a design file has and a screenshot does not. An agent given pixels can reproduce the arrangement; an agent given annotations and a11y requirements can make a decision about a state that was never drawn. Second: **the "heterogeneous codebases" problem explains the generic look better than any prompt critique** — the model is not choosing a bland layout, it is falling back to the distribution because it has no reliable house signal. Third, his own account of what changed in the job is unusually clear: *"I'm not bottlenecked by my own ability to write code, but rather I'm bottlenecked by my ability to think and my ability to solve specific problems"* — the same shift the whole brief has been documenting, said without any hype at all.

**Pros:**
- **It identifies the mechanism rather than the symptom.** Agents write generic UI because design-to-code context is lossy, not because models lack taste — and a lossy channel can be improved.
- **Code Connect is a real, shipped countermeasure**, not an aspiration: design components mapped to the components that exist in code, so the agent reuses instead of recreating.
- **The two-tier context pattern — sparse metadata first, then zoom into the node you need — is the token-budget discipline** every MCP server will have to adopt, and it is worth knowing as a design principle for our own prompts.
- **Accessibility requirements travelling as context** is the detail with the most immediate application to a hospital product: a contrast floor in a design file is a build constraint, and a contrast floor in a screenshot is a comment nobody reads.
- It is a primary source — the engineer who built the thing — rather than a think piece about the thing.

**Cons:**
- **No published measurements.** Every claim about context quality and code quantity is qualitative, and no comparison of agent output with and without Code Connect exists in the piece.
- **It is a vendor talking about its own integration** on a trade site, with the interviewer doing very little pushback.
- **The unresolved problem is stated, not solved:** heterogeneous codebases still render differently, and no baseline guarantee is offered — which means the answer to "will this work in our repo" is still "try it."
- **The 25,000-token MCP response limit and the truncation behaviour that makes the two-step pattern necessary** are not discussed here at all, so a reader would not learn the practical constraint that shapes the workflow.
- **It covers design-to-code only.** The reverse direction — code captured back into Figma, which strips state, routing and event handlers on the DOM walk — is where the round-trip really breaks, and it is out of scope.

**Should we...**
- 👀 **Watch it** — and act on the one sentence that is already actionable.
**Why:** This is the missing half of the DESIGN.md argument. Yesterday's brief said the contract has to exist as a file; Muttur says what has to be *in* it: not just `Canvas #08090A / Body 14px`, but the annotations and accessibility requirements that explain why. Concretely, the Unified Platform's design system should carry, per component, the things a screenshot cannot: that the call-out button must remain reachable at 320px, that the schedule grid's muted text never drops below its contrast floor, that the empty state is a real state with its own hierarchy. Write those into the same file as the tokens and every future generation inherits them; leave them out and the agent will draw the happy path beautifully and invent the error state badly. The second reason to keep this on file is defensive: when someone asks why the AI-built version of a page looks like every other AI-built page, the answer is not the model, it is that we handed it pixels instead of intent.

## Slop Radar
- **The settled routine.** Ask any design agent today what it will do and it returns a numbered pass order — Unslop's nine (spacing, colour, icons, copy, type, layout, details, visuals, motion), StageShow's seven, DSG's three blocks. The method has become the tell. The output of a fixed pass order is a page where you can *see* the order: spacing that is right, then a colour that is right, then typography that is right, and no single decision that required all three at once.
- **Volume sold as choice.** Six drafts (Figma, yesterday), six directions (Floot, today), ten skills to install, five tools one brief. The number of options is now the product's headline feature, and it is the cheapest thing in the pipeline to produce. **Six options that do not differ in a stated way are one option printed six times** — and the review cost of six is six times the review cost of one.
- **Craft applied at the prompt layer only.** *"I didn't open Figma once… I art-directed AI. Spacing, type, hierarchy and imagery were all my calls"* (@matteodotsui, 17 Nov, one screen, posted for a verdict). The claim is true for a single frame and false for a series, and series is all a residency programme ever makes: six Grand Rounds cards, twelve social frames, one Sub-I packet per cycle. Taste at the prompt cannot hold a set together; only a rule can.
- **Contrast as a style.** Now that contrast is computable — TaishoDS pins every step to a ratio, Contra's rubric scores it — "accessible" is becoming a look: nothing allowed to recede, no quiet divider, no disabled state that reads as quiet rather than broken. A page where every element clears the floor has no hierarchy left. The discipline fixes the reading and, applied without judgement, flattens the composition.

## Overall Assessment
Four of today's seven finds are about the same object from four directions, and it is the object this brief has been circling for a fortnight: **the thing that sits between the intent and the pixels, and whether a human can inspect it.** Andy Clarke puts the oldest and best version in the room — the material comes from a conversation with a client, and the design is the trace of a detail someone mentioned in passing. TaishoDS puts the mathematical version: write the ratio each step must hit, then compute the colour, and publish the attempt that failed. Floot puts the process version: options side by side, one variable per frame, every frame labelled with a reason — *"it is easier to disagree with a reason than with a picture."* open-artifact puts the file version: plain local folders you can diff and comment on, with the comments routed back into the session. And Muttur puts the interface version: what has to travel is annotations and accessibility requirements, because geometry is a screenshot and intent is a design.

Read together, the message this week is narrower and more useful than "use better prompts": **every one of these mechanisms assumes somebody has already decided what the thing is, and none of them can supply that decision.** Clarke's client conversation, TaishoDS's contrast targets, Floot's state list, open-artifact's design system, Figma's annotations — all of them are downstream of a judgement that has to be made by a person with the brief in front of them. That is why the volume features keep failing: they multiply the output of a decision instead of improving the decision.

So the highest-leverage move this week is not a tool, and it is not another design system document. It is to write **the one page that precedes both** — the two or three sentences answering Clarke's question (what is it about this department that nobody else could say) and the four or five constraints answering TaishoDS's (what must the muted text, the divider and the disabled label each clear, on which background). One page, two halves: the idea and the contract. Every prompt we write from Monday reads it first; every asset gets judged against it; and the next time somebody proposes generating six directions, the six have to differ in something the page already named. Then run the review ritual that Floot's docs describe better than any agency memo: **three directions maximum, each varying one thing, each carrying its reason, judged side by side at the size it will actually be seen, with the real type on.** That costs nothing, needs no vendor, and is the difference between a department that uses these tools and a department that is used by them.
