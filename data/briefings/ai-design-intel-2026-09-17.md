### The Dark Arts of Skill Engineering
**Link:** https://x.com/pbakaus/article/2077114326987525 (mirror: https://paulbakaus.com/impeccable-by-design)
**Type:** Technique
**What it is:** Paul Bakaus's follow-up to Impeccable — a nine-technique guide to writing agent skills that actually change model behaviour instead of hoping a well-worded prompt holds. The core reframe: a skill is not a saved prompt, it is a *harness extension*. The nine techniques are make them argue, force divergence, route like a frontier model, give them memory, scripts that talk back, hooks that fight back, live-wire the browser, compile to every harness, and design for the weakest model. Impeccable itself is now at 68,635 stars, Apache-2.0, and was pushed again on Sept 16.
**What's good about it:** This is the design-director-grade answer to the problem that every "AI design" conversation skips: prompts are probabilistic, so any rule you write in prose is a suggestion, and suggestions lose to model defaults. Each of the nine techniques moves a guideline out of prose and into machinery. "Make them argue" is the most valuable entry — Impeccable's `/critique` spawns two sub-agents that never see each other's work (one LLM design director, one deterministic detector plus browser evidence) and synthesizes the results, precisely because handing the detector output to the reviewer biased it both ways. "Force divergence" explains the migration we tracked yesterday: banning purple gradients didn't restore taste, it "moved the model from one region of latent space into another," which is exactly how we got beige.
**Pros:**
- Nine named, separable techniques with concrete implementation notes — you can steal one without adopting the whole system
- "Force divergence" offers three stackable de-convergence methods (shave the top 3 safe picks, generate ~50 and have a blind reviewer keep the 5 most distinct, seed a palette outside the model's preferred region) — directly usable for our typography and colour decisions
- "Design for the weakest model" states the rule we keep learning: if a gate in your skill can be skipped, it will be, so make gates un-skippable rather than better-worded
- "Give them memory" describes snapshot-based state (a critique writes a scored snapshot; polish reads it as its fix backlog) with a file-path-derived slug so a teammate inheriting the same file inherits the same history
- Everything is open source and this version already compiles per-harness — the Claude build carries Claude-specific slop rules, the Gemini build kills image-on-hover motion
**Cons:**
- The techniques are about harness engineering, not design judgement — they make a model obey your rules, they do not tell you which rules are right. Prose still matters (Bakaus runs ablation evals on almost every line), and he says so
- Sub-agent spawning is not portable: on Codex a skill cannot spawn one unless the user has enabled parallel work, which makes the strongest technique the least reliable one
- It is written by a vendor with a funded company behind it (Renaissance Geek, a16z-backed, now partnered with GitHub) and the article doubles as an adoption argument for Impeccable
- Nine techniques is a real engineering project; none of this is a five-minute change to how we prompt
**Should we...**
- ✅ **Adopt it** — use it now; it raises our design ceiling
**Why:** For Shareef the value here is not Impeccable — it is the pattern library. Every quality standard we currently hold in prose ("no purple gradients," "single accent," "SVG icons not emoji") lives in a prompt and therefore loses to model defaults the moment we generate at volume. The three techniques that map onto our actual workflow are "force divergence" for typography and palette decisions on campaign and Sub-I creative, "make them argue" for the review stage of any generated page, and "design for the weakest model" as the rule that stops a QA gate from being quietly skipped on a cheap-model run. Note the self-implicating detail: Bakaus's own explanation of *why* the purple-gradient era became the beige era is the same diagnosis we filed yesterday. Combined with "Signs of AI design" below, this closes the loop — one document tells us what our output should never look like, this one tells us how to make the agent physically unable to drift there.

### Signs of AI design (field guide + `design-rules.md`)
**Link:** https://github.com/febbhav/signs-of-ai-design
**Type:** Tool / Idea
**What it is:** A field guide to the visual tells of AI-generated design — websites, app interfaces, images, video, logos, slide decks, social graphics, illustration, print — modelled explicitly on Wikipedia's "Signs of AI writing." Each entry lists what to look for, *why* models produce it, and its false positives, and carries two labels: reliability (how much one match tells you) and status (whether the sign still applies to current tools, because models patch their tells). The same content also ships as `design-rules.md`, written to be dropped into a project as `CLAUDE.md`, `.cursorrules`, or `AGENTS.md`.
**What's good about it:** The intellectual discipline is what makes this usable rather than a snark list. Three rules of interpretation head the document, and the second is the one that matters most: *judge the absence of decisions, not the presence of a style* — "the tell is not that Inter is a bad typeface. The tell is Inter, unchosen, next to twenty other unchosen defaults." That is a critique standard a design director can actually apply to our own work without becoming a font snob. It also names the second-wave palette migration and gives it a status label ("cream and beige 'tasteful' palette — moderate, active, rising"), records that the emerald fallback appears when a prompt bans purple, and traces the causal chain honestly: Tailwind's 2019 demo accent → tutorial saturation → training data → models learned modern web equals indigo → generated output re-enters training corpora and compounds the bias.
**Pros:**
- Two-way document: a detector for reviewing work and a rules file that tells a coding agent what to design against — the same artifact serves both the QA gate and the generation prompt
- Reliability and status labels are honest about decay, with an explicit "signs that no longer work" section (hands and garbled headline text are largely fixed in frontier models; human detection of AI images is now ~62%, barely above chance)
- Covers surfaces our other sources ignore — logos and branding, slide decks, print, and copy embedded in design — not just web UI
- The typography section is unusually precise for this genre: Inter unchosen, Geist as v0's default, Space Grotesk as the "escape" that became its own tell, Instrument Serif as the newest reflex
- CC BY-SA 4.0 — free to use, adapt, and build tooling on internally
**Cons:**
- **Not a fresh find** — the repository was created July 22, 2026 and last pushed July 31. What is current is the ecosystem around it: the Smithsonian's Sept 11 coverage of decoy typography and the broader slop-guide wave (impeccable.style/slop, solodesign.cc, 925 Studios) that this guide consolidates. Treat the guide as reference material, not this week's news
- 21 stars, single maintainer, and the content is prose-heavy — there is no CLI, no scoring, no integration, unlike impeccable's detector
- Its own false-positive discipline cuts against over-reliance: every entry is defensible in isolation, so it is only diagnostic in combination, which means it cannot be automated into a pass/fail gate without writing the stacking logic yourself
- The `design-rules.md` phrasing ("prefer a real decision over the reflex") is deliberately non-prohibitive, which means an agent can rationalize around it — the same weakness Bakaus's "design for the weakest model" section warns about
**Should we...**
- ✅ **Adopt it** — use it now; it raises our design ceiling
**Why:** This is the artifact that turns the last two weeks of converging intel into one document. We have separately captured Impeccable's anti-pattern list, the "Claude beige" tells, and the framework-default explanation; this guide collects those plus the categories we had not covered (logo and brand marks, decks, print, embedded copy) and adds the two things a checklist needs to survive contact with real work: a *false-positive* field and a *status* field. For the Urology Unified Platform the practical audit is narrow and useful — the guide flags dark-hero-with-glow as "weak alone" because it is also Linear's and Raycast's deliberately-built house style. Our platform is premium dark with a single accent, so the question the guide forces is not "is dark slop?" but "is our dark a decision with a system behind it?" That is a better question than any we have been asking. For the social programme and Sub-I materials, the value is the rising-status entries: cream grounds, oversized italic serif heroes, emerald fallback. Pull `design-rules.md` into the design flow as a constraint and keep the guide for review — but note the honest caveat in the cons, that it is July material wearing a current news cycle.

### Stefan Sagmeister: "design should be beautiful again"
**Link:** https://koreaherald.com/article/10875909
**Type:** Idea
**What it is:** A Korea Herald email interview with Stefan Sagmeister ahead of the Herald Design Lounge 2026 (theme "Beyond Visuals," Oct 2, Dongdaemun Design Plaza, opening keynote "Finally, Something Good"). Sagmeister argues that solving a problem is nothing to brag about — any competent professional can produce a readable poster in an afternoon — and that making something beautiful is where the actual work starts. On AI he is asked directly whether models deepen design's sameness or could break it.
**What's good about it:** The answer is the sharpest single sentence any designer has published on AI taste this week: *"By default it deepens it, because a model trained on everything returns the average of everything. It can also break it, but only for somebody who arrives with a strong opinion, so the gap between designers who have one and designers who do not is about to become very visible."* That is the same conclusion the Bakaus article and the signs-of-AI-design guide reach from completely different directions — tooling and taxonomy respectively — landing on the human's opinion as the load-bearing element. It also carries a genuinely unfashionable claim that is useful precisely because it is unfashionable: that "templated" is the disease, not "practical," and that the pursuit of beauty is a professional standard rather than indulgence. His infographic remark is a clean articulation of why designed documents outlast generated ones: an infographic asks to be believed and then forgotten, while a painting kept 150 years gets hung in a living room and looked at again for decades.
**Pros:**
- Independent third-party confirmation of the convergence thesis, from a practitioner with no tool to sell and no roadmap to defend
- Names the mechanism ("returns the average of everything") and the countermeasure (a strong opinion) in one breath — a usable standard, not a lament
- Reframes "beautiful" as a professional obligation, which is the exact register our design work has to hold against a department that will otherwise accept competence as sufficient
- The infographic-versus-painting distinction is directly applicable to our generated documents — it explains why the reimbursement sheet and Sub-I packet should be designed rather than templated
- Free, short, and quotable — usable as framing language in a design brief or a review
**Cons:**
- It is an interview ahead of a conference — no artifact, no method, nothing to install. The value is framing, not capability
- "Make it beautiful" is unfalsifiable as stated and is exactly the instruction a model will satisfy with the median of beautiful things, which is the trap the same interview warns about — Sagmeister does not resolve that tension
- Beauty-first is a contested position in a healthcare and academic context, where clarity, accessibility, and compliance outrank expression for some of our surfaces
- The practical question — how do you tell whether a designer has a strong opinion before the work is done — is left open
**Should we...**
- ✅ **Adopt it** — use it now; it raises our design ceiling
**Why:** This is the brief's thesis statement, and it lands on Shareef specifically because he is the one with the opinion. The platform has a committed position — navy #003da5 and never teal, single accent, real photography, light #f5f5f0 campaign grounds — and every one of those is a decision rather than a default, which is exactly what Sagmeister says is about to become the visible dividing line. The actionable use is rhetorical and cheap: put the line in the design flow as the standard against which generated work is judged. It also supplies the answer to a question that will come up in a department building a social programme on AI tooling — why keep a human art director in the loop when the model is fast — and it is a better answer than "quality," because it names the mechanism. Pair it with the two items above: the guide says what the average looks like, the dark arts say how to make the agent unable to produce it, and this says why a human with a position has to be steering.

### Remotion Motion Graphics agent skill
**Link:** https://github.com/Boriwatopal/agent-skill-remotion-motion-graphics
**Type:** Tool / Workflow
**What it is:** An MIT-licensed Claude agent skill for building kinetic-typography, logo and brand-promo motion graphics *as code* with Remotion (React), shipping an MP4, and producing both 9:16 and 16:9 from one codebase. It covers reading a reference clip and a brand's SVG logo, a scene-per-beat project structure with reusable animation helpers, a verify-by-contact-sheet loop, and Remotion Studio for tweaks. Built while cloning a reference promo for hundredable.com; the workflow is general, the video is not included.
**What's good about it:** The verify-by-contact-sheet loop is the premium signal. The workflow is `tsc` → 0.3-scale render → *look at every contact sheet* → still-render suspicious frames → fix → final render → pull a frame from the final file as proof. That is an editor's discipline, and it is the same closed-loop principle we valued in the pdf-design skill: the agent has to look at rendered output before handing anything over. The starter code is genuinely well-considered for our purposes — `layout.ts` derives every position from `useVideoConfig()` so both aspect ratios work from one source, `theme.template.ts` holds brand colours and named scene durations, `SvgArt.tsx` animates SVG artwork per path so letters can pop individually, and `Cursors.tsx` supplies arrow and dot cursors that spring between targets. Sixteen named motion patterns (phrase swap, pill → button → click, circle wipe, ticker plus panel wipe, route drawing, spotlight, burst → lockup) are documented with the timings that actually worked.
**Pros:**
- Motion design as versioned code — an MP4 is a build artifact, so a revision is a diff, not a re-export from a proprietary project file
- Both aspect ratios from one codebase, which matters for a social programme that needs 9:16 and 16:9 from the same asset
- Real gotchas captured from practice (CSS applies `filter` before `clip-path`, so blur the parent and clip the child or a soft spotlight gets razor edges; `url(#gradient)` fills render black without their `<defs>`) — these are the details that separate a workflow doc from a tool list
- The 13 official Remotion rule files are vendored in unchanged and credited to the Remotion team
- Node 18+, ffmpeg, and no proprietary tooling — it runs in our existing environment
**Cons:**
- Very new and essentially unvetted — 16 stars, single-author repo created September 13, 2026, one commit, no license file issues but no community either
- Requires Remotion 4, a Chrome download on first render, and comfort with React; this is a build pipeline, not a design tool
- Distribution is awkward — installation is a manual `git clone` into `~/.claude/skills/`, and the packaged `.skill` file targets claude.ai, so our Hermes path needs a port
- The skill is explicitly an adaptation of one reference video's motion language; the patterns are good but the aesthetic is derived, which is the same "arrive with an opinion" problem Sagmeister names
**Should we...**
- 👀 **Watch it** — promising, not proven yet
**Why:** The residency programme's social and marketing creative needs motion — bumpers, kinetic type for faculty clip intros, animated titles from the 28-clip library — and "animate it as code with a verification loop" is the right shape for how we work, because it makes motion design reviewable and repeatable rather than a hand-off to a proprietary timeline. The reason this is Watch and not Adopt is the same reason we have repeatedly chosen reuse over adoption: a one-commit, 16-star repo is a pattern to borrow, not a dependency to install. Borrow the three things that are genuinely good — the scene-per-beat structure with named durations in one theme file, the `useLayout()` approach that makes 9:16 and 16:9 fall out of one source, and the contact-sheet verification gate — and implement them against Remotion directly if and when motion work becomes a real deliverable. The `SvgArt.tsx` per-path animation approach is also worth studying for the platform's SVG icon set, though icons are static today. Revisit if it accrues contributors or the Remotion team's own skills package picks up the same workflow.

### ShieldFont / decoy typography
**Link:** https://www.smithsonianmag.com/smart-news/designers-and-computer-scientists-create-new-decoy-fonts-that-humans-can-read-and-ai-bots-cannot-180989470/ (project: https://shieldfont.org/press)
**Type:** Idea / Tool
**What it is:** Copenhagen type foundry Playtype and creative studio Seneda & Abrucio (S&A) released ShieldFont — an open-source web font that renders normally to human readers while swapping roughly half of all content words (nouns, verbs, adjectives, adverbs) in the underlying source code, so what scrapers collect is coherent but wrong. It exploits OpenType ligature and glyph substitution: the font tells the browser to display one set of characters while the HTML contains different words. Testing reported 55.8% of shielded passages no longer made the same factual claim as the original, and over 90% of ShieldFont pages are rejected by automated quality filters. Ships with an online encoder, a React component, CSS/CDN integration, and a custom font builder. Separately, the Smithsonian piece covers MixFont's Decoy Font (two text layers — clear outline text over blurred dark text, using hybrid imaging) and its earlier Ghost Font.
**What's good about it:** This is a typographic idea rather than a font swap, and that is rare in a week of tooling announcements. The intellectual move is elegant: instead of blocking scrapers at the door — robots.txt, which the founders note was being "routinely bypassed" — it lets them in and makes the material worthless. The mechanism is legitimately inventive, exploiting a font-technology feature (GSUB) from the inside rather than adding a layer on top. The type-design craft is real, too: it is built on Playtype's Optik, a proper retail typeface, not a novelty display font — which means the human-facing typography is professional, not a stunt. And the framing is precise and defensible: "not an anti-AI project — we're simply against the idea that publishing is the same as consenting."
**Pros:**
- Genuine typographic craft behind it — Playtype is an established Copenhagen foundry and ShieldFont is built on the real Optik family, so the human-facing design is not compromised
- Ships with practical integration paths (encoder, React component, CSS/CDN, custom font builder with a private mapping)
- Six weights of the protected Optik web font are downloadable from the press kit for evaluation
- The white paper is titled "The Consent Layer," which frames the problem as a design and ethics question rather than a security one — useful language for a department publishing clinical and academic content
- The Smithsonian coverage documents the broader category (MixFont's Decoy Font, Ghost Font), so the idea has independent designers working the problem
**Cons:**
- **The SEO cost is disqualifying for most of our surfaces.** Search engines index the decoy text, and you cannot distinguish Googlebot from an AI scraper — the same bytes go to both, so content you want to rank must not be wrapped. Fast Company's report that 90%+ of shielded pages get rejected by quality filters cuts both ways: it means our own search presence is at risk on any page we protect
- It is a defence against mass scraping, not a licence, and the legal question (whether publisher consent is required for training) is unsettled — the US Supreme Court declined to hear Thaler v Perlmutter in March 2026, leaving purely AI-generated output unprotectable, but that does not resolve scraping
- Protecting 24–56% of words means a real percentage of the assembled text is wrong; for anything patient-facing or clinical that is not a trade-off we can make
- Adoption is early and the countermeasure is adversarial by nature — scrapers can be updated to re-render pages through a browser, which defeats it entirely
**Should we...**
- 👀 **Watch it** — promising, not proven yet
**Why:** Every one of Shareef's design surfaces is published — the Unified Platform, the residency programme's marketing site, the Sub-I materials, the campaign creative — and the 28-clip faculty content library is exactly the kind of material that gets harvested without anyone asking. So the problem ShieldFont addresses is real and close to home. The reason it stays at Watch is that its cost lands squarely on the thing we most need: discoverability, from search engines we cannot separate from AI crawlers. A residency programme living on prospective-applicant search traffic cannot afford to serve decoy text to Googlebot. The pattern is worth holding anyway, because the idea generalises — the insight is that publishing should not be treated as consent, and that a design artifact (a typeface) can be the enforcement mechanism. Track whether a variant appears that distinguishes crawler types, or whether search engines publish a supported signalling method that lets publishers differentiate. Until then the honest verdict is that the craft is admirable, the ethics are clear, and the SEO trade is one we would not take on a public-facing surface.

### Smartdesign / "Design authority"
**Link:** https://foragents.pages.dev/ (HN submission Sept 16, 2026 18:51 UTC)
**Type:** Idea
**What it is:** A short design-language manifesto for humans and agents building products together, framed as "the shared standard." It states the problem (AI can produce a polished interface before it understands the person using it; without an authority to guide decisions, speed turns into sameness and decoration substitutes for hierarchy), then gives six principles: intent first, minimal chrome, visual restraint, accessible by default, use what exists, errors stay visible. Section four reframes states as design ("feedback is not decoration"). It closes with a five-question final check.
**What's good about it:** The problem statement is unusually well-aimed for a one-page manifesto. "Chrome as a substitute for hierarchy" is a precise name for a failure mode we see constantly in generated UI — cards, effects and labels competing for attention because nothing established what matters first. Same for the paired contrast: a technically complete interface can still make the important thing harder to find. The final check is genuinely usable as a pre-ship gate, and two of its five questions are better than anything on a standard design checklist — "can every interaction be completed with a keyboard?" and "can anything visible be removed without reducing usability?" The states-as-design section is correct and rare: loading, empty, error, success and disabled are design problems, not afterthoughts, and the guide's insistence that a failed action must never render as an empty state or a successful-looking result is a real accessibility and trust standard rather than a stylistic preference.
**Pros:**
- Six principles stated briefly enough to paste into a prompt or a review template, and specific rather than aspirational
- "Errors stay visible — never turn a failed action into an empty state or a successful-looking result" names a specific, checkable failure mode that generated UI hits constantly
- The final-check questions are keyboard-accessibility- and removal-based, which is how a design director reviews rather than how a checklist ticks
- Free, single page, copy-to-clipboard available — trivially brought into our flow with no install
- Written for agents explicitly, so the register is already the one a model will be given
**Cons:**
- It is a manifesto page, not a system — no tokens, no components, no detector, no code, nothing verifiable. Adopting it changes nothing except the words in a prompt, which is precisely the weakness Bakaus's article identifies
- No named author, no version, no changelog, no repo — provenance is unverifiable and it could vanish
- Overlaps heavily with standards we already hold (accessibility, restraint, reuse existing components) so most of the marginal value is in phrasing rather than content
- "Minimal chrome" and "visual restraint" are the same instruction a model will fulfil by producing generic minimalism — the guide does not distinguish restraint as a decision from restraint as a default, which is the distinction that actually matters
**Should we...**
- 👀 **Watch it** — promising, not proven yet
**Why:** The platform work already lives by most of this, so the honest read is that Smartdesign is a good statement of standards we hold rather than a new capability. Its one genuinely additive contribution is the final-check list, and specifically the removal question — "can anything visible be removed without reducing usability?" — which is a sharper review instrument than "is it clean," and which our card-based grids and icon system would benefit from being tested against. The second useful piece is the errors-stay-visible principle: as the platform accumulates surfaces (on-call, shifts, reimbursement, evaluations, notifications), the failure mode that will do the most damage is an action that fails silently and renders as something that looks fine. Worth adopting as review questions; not worth treating as a system, and not something to install. If Shareef wants the principles at the platform's quality gate, file the five questions verbatim rather than the whole page.

### Creative Web Intelligence Library v6
**Link:** https://github.com/r0ine/creative-web-intelligence
**Type:** Tool
**What it is:** A rule-and-recipe library for AI coding agents working on creative web projects, currently at v6. v1.1 shipped 403 reusable rules across typography (45), colour (102), layout (40), motion (52), 3D/WebGL (92), interaction (30) and anti-generic/anti-AI-slop (42), plus 59 interaction/layout recipes, 60 machine-readable palette recipes with measured contrast metadata, and an OKLCH colour workflow. v2 added 120 font recipes, 90 composition recipes, 80 motion recipes, 50 3D art-direction recipes and a compatibility graph. v2.2 added 100 scroll-choreography recipes and a Scroll Director. v3.2 added Wada colour intelligence, typography hardening, motion grammar and visual-fingerprint QA. v5 made its anti-AI rules blockers rather than advice. v6 extends to all visual media (3D, video, frame sequences, generated assets) and teaches when *not* to use them.
**What's good about it:** The scale and structure are real, and a few subsystems are more sophisticated than anything else in this brief. Wada colour intelligence is a legitimate art-historical framework rather than a swatch library. The colour module is explicitly designed as "a teaching system, not a swatch folder," with harmony models, semantic roles, area/saturation/lightness hierarchy, and OKLCH workflow. The anti-slop rules being upgraded from advice to *blockers* in v5 is the correct response to the problem Bakaus identifies. The open-source ecosystem registry with a LICENSE_MATRIX and an explicit rule that overlapping frameworks must not be combined without a written reason shows systems thinking about the agent's toolchain, not just about aesthetics. The honesty in the limitation note is also unusual: it states plainly that no system can claim to have "watched all of YouTube and Instagram," that Instagram blocks automated playback, and that the library uses a research pass plus an explicit capture queue instead — with VERIFIED / INDEXED / SYNTHESIZED / NEEDS_CAPTURE labels on its sources.
**Pros:**
- 403 named rules with category counts and machine-readable JSON, plus a schema for adding recipes — this is structured enough to select from rather than read wholesale
- 60 palette recipes ship with measured contrast metadata, which makes colour choice checkable against accessibility rather than eyeballed
- Provenance labels (VERIFIED / INDEXED / SYNTHESIZED / NEEDS_CAPTURE) are more honest than most design references, and the methodology is documented
- v5's shift from advice to blockers directly answers the "if the gate can be skipped, it will be" problem
- Scroll is treated as narrative and spatial progress rather than a universal fade-up trigger — a genuinely uncommon distinction
**Cons:**
- **Three stars, created September 13, 2026, no license.** This is a one-person research dump with essentially no adoption and no stated terms for commercial use — which matters for department work
- The scope is the problem: 403 rules plus recipes plus directors plus integrations is far more than any review can use, and there is no evidence anything has been tested against real projects
- Its core claim — a rules library stops agents building generic sites — is unverified, and the library itself contains no evaluation of its own effectiveness
- The "no honest system can claim to have watched all of YouTube and Instagram" caveat tells you the research base is a synthesis of secondary sources, not first-hand design analysis
- Heavy overlap with the tighter, better-adopted options in this brief (Impeccable's 44 anti-pattern rules, signs-of-AI-design's typography section) at a fraction of the review cost
**Should we...**
- 👀 **Watch it** — promising, not proven yet
**Why:** The typography and motion sections are worth a look specifically because those are the two areas where our own work has the least written standard — we have a committed palette and a committed font choice, but no documented rules for type scale, spacing rhythm or motion grammar, which is exactly what the library claims to supply (45 typography rules; a motion grammar in v3.2). The Wada colour framework is also the kind of thing worth reading once, since it gives a principled vocabulary for the "several muted colours at equal visual weight, no dominant accent" problem the slop guide flags. But three stars, no licence, and a five-day-old synthesis are disqualifying for adoption, and the honest comparison is that Impeccable's 44 anti-pattern rules and signs-of-AI-design's typography section cover most of the same ground at a fraction of the review cost and with real adoption behind them. Revisit only if it gains stars and a licence; the concept of a blockers-not-advice rules file is already better served by what we have.

## Slop Radar
- **The migration itself is the tell.** Purple gradients were banned, so output moved to cream grounds and italic serif heroes. Bakaus's line is the one to remember: bans "move the model from one region of latent space into another." Every time we add a prohibition, we should ask what the model will do *instead* — and constrain that too.
- **Emerald fallback.** When a prompt bans purple, models cascade to emerald (#10B981) as the next safe accent, sometimes overriding explicit written guidance against green. Worth adding to the QA gate on the campaign creative, which already carries navy and a light ground.
- **Cream and beige "tasteful" grounds — rising status.** The second-wave default that replaced purple. Our campaign work sits on #f5f5f0, which is a deliberate, documented choice tied to real photography — the distinction that matters is that ours is defined, not inherited from the anti-purple reaction.
- **Inter unchosen.** The tell is not the typeface, it is the typeface beside twenty other unchosen defaults. The platform legitimately is Inter, so the differentiator has to come from hierarchy, spacing, and decisive use of the accent — which means every surface needs a visible decision the default would not have made.
- **Rules that are advice rather than machinery.** A prohibition in a prompt is a suggestion. If a quality gate in our flow can be skipped by a weak model, it will be — make gates un-skippable or accept that they are decorative.

## Overall Assessment
Three sources this week reached the identical conclusion from completely different directions, and that convergence is the actual finding. Bakaus explains it mechanically (a model returns the median of its training data; bans relocate the median rather than escaping it). The signs-of-AI-design guide explains it diagnostically (judge the absence of decisions, not the presence of a style). Sagmeister states it plainly ("by default it deepens it... it can only break it for somebody who arrives with a strong opinion"). The gap between designers who have an opinion and those who do not is the thing that is about to become visible — and the practical corollary is that tooling can only enforce a decision someone else made. This is the note under everything in this brief: none of these seven finds generates a point of view, and the good ones are all machinery for holding one you already have. Shareef's highest-leverage move this week is therefore not a tool — it is to convert the convergence into one document. Take Impeccable's 44 anti-pattern rules, the cream/emerald/italic-serif tells from this week's guide, and the platform's own committed decisions (navy #003da5 never teal, single accent, real photography, #f5f5f0 campaign grounds, SVG icons, one documented correction about "Call Out") and write them into a single house standard with a false-positive note on each line. That document is the opinion; the tooling in this brief — Impeccable's routing and critique patterns, the blockers-not-advice upgrade, the contact-sheet verification gate — is what stops a weak model from quietly discarding it. Two weeks of intel have been pointing at this. The second move, if there is room, is to read the Bakaus article's "force divergence" section properly, because its three de-convergence methods are the only concrete technique anyone has published for generating options outside the model's preferred region — and that is the specific problem our social programme will hit first, at volume, in the next few months.
