### Contra Labs — Frontier Design Benchmark, and the model called Gestalt
**Link:** https://contralabs.com/research/frontier-design-benchmark · methodology https://contralabs.com/frontierdesign/methodology · rubric https://contralabs.com/frontierdesign/rubric · **live leaderboard** https://contralabs.com/frontierdesign/leaderboard (verified 200, updated Nov 2026) · published **18 November 2026**, covered through 21 Nov
**Type:** Idea / Tool (a benchmark, and the slop taxonomy that falls out of it)

**What it is:** The first design benchmark built from real briefs and judged by working designers rather than by whoever showed up. Contra Labs (with BenchFlow) took **33 tasks — 27 rewritten from real jobs posted on Contra, 6 from startup partners who supplied their own brand assets, files, product images and decks** — and ran 15 frontier models through each one in an agent sandbox, recording output, cost and full trajectory. Then they commissioned **human "golden" designs** for the same briefs from working designers, including three moodboard directions, feedback and a revision round. **35 professional designers cast 4,311 votes over five rounds**, names hidden, sides shuffled, converted to Elo with a Bradley-Terry model; split-half ranking agreement is Spearman 0.95 across 200 random splits. The headline: a **stealth model called Gestalt sits first at 1,931 Elo with a 90% win rate, above the human golden designs at 1,739 and Claude Fable 5.2 at 1,713.** Contra's co-founder says on Hacker News, flatly: *"it is not two models. Its a single model (Gestalt). The other bar on the benchmark is a human created dataset. Suffice to say we didn't expect anyone to beat it, but here we are. And no, it is not Contra model."*

**What's good about it:** Three instruments, and the third is the one to keep. **The rubric** is 35 dimensions across structure, visual craft, and content-and-use — type hierarchy, spacing, contrast, too many fonts, "whether everything renders whole" — validated by checking its critiques against what designers actually wrote, with decoys mixed in; it correlates with the Elo ranking at r = 0.94. **The Slop Detector** is a classifier trained on human-made and AI-made pages that outputs 0–100 and *names the features that drove the score*, reading a few hundred signals across 18 dimensions. And then the result that makes the whole thing worth reading: **the human golden designs score 73.6 on the rubric — sixth of sixteen, behind five models — because of execution slips a designer can point at, "a button label too dark to read against its background, or a wordmark cut off by the section above it." Designers saw those same slips and still chose the human work in 74% of votes (425 of 574).** That is the cleanest demonstration I have seen that *craft score ≠ taste*, and it is stated by the people who built the score, against their own instrument.

**Pros:**
- **The slop list is a checklist you can run this afternoon, with frequencies attached.** A small spaced-out uppercase label above a heading that repeats the heading: **93% of AI pages in the training set do it on at least half their sections, against 11% of human pages.** Numbered section indices (01, 02, 03): **17% of AI pages, and no human page at all** — not one in the training set, not one golden design. An italic word in the headline: Kimi K3.1 Pro 51%, GPT-6 Astra 43%, **zero human golden designs**. Everything in a bordered card: Gemini 4 Argon 97%, Gemini 4 Carbon 94%, Muse Spark 1.3 86%.
- **It separates a lab's fingerprint from a model's quality.** Claude puts a horizontal arrow (→) on links and buttons on 96% of Fable 5.2's pages against 46% elsewhere; GPT uses a diagonal arrow (↗) on 94% of GPT-6 Astra's pages against 12%; DeepSeek and Grok use no arrows at all on ~80% of pages. GPT-6.1 Sol writes headings as two- or three-phrase slogans on 55% of pages against 4%; Gemini 4 Argon dresses ordinary marketing sections as technical readouts — status labels, figure references — on 91% of pages against 35%; DeepSeek centres every section heading on 44% of pages against 1%. *This is how you tell which model made something, which is the first step to not sounding like any of them.*
- **The brief realism is the right correction to "build a landing page for a coffee shop."** Median real brief: **138 words, $1,050 budget, and 75% stack two or more constraints** such as brand assets, audience or business goal. Nineteen of 27 briefs supply the client's logo as the starting point; 17 require it to work on a phone.
- **It prices the difference.** Contra publishes mean cost per output on a log scale next to Elo — coverage reading that chart puts **Claude Fable 5.2 at $127.22 a design (a lower bound) against $1.31 for GPT-6.1 Sol, 45 Elo points behind.** A design director's real question is never "which is best" but "which is best per hour of my attention," and this is the first benchmark that even allows the question.
- **The negative findings are published.** The Slop Detector's two 1.2 readings come from opposite causes — DeepSeek V4 Pro because many of its pages are *unfinished*, Gestalt because its pages are finished and good. A benchmark that reports its own confound in the same paragraph is a benchmark you can use.

**Cons:**
- **Nobody has disclosed how many attempts Gestalt got.** The report describes the blind voting but leaves the maker unnamed and gives no comparable account of how a design went from brief to ballot. If Gestalt is best-of-forty and Fable is first-shot, the 218-point gap is measuring a studio's selection process, not a model. Kingy's question is the right one and it has no answer in the material.
- **Contra is not a neutral party.** They are a design marketplace with 2M creatives, they ran this with a partner (BenchFlow), and they are *"working with the team to continuously evaluate its quality and provide feedback"* — i.e. the top-ranked entrant is a commercial relationship they are managing. Every number here is theirs.
- **It benchmarks websites and web apps, not brand systems.** The 33 tasks are marketing sites, landing pages, one crowdfunding checkout, a partner portal, an audio tour and a voice notebook. Nothing here tests an identity, a poster series, a print system, or type at scale — the surfaces where Shareef's marketing work actually lives.
- **The two snapshots disagree and nobody explains it.** The research page says 1,928 and 378 of 423 votes (89%); the live leaderboard says 1,931 and 90% on 420 votes. Small, but a benchmark's credibility is its arithmetic.
- **The rubric rewards the absence of mistakes, which is exactly what it says it does** — and then the report treats a 0.94 correlation with preference as validation. It is a correlation between two things measured by the same panel in the same study.

**Should we...**
- ✅ **Adopt it** — the slop list as a review checklist, starting with the next thing we ship.
**Why:** This is the first artefact in a fortnight of scanning that gives the marketing programme a *test* rather than an opinion, and it is free. Every design review in the department can now begin with four questions that have base rates behind them: **does a small spaced capital label sit above the heading repeating it; are the sections numbered; is one word of the headline in italics; is every content group in a bordered card?** A yes to any of those is a tell, and a yes to all four is a page that a designer will clock in under a second and a patient will feel without being able to say why. Applied to the Unified Platform — a premium dark product with one accent and Inter — the exposes are sharper still, because the platform's design is *restraint*, and every one of those four patterns is a small piece of decoration standing in for hierarchy: an eyebrow label where a weight change would do, a "01" where a section break would do, a card where a rule would do. The second thing to take is the finding, not the leaderboard: **human work lost the rubric and won the vote.** That is the argument for spending the time on the *unmeasurable* half — the photograph that is actually of this faculty member, the sentence only this department could write — because the measurable half is now the cheap part, and it is about to be free. The third thing is the warning inside the Slop Detector's 1.2: a page can be finished, competent, mistake-free, and still arrive from the centroid. When Gestalt and an unfinished DeepSeek page score identically as "not AI-looking," the detector is telling you it measures distance from average, not presence of an idea.

### Siteprint — the design system, measured and handed to the agent
**Link:** https://siteprint.app · Safari extension https://siteprint.app/extension/ · MCP https://siteprint.app/mcp/ · App Store https://apps.apple.com/us/app/siteprint-design-to-ai-prompt/id6817107438 · surfaced **18 November 2026** (Dynamic Business)
**Type:** Tool (a measurement instrument that emits machine-readable design contracts)

**What it is:** A Safari extension for Mac, plus a web version, that reads the page in front of you and measures **what Safari is actually rendering**: the palette with each colour's *role* (canvas, surface, text, muted, accent, border) ranked by how much of the page it paints; the type scale with family, size, weight, line height and colour per level; spacing, corner radii, shadows; a section-by-section layout blueprint with grids and rows; components; the page's own animation CSS including hover, focus and press states; and the SVGs and images it uses. Then it writes that out as a **rebuild prompt** (compact, detailed or full), a **DESIGN.md**, **Tailwind v4** and **shadcn/ui** themes, CSS variables, JSON and Figma tokens — and with Pro, serves every saved scan to Claude Code, Cursor or Codex through a **local MCP server**, so the agent asks `get_design` instead of screenshotting your work and guessing.

**What's good about it:** It closes the loop this brief has been circling for two weeks. On 19 November the Knapsack talk established that a model asked to build against conflicting sources produces *"plausible but wrong"* output and will keep generating even after it names the conflict; the fix offered there was a contract — agree, stop, or refuse. Siteprint is the missing artifact that makes that contract possible, because it converts a rendered page into **the measurements themselves**, not a description of them. Second: **the measurement is the right unit of source-of-truth.** Every design argument in this department has been conducted in adjectives — "premium," "clean," "too busy" — and every argument ends the same way, with a screenshot and a shrug. A DESIGN.md that says `Canvas #08090A / Surface #101112 / Text #F7F8F8 / Muted #8A8F98 / Display 64px weight 510 / Body 14px weight 400 / Radii 8, 14, 16` ends the argument in one glance, and it is the same file an agent can read. Third: **Match** — point it at a saved scan and a build you made, and it opens both in real browser instances, diffs the visual properties, and emits a *fix prompt* naming the deviations. That is regression testing for design, which almost nobody has.

**Pros:**
- **Reads a page behind a login, in your own browser, with no server involved** — no account, no analytics, no tracking, on-device only. For a hospital platform, "the tool that measures our intranet has to run locally" is not a preference, it is the constraint, and this one is built around it.
- **Emits the formats agents actually consume**, rather than a PDF style guide nobody opens: Tailwind v4 theme, shadcn/ui theme, CSS variables, JSON, Figma tokens. One scan, every consumer.
- **The MCP endpoint is the difference between a nice export and a working pipeline** — the agent fetches the design instead of being pasted a screenshot it must interpret.
- **`Compose` (mix colour from one site, layout from another) is genuinely useful for reading references** as systems rather than as vibes, and for asking "what if our type were set at Linear's density but on our grid."
- **A real free tier** — unlimited inspection, token export and library, plus one prompt and one DESIGN.md to try — so the experiment costs nothing and the decision takes ten minutes.

**Cons:**
- **Mac and Safari only, macOS 14+.** The Unified Platform is built and reviewed on Linux and in a browser; this is a laptop tool for a Mac owner, and Shareef is not one.
- **The pricing pages contradict each other**, which is a trust tell for anything you would put in a workflow: the extension page says **Pro $19.99 once**, the homepage says **$49/year**, and the compare page says the web version is **$9 a month or $69 a year.** Three prices, three pages, no reconciliation.
- **A measured system is not a designed system.** It will faithfully extract a page whose hierarchy is wrong, whose type scale is arbitrary and whose colour roles are contradictions — and hand it to an agent as law. Garbage in, canon out.
- **`Compose` is a cloning machine.** Mixing four sites into "a new style direction" produces the visual equivalent of a cover band, and it is precisely the failure mode this brief exists to prevent: derivation without a referent, taste assembled from other people's decisions.
- **The DESIGN.md is only as good as the CSS it read**, and Tailwind-heavy sites with arbitrary values will export a scale that looks like noise (37px, 15px, 13px). It has no opinion about whether the measurements are any good, and it says so nowhere on the marketing pages.

**Should we...**
- ✅ **Adopt it** — but point it at *ours*, first, not at somebody else's.
**Why:** The highest-value use of this tool is not inspiration, it is **audit**: run it across the Unified Platform's live surfaces — dashboard, schedule, call-out, evaluations, notifications — and diff the five DESIGN.md files against each other. If the spacing rhythm, the radius set, the accent usage and the type scale do not match across those five pages, we have found the 43 disagreements Knapsack found, in our own house, in an afternoon, with an instrument instead of an argument. Then write the reconciled file into the repo and make it the first thing every generation prompt reads. The Chapter Player gets the same treatment: it is a polished light theme that has never had its rules written down, which means every future change is a coin flip. What to refuse is the temptation in `Compose` and in the "rebuild" prompt — the tool will let you make something that looks like Linear in four minutes, and it will be Linear's decisions wearing our logo, which is a worse outcome than the generic AI look because it is a *convincing* one. Use it to measure, not to borrow.

### Figma AI — six drafts, or three models, on one prompt
**Link:** https://www.figma.com/release-notes/ (release **18 November 2026**, "Generate multiple images from a single prompt") · help https://help.figma.com/hc/en-us/articles/24004542669463 · coverage https://designcompass.org/en/2026/11/20/figma-ai-image-model-comparison
**Type:** Tool / Workflow (a small feature that changes the unit of judgement)

**What it is:** Figma's image tools now do two different things where they used to do one. **One model, many options:** ask for 2, 4 or 6 images from a single prompt in *Make image* or *Edit image*. **Several models, one image each:** add up to three models to the same prompt with the plus button and get one interpretation from each, side by side on the canvas. Hovering a candidate previews it **inside the actual layer** it will fill; *Place all on canvas* lays every option out for comparison. It works across Figma Design, FigJam, Slides and Draw, is available on all plans with edit permission, and draws on shared AI credits at **2 credits per image on GPT Image 2.5 Sunburst, 6 on Nano Banana 2 Lite and 16 on Nano Banana 2.**

**What's good about it:** **The comparison is on the same prompt, which is the only fair comparison.** Almost every model opinion in this industry is formed by running two prompts in two tabs a week apart and remembering which one felt better; that is how model folklore gets made. Three models on one brief, one canvas, one credit table removes the folklore and leaves you with evidence about *this* brief. Second, and the part that matters more: **preview-in-place changes what you are judging.** A generated image alone is a picture; the same image inside the hero with your real headline over it is a design decision. The best write-up of the feature makes the point better than Figma did, and it is worth quoting as a pipeline: *frame first* — lay out the hero with final type and spacing and hide the text layer; *cheap exploration* — six options on the 2-credit model; *model check* — take the best direction and run it once across three models to see which handles the light and material you need; *refine on one model*; and **type back on — judge the image as part of the screen, not as a picture.**

**Pros:**
- **It turns model choice from a belief into a measurement**, per brief, at a known cost. Two credits versus sixteen is a real decision you can now make with your eyes.
- **Preview-in-place is the anti-slop feature**, quietly: an image that looks atmospheric in isolation and destroys the type hierarchy in place will fail immediately, which is exactly the judgement a designer makes and a prompt cannot.
- **Six variations from one model is the right way to explore a direction** — variation inside a model, not across models — and the feature keeps those two questions separate.
- Works in FigJam and Slides, which means the same comparison discipline reaches the deck and the workshop, not just the design file.
- No new subscription, no new tab, no export: the comparison happens where the artifact lives.

**Cons:**
- **Volume is not judgement, and six is a volume number.** The default failure of this feature will be designers generating six, picking the prettiest, and calling it art direction — the same slot machine, now with a contact sheet.
- **The cheap model becomes the default by economics**, and it is the cheap model that flattens light. A team that explores at 2 credits and ships at 2 credits has quietly chosen the weaker renderer for everything.
- **Credit costs are stated "as of October 15, 2026"** and can move; building a habit around one model's price is building on sand, and a six-image Nano Banana 2 run is 96 credits for one prompt.
- **No seed control and no reproducibility story** in the release notes — the thing that makes generated imagery usable in a system (same seed, one change, everything else holds) is absent, which is the same gap this brief has flagged in every image tool for a fortnight.
- Three models is a short list, and it is Figma's list; the comparison cannot tell you that a model you do not have access to would have done it better.

**Should we...**
- ✅ **Adopt it** — as a *review* habit, not a generation habit.
**Why:** The residency programme's image decisions are currently made one render at a time, by whoever is at the keyboard, which means the standard drifts with the person. A three-model comparison on one brief, run once per campaign before anything is approved, converts that drift into a documented choice: this brief, these three models, this one won, here is why. That is a sentence you can put in a file and defend in a year. The second use is the one that pays immediately — **preview-in-place on the Unified Platform's empty states, card backgrounds and hero panels**, because those are the places where a generated texture or illustration either supports the type or fights it, and the only way to know is to see it with the real Inter headline on top. The discipline to hold is the pipeline's last step: type back on, always. A generated image never gets approved as an image in this department; it gets approved as a background under a real headline, a real name and a real date.

### &Walsh × Insomnia Cookies — a photograph for a logo, and a two-tier campaign system
**Link:** https://creativeboom.com/news/walsh-takes-a-bite-out-of-the-moon-for-insomnia-cookies/ · published **19 November 2026** · Creative Boom · work by **&Walsh** (Jessica Walsh); art director Carolina Vazquez
**Type:** Work (an identity refresh, judged as a specimen)

**What it is:** A refresh, not a rebuild, for a 375-store late-night bakery that invented its own category and then watched everyone else enter it. The old identity had a cookie moon — a drawing. &Walsh kept the idea and **swapped the drawing for a photograph: a real melted chocolate-chunk cookie, half-eaten into a crescent**, with stars around it. Around that moon sits a system built on the hours the brand operates: a late-night purple, a run of cookie-dough browns, rounded chunky type by Julio Zukerman, illustration by Zukerman and Simran Meshram, photography by Henry Hargreaves with food styling by Charlotte Omnes and Daniella Khan, motion by Pedro Magalhães, Igor Fajardo and Marco Del Pra. The strategy is one promise — **"Always Warm"** — and the rollout runs across campaigns, digital, newsletters, print, packaging and the pizza-style boxes regulars know. The structural idea is a **tiered system: most launches share the same warm, appetising photography and art direction, while a select few campaigns get a more graphic approach.**

**What's good about it:** **The photographic logo is a real risk taken for a real reason.** A photograph of food is the only asset that can make a late-night delivery promise legible at a glance, and a drawing of a cookie cannot — so they took the awkward route, and then did the unglamorous professional thing: **kept a vector version in reserve for the sizes and print jobs where a photograph falls apart.** That is what craft looks like in practice — commit to the idea, then engineer around its failure modes instead of pretending they do not exist. Second: **the tiered system is the answer to a problem this brief named yesterday.** The 20 November item established the tile law — a set of twelve frames fails not because each is bad but because they are inconsistent with each other. &Walsh's response is to declare, in advance, that most campaign work will look like the same warm photography and a few pieces a year will be allowed to break into graphics. That is a governance rule, not a mood board, and it is the cheapest way to keep a set coherent while still letting a big moment shout. Third: the type and colour are *derived* from the promise — rounded and chunky because "warm," browns because dough — which is the derivation discipline the whole brief keeps insisting on, executed by a studio with the budget to do it properly.

**Pros:**
- **A refresh that keeps the equity it inherited.** The cookie moon survived; only its medium changed. Most agencies would have replaced it to have something of their own, and the case study is explicit that the strategy was to *recommit* rather than reinvent.
- **The two-tier rule is copyable tomorrow**, at any budget: state which tier a piece of work is in before it is designed, and let the tier decide how far it may depart.
- **Real production credits, named**, all the way down to food stylists and three motion designers — which means the identity was built to move, not just to sit on a grid.
- The purple is doing a job: it is the colour of 2am, and it is deliberately not the red-and-cream of the delivery category it competes with.
- It is a rare case of a *refreshed* identity being judged on whether it feels like the same brand, rather than whether it looks newer.

**Cons:**
- **The photographic logo is a permanent production tax** — every co-branded use, every small size, every embroidered cap and every single-colour print job now has an exception path, and exceptions are where brand systems rot.
- **Purple is the category's default late-night cue**, so the palette is differentiated from the bakery down the street and identical to the mood of every app that wants to feel nocturnal.
- **"Always Warm" is a promise every delivery brand makes**, and a promise is not a differentiation unless the work proves it — the case study shows the feeling, not the evidence that anyone noticed.
- **The tier system has no rule for which tier a campaign enters.** "A select few" is an editorial judgement made by whoever is in the room, which is exactly the kind of unstated rule that gets abused once the founders are busy.
- It is trade-press coverage supplied by the agency: no metrics, no before/after conversion, no failure discussed, and the correspondent's own aside about not being tempted by late-night snacks is doing more work than the analysis.

**Should we...**
- ✅ **Adopt it** — the two mechanisms, not the look.
**Why:** The residency programme runs the same problem at a smaller scale: a Grand Rounds series, a match-season campaign, a Sub-I welcome packet and a dozen social frames a month that all have to feel like one department without looking like one template. **Write the two tiers down.** Tier one is the standing work: real photographs of real faculty, navy, Inter, a fixed type position, no illustration. Tier two is the two or three moments a year that earn an exception — match day, the residency fair, a commencement — where a graphic device is allowed in. Announce the tier before the design starts and the argument about "can we try something different" becomes a scheduling question, which is answerable. The second mechanism is the photographic-logo lesson applied literally: **our best brand asset is a real photograph of a real person, and we should be willing to use it where a drawing would be easier.** The Chapter Player already does this; the marketing collateral mostly does not.

### Verve × Zero — "Make CRM history", derived from a fossilised typewriter
**Link:** https://www.designweek.co.uk/verve-casts-legacy-software-as-the-stone-age-in-rebrand-for-ai-startup-zero/ · published **20 November 2026** · Design Week · work by **Verve** (Amsterdam); CD Walewijn den Boer, strategy director Eveline Koppejan, strategist Michelle Arrazcaeta, illustrator Kevin Lebon; type by **Calame.Bureau** (Install)
**Type:** Work / Idea (an identity and the method that produced it)

**What it is:** A full brand — strategy, visual identity, digital design — for Zero, a Helsinki startup building AI-native CRM to replace Salesforce and HubSpot, funded with a $10.3m seed in September. The positioning is an **extinction event**: legacy platforms are a Stone Age landscape of cracked earth and fossilised tools; Zero is an alien technology so advanced it barely registers as software. The strapline is **"Make CRM history."** The identity runs on two colliding layers — a hand-illustrated Neolithic ground (drifting monoliths, fossilised tools, cave drawings of the frustrations of selling with old software, citing the opening of *2001* and the hovering craft in *Arrival*), with a crisp product layer superimposed to show the software solving those ancient problems. The secondary element is **Zorb**, a gleaming space object with a small face whose moods are rendered as force fields built from hand-drawn cave inscriptions — designed to eventually be the face of a chat-only interface. The signature yellow was kept because the founders named the company at a restaurant with yellow tables, and Verve notes no competitor in the category claims the hue.

**What's good about it:** **The origin story is the method, and it is exportable to any brief at any budget.** The concept came from a flight delay: the team went into a Helsinki museum, saw a **typewriter that had become a fossil**, and the whole identity followed — *"Extinct office technology – that was the story. The old tools are turning to stone, and Zero is bringing the new ones."* That is the single most useful sentence in today's brief, because it is a *procedure*: go somewhere physical, look at objects, and let the idea arrive from something you can hold. It is the opposite of a mood board and it produces work no model can generate, because the model was not in the museum. Second: **the two-layer structure is a real system, not a collage.** A primitive ground with a precise product plane on top is a rule that scales to a billboard, a website and a chat window without re-deciding anything. Third: **the colour decision has a reason attached and a competitive test applied** — the yellow survived because it came from the founding story *and* because nobody in the category owns it. That is how you argue a colour in a review.

**Pros:**
- **The derivation chain is complete and auditable:** museum object → extinct tools → stone landscape → brand layer that solves the old frustrations → strapline. Every element can be traced to the origin, which is the test this brief keeps applying.
- **A mascot designed for a job** — Zorb's moods are the future chat interface's emotional register, not a sticker, and the force-field idea ties the most futuristic asset back to the prehistoric ground.
- **Verve's new working model** — creative director and strategist jointly leading from pitch through account — is a structural claim, and the case study is honest that the museum visit was possible *because* both were in the room.
- The choice against a safe route is documented: they were shown an anti-brand rebellion and a lifestyle-tech route and picked the riskier one.
- Retained equity where it earned it (the yellow) and replaced the rest, which is the correct order of operations for a young brand.

**Cons:**
- **"Challenger frames the incumbent as prehistoric" is one of the oldest moves in B2B.** It was fresh when it was a metaphor; here it is a literal illustration of the metaphor, which makes it clearer and less surprising at the same time.
- **An illustration-led identity is expensive to extend and it drifts at small sizes** — cave drawings and monoliths do not survive a 16px favicon or a one-colour fax, and every new asset requires an illustrator rather than a template.
- **Yellow as a brand colour in a software product is a collision**: it is the universal warning and pending-state hue, so the product layer will constantly be asked to distinguish brand from alert, and the article does not say how.
- **Zorb is a bet that the AI product will want a cute face.** If the category moves toward invisible agents and quiet interfaces — and the best work is moving that way — the mascot becomes the most dated asset in the system.
- No metrics, no recognition research, agency-supplied quotes throughout, and the strategic claim ("the brand gives Zero the conviction to say that out loud") is not the same as evidence that the market heard it.

**Should we...**
- 👀 **Watch it** — take the method now, leave the aesthetic.
**Why:** Two things here are worth adopting this week and one is worth refusing. Adopt: **the museum procedure.** The next time the residency programme needs a campaign idea — match season, the Sub-I welcome, a commencement — the brief should start with an hour somewhere physical, looking at the objects of the discipline: a cystoscope, a resectoscope, a ureteral stent, the urodynamics chair, a 1970s operative note, the actual instruments. The idea that arrives from holding something real will be more specific than any prompt, and it will survive the question "why this?" because the answer is a room you can describe. Adopt too: **the hue test.** Before any new campaign colour is chosen, ask what hue nobody in academic medicine claims — the honest answer is that the entire category is navy and blue, which means Montefiore's navy is a *category* colour and differentiation has to come from type, photography and composition rather than hue. That is a strategically useful, slightly uncomfortable conclusion, and it is worth writing into the brand document. Refuse: the illustrated-extinction aesthetic. It is well made, it is coherent, and it is a costume — the same objection this brief raised to BrandOpus × Hg on 19 November, arrived at from the opposite direction.

### Learning Design Taste — the model got better and less distinctive
**Link:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.20612 · **submitted 16 November 2026** · Sujay Choubey, Siddhant Saxena, Sarthak Kapil, Suhas Motwani
**Type:** Idea (a research result with a negative finding worth more than its positive one)

**What it is:** A paper asking whether a small code model can *learn* design taste rather than be prompted toward it. Code-generating models produce interfaces that build and run but are *"often weak in hierarchy, spacing, typography and composition."* The method has three parts: a **layered verification framework** (hard gates, render-grounded metrics, rubric judges, within-group relative preference, a blinded expert panel), with a signal admitted to the reward only if it passes an **admission protocol** testing agreement with experts, causal sensitivity, resistance to "hack traps," and stability — which admits **7 of 16 render signals and 6 of 10 rubric dimensions**; **synthetic design environments** built as parameterised generators with measured difficulty ladders, where base pages are written once and instances produced by syntax-tree perturbation and constraint sampling under an adaptive curriculum with a quarantine rule against reward over-optimisation; and **agentic RL** on a **4B code model** over multi-turn episodes where it edits and builds a repository and reads a *textual report of its own render*.

**What's good about it:** The **admission protocol** is the honest part, and it is the part every "design rubric" product skips: before a metric is allowed to shape the reward, you test whether it agrees with experts, whether it responds to real causal changes, and whether it can be gamed. Admitting 7 of 16 signals is an admission that half of what you can measure about a page is not worth optimising. Second: **the results are stated with their own failure.** Taste Index rises **0.630 → 0.795** in-family and **0.598 → 0.700** on held-out families; it wins **0.600** of blinded expert comparisons against the base model and **0.569** against a single-turn control. And then: *"Most of the advantage depends on the inference-time loop"* — i.e. the model did not learn taste so much as learn to use the render-read-edit loop, which is a workflow finding wearing a model finding's clothes. Third, and this is the sentence the whole brief exists to find: **"cross-brief distinctiveness falls slightly below that of the base model, the only success criterion not met."**

**Pros:**
- **It is the first quantified evidence that optimising toward a rubric produces competent, convergent work.** Train a model to satisfy designers on 35 dimensions and its pages get better *and more alike*. That is the slop thesis with a number attached, published by people who did not want that result.
- **The infrastructure finding is practically useful**: most of the gain came from the loop — render, read a textual report of your own render, edit — which is exactly what a design skill or an agent harness can give you today for free.
- **Taste is treated as a measurable construct with a stated instrument**, not as mysticism, and the instrument is published with its admitted dimensions and its exclusions.
- **The synthetic-environment method** (base page + syntax-tree perturbation + measured difficulty ladder + quarantine against over-optimisation) is a sane model for generating design training data without a marketplace of real briefs.
- It is a small model — 4B — which means the ceiling is not scale, and the result is about method rather than compute.

**Cons:**
- **One paper, four authors with no stated affiliation, no linked code, dataset or artifact** in the abstract — nothing here can be reproduced or inspected, and the pages it produced are not shown.
- **The Taste Index is their own composite**, so "0.795" means nothing outside this paper; the more honest numbers are the blinded win rates, which are 0.600 and 0.569 — good, not decisive.
- **The distinctiveness regression gets one clause.** It is the most consequential finding in the paper for anyone in brand work and it is reported in passing, with no magnitude, no diagnosis and no attempt to fix it.
- The evaluation is on **web pages**, the same narrow surface as every other design benchmark, and nothing tests whether the learned taste transfers to a deck, a poster or a campaign system.
- Trained against a rubric that a panel of designers validated — which means the model is being optimised toward *the consensus of 35 people*, an inherently averaging operation. The paper does not engage with the possibility that this is the mechanism behind the distinctiveness drop.

**Should we...**
- 👀 **Watch it** — and take the negative finding as a standing warning.
**Why:** This paper is the intellectual justification for the thing this brief keeps insisting on, and it should be quoted in any internal argument about "can't we just give the model our rubric." The answer, with evidence, is: **you can, and it will make the pages cleaner and more alike.** The four tells from Contra, the generic-check signatures from unaverage, the tile law from Van de Sande — all of them are descriptions of the same gravitational pull, and this is the first measurement of it. The practical implication for the Unified Platform is uncomfortable and worth sitting with: every time we tighten a generation prompt with another "always do X, never do Y," we buy consistency and pay in distinctiveness, and there is no instrument in the department that would tell us we had paid. The remedy is not to stop writing rules — it is to keep one deliberate exception per surface, decided by a person, that no rule would have produced.

### unaverage-design — idea and material first, filters last
**Link:** https://github.com/jakob-meyer/unaverage · created **17 November 2026**, **v3.4.4 pushed 20 November** · Apache-2.0 · **22★, 0 forks, 0 open issues, one contributor**
**Type:** Tool / Workflow (a design skill whose ordering is the idea)

**What it is:** A design skill for Claude Code, the Claude app and Codex, covering an implausible breadth — websites, product UI and states, decks, social posts and carousels, ads, logos and brand systems, print, posters, packaging, trade-fair booths. What makes it worth reading is not the coverage, it is the **order of operations** and the **questions**. Before proposing anything, it asks five things: *what do your customers hold or look at; name one or two things you love the look of, from anywhere, not only websites; what can you no longer stand to see in your field; what do you tell someone who hesitates; what must I not promise or show.* Then it produces **three directions at three distances** — close to the category at its best (never the generic default), at the edge of the familiar, and bold — each named from your project, each with what you will see, why it fits, and **the risk**. Then it builds in the target medium (HTML/CSS/SVG at real size, rendered), runs blocking gates (WCAG 2.2 AA contrast, horizontal overflow at 320px, minimum font size per medium, export sizes, and "truth"), has **subagents in a fresh context play art director and target-audience reviewer** seeing no code and none of the reasoning, and runs a **generic check** that counts signatures — icon-library icons used as imagery, gradient text, coloured glows, card grids, template font stacks — replacing a generic element with something from the product's world rather than deleting it.

**What's good about it:** **The five questions are the product.** They are the questions a real art director asks in the first meeting and never writes down, and they are designed to extract *objects and aversions* rather than adjectives: nobody answers "what do your customers hold" with "premium," and nobody answers "what can you no longer stand to see in your field" with a compliment. Second: **the ordering claim is argued from failure.** *"What the German tests taught: rules and checks alone make results clean but empty. What worked against the skill's own older versions was idea and material first, then the system, then the filters."* That is the correct hierarchy and it is the opposite of how almost every design prompt in circulation is built, where the negative list comes first and does all the work. Third, and rarest: **the honesty about evidence.** It names which formats were tested blind and when (seven formats plus correction mode in the German line, 9–10 October; version 3.4.0 measured on two landing pages, one web app and one sales deck as a harm check, 30–31 October), states that social posts and brand work *"were never measured with 3.4 in a scored round; their numbers come from versions 3.0 to 3.2,"* and labels its own durations as *"the skill's own estimates, not measurements."*

**Pros:**
- **It refuses to recommend a direction whose material you do not have**, and tells you what would bring it back up — the single most useful thing a generator can say to a department with no photography budget this quarter.
- **The mockups must differ on at least two of five stated levels** (anchor, type voice, density, colour strategy, signature moment), not just colour — which makes "three directions" mean three ideas rather than three tints.
- **The best-rated version is delivered, not the last one**, and any fix made after the final review is explicitly labelled unreviewed. That is a version-control discipline no other skill in this genre has.
- **The generic check replaces rather than deletes**: a generic element is swapped for something from the product's world that does the same job. Deleting a gradient leaves a hole; replacing it leaves an idea.
- **Fresh-context reviewers with pairwise comparisons run in both orders** — a real methodological detail borrowed from evaluation research, not vibes.

**Cons:**
- **It is the fifth design skill in this brief in a fortnight**, and the pattern is now a genre: a preset, a linter, and a claim to studio-level output. The market is filling with taste as a downloadable package, which is its own kind of tell.
- **22 stars, one contributor, four days old, self-reported tests with a simulated user.** The German tests are real work; the English ones are the author grading his own homework.
- **Thirteen style directions is a template library wearing a method's clothes** — the same criticism this brief made of editorial-taste, and the ranking step does not dissolve it, it just makes the template choice legible.
- **The breadth is a marketing decision, not a capability.** Small caps, merch and trade-fair booths have not been measured, and the skill says so — which is honest and still means the format list oversells what is proven.
- A skill cannot ask a real client those five questions well. The entire value depends on someone with taste answering them, and the skill's own example answers ("a stamped roast card," "old pharmacy labels") are the kind a designer supplies, not a physician or a programme coordinator.

**Should we...**
- 👀 **Watch it** — and steal the questions today.
**Why:** The question set belongs in the Sub-I welcome brief and the match-season brief *before* any tool is opened, because it is a better brief than the one we currently write. "What do applicants hold or look at in the first week?" and "what can you no longer stand to see in residency marketing?" will produce more specific answers than any brand-voice document, and the second question in particular will surface the thing everyone in the department is quietly sick of — which is the most reliable raw material for work that does not look like everyone else's. The ordering claim is the second thing to adopt, and it costs nothing: in every generation prompt we write from now on, put the idea and the material first, the system second, and the prohibitions last. The prohibitions are the strongest part of a prompt and the least generative; used first, they produce pages that are clean and empty, which is exactly the failure mode the author names from his own testing. What not to do is install it and expect studio output: the skill's own numbers say the social and brand work has never been scored, and social and brand are most of what this department makes.

## Slop Radar
- **The eyebrow label.** Small, spaced-out uppercase above a heading, repeating what the heading already says: **93% of AI pages do it on at least half their sections, against 11% of human pages** (Contra's training set). It is the single most reliable tell in circulation, and it is in half the decks we have made this year.
- **Numbered sections.** 01, 02, 03 as an index down a page: **17% of AI pages, and not one human page in Contra's set** — not a single golden design. It reads as "designed" and it is a stamp.
- **Spec-sheet cosplay.** Ordinary marketing sections dressed as technical readouts — status labels, figure references, three tiers of small labels under one heading. Gemini 4 Argon does it on 91% of its pages against 35% elsewhere. In a hospital this is worse than elsewhere, because fake instrumentation borrows the authority of real data.
- **"Human designers are cooked."** The one in-window X post on the week's biggest design story (@Layton_Gott, 19 Nov) led with the extinction frame. The data says the opposite: human golden designs **won 74% of votes** while scoring sixth on the rubric. The hype misreads the result it is quoting.

## Overall Assessment
The week's real news is not that a stealth model beat human designers on a leaderboard — it is that **design taste became an instrument, and the instrument immediately found its own limit.** Contra built a rubric that predicts designer preference at r = 0.94, then watched that rubric rank human work sixth while designers chose it 74% of the time; TD-Bench trained a model to satisfy such rubrics and documented the model getting better and less distinctive. Put those two results together and the strategic picture for this department is unusually clear: **execution — spacing, hierarchy, contrast, whether everything renders whole — is now the cheap, measured, automated part, and it is about to be free and uniformly competent.** What remains expensive, and what no rubric in these studies captured, is the thing that made the human golden designs win anyway: a real photograph of a real person, a sentence only this department could write, an idea that came from a museum rather than a mood board. So the highest-leverage move this week is not a tool, it is a document: **write the DESIGN.md for the Unified Platform.** Measure the five live surfaces, reconcile the disagreements into one file with a reason beside each value, put it in the repo, and make it the first thing every prompt reads — because every item in today's brief, from Siteprint's MCP to Contra's four tells to unaverage's ordering, assumes the machine has a contract to build against, and ours has only ever existed in our heads. Then, with the contract in place, spend the rest of the year on the half the rubric cannot see.
