### Automattic Design — Design Writing: Working with AI Agents
**Link:** [automattic.design/2026/11/16/design-writing-working-with-ai-agents](https://automattic.design/2026/11/16/design-writing-working-with-ai-agents/)
**Type:** Workflow
**What it is:** Automattic's design writing team turned the research on AI writing tells into two installable agent skills — `agentic-ux-copy` for interface copy and `voiceover-scriptwriting` for narration — each pairing a deterministic linter with human judgment, and published the research-backed guidance and downloadable instructions to their public design system.
**What's good about it:** The research review is the best short summary of the evidence I have read, and every number is sourced. Pew classified ~490,000 English pages from Common Crawl spanning January 2021 to July 2026 using the Pangram detector: AI-typical vocabulary has more than doubled since 2023, negative parallelism — "it's not just X, it's Y" — has nearly tripled, and about 9.4% of .com pages show signs of AI authorship, roughly double the rate for .org. Jakob Nielsen found ChatGPT writes at a 16th-grade level, comprehensible to the top 5–10% of readers, against a long-standing usability recommendation of 8th grade; asked for 8th grade it produced 10th, and he had to request 6th to get 8th. Wikipedia's editors track the vocabulary drift by model era — "delve" peaked in 2023 and faded, and newer models lean on "emphasizing," "enhance," "highlighting" and "showcasing." Then the craft position, which is the part worth stealing: brand files must be built on real copy examples rather than adjectives, because "persona and tone instructions do around 5% of the work people assume, and the examples carry the rest." The linter ships 47 banned words and 33 banned patterns plus a set of judgment calls flagged for human review. Three rules carry most of the load — no parroting (don't restate the prompt, the feature name, or the heading you sit under), no repetition (vary sentence structure, not just vocabulary), and no concrete verbs on abstract concepts. And the voiceover skill is refreshingly practical: spoken English runs 130–150 words per minute so a 15-second slot holds 30–40 words; if the script runs long, cut a point, never speed up the read, because rushed delivery makes a video feel cheap.
**Pros:**
- The verbatim-claims rule is a genuine risk control: "more than 99.99% uptime since 2005" must never be paraphrased into "guaranteed 99.999%," which is the difference between a style preference and a legal problem.
- The voiceover skill normalizes numbers to spoken form per language ("11" is "eleven," "once," "elf") and pulls emotion tags from per-model dictionaries covering six TTS providers, because tag syntax doesn't transfer between models — the portability detail that separates a real skill from a demo.
- It treats consent and disclosure as steps the agent cannot skip, citing the SAG-AFTRA Interactive Media Agreement and EU AI Act Article 50 rather than leaving them in a policy document.
- The core skill is kept deliberately light and loads per-brand reference files on demand, and brand files record their source and date and get re-distilled when the guide changes — a maintenance model, not a one-time prompt.
**Cons:**
- The skills themselves are internal; what is public is the research guidance and downloadable instructions. You are rebuilding the linter, not installing it.
- They admit the fatal flaw themselves: ban lists go stale as model vocabulary shifts by era, so a linter is a treadmill that needs a named owner or it silently regenerates the problem.
- 47 banned words will produce false positives on clinical and academic copy, where "significant," "protocol" and "cohort" are load-bearing. A hospital department cannot adopt the word list unmodified.
- Voiceover timing is planned against 130–150 wpm, a reasonable general rate that drifts by voice and by language; the buffer is a rule of thumb presented with more confidence than the evidence supports.
**Should we...**
- ✅ **Adopt it** — use it now; it raises our design ceiling
**Why:** This lands squarely on Shareef's least-defended surface, which is words. The platform and the player have visual systems; the residency marketing, the Sub-I welcome packet and the email drafts have voice, and voice is exactly what degrades first when copy gets delegated. The three rules are adoptable today with no tooling at all — the no-parroting rule alone would delete the most common failure in departmental writing, which is a card whose description simply repeats its own heading. The 5% finding reshapes how a brand file gets written: stop putting "warm, authoritative, humanistic" at the top of a prompt and start pasting eight real sentences that sound right. The voiceover skill matters concretely because there are 28 faculty interview clips sitting there and every one needs a script that fits a spoken duration rather than a page — plan against word count, and when it runs long, cut a claim instead of compressing the read. One adaptation is mandatory before use: prune the ban list against clinical vocabulary, or the linter will fight the medicine.

### Figma AI — multi-model image comparison
**Link:** [designcompass.org/en/2026/11/20/figma-ai-image-model-comparison](https://designcompass.org/en/2026/11/20/figma-ai-image-model-comparison/)
**Type:** Tool
**What it is:** A Figma AI update, released November 18, that generates multiple image drafts at once and compares output from different models on the same canvas — two, four or six images from a single model, or up to three models side by side on an identical prompt.
**What's good about it:** It puts the agency habit into the tool designers already have open. Instead of prompting, judging, re-prompting and losing the earlier attempt, you get a grid: six drafts from one model, or one image each from three models — the credit guide currently lists GPT Image 2.5 Sunburst, NanoBanana 2 Lite and NanoBanana 2 — all from the same prompt, with reference images allowed to describe the direction. The most useful detail is the hover preview: when filling an existing layer, hovering a candidate shows how it would actually look applied to that area, so the judgement happens in context rather than against a floating thumbnail. "Place all on canvas" drops every candidate in for comparison. Both make and edit are covered, and images you inserted yourself are eligible for editing. Available on all plans, requires file editing permission, and Starter limits it to Figma Design.
**Pros:**
- Comparing models on one prompt, in one frame, with the same reference images, is the only honest way to choose a model for a specific job — and it takes a minute instead of a subscription.
- The hover-into-layer preview removes the biggest source of wasted generation, which is judging an image out of the composition it was made for.
- Sitting inside the design file means the chosen image arrives already placed, at the right scale, in the right layer — no export and re-import through a folder.
- Reference images are accepted alongside the prompt, which is the difference between describing a look and showing one.
**Cons:**
- Credits are shared across all Figma AI features and scale with both model and image count; six images across three models is eighteen generations of spend for one decision, and the meter is easy to forget.
- The comparison screen scores nothing. No brief-adherence check, no brand-fit flag, no slop read — you are the only judge, which means the tool multiplies output faster than it multiplies judgment.
- The model roster is a rolling list Figma controls and the credit guide revises; a workflow built around one model name has a shelf life you do not set.
- Three models maximum in comparison mode, and one image per model there — enough to choose a direction, not enough to choose a hero.
**Should we...**
- ✅ **Adopt it** — use it now; it raises our design ceiling
**Why:** Adopt the practice more than the panel. This is the strongest evidence yet that the industry accepted the correct answer to AI design's biggest usability problem — people cannot describe what they want but can pick a favourite in three seconds, so stop asking for a description and start showing six things. That principle is already load-bearing in how preview surfaces get reviewed here, and it is why a side-by-side comparison page works where a written brief does not. Where it changes things for Shareef is the social and marketing pipeline: today a single image gets generated, evaluated in isolation, and either kept or quietly abandoned. Six candidates against two reference images, previewed in the actual post composition, converts that into a decision with a record. The discipline it demands is the one a rubric supplies — bring your own standard, because Figma will hand you eighteen options and no opinion. And the honest limit: a comparison grid is not art direction. If the reference images are generic, six drafts will be six flavours of the same thing.

### Canva 2027 Design Trends — "Make it real, make it yours"
**Link:** [canva.com/newsroom/news/canva-2027-design-trends](https://www.canva.com/newsroom/news/canva-2027-design-trends/)
**Type:** Idea
**What it is:** Canva's annual forecast, published November 17 from its own search and template-usage data, naming five directions for 2027 — Archive Era, Codebreaking, Slow Burn, Rebel by Design, Wonderstruck — under one thesis: when anything can be generated generically in seconds, the things people choose to make slowly start to stand out.
**What's good about it:** The thesis is the news, and it comes from the least likely source: the platform that profits from fast template production has published a forecast arguing against its own core use case. "Making It Real means designing with something tangible at the center: a visible process, a clear point of view, or a moment of wonder." The evidence is specific — searches for "whimsy" up 720%, "vibe coding" up 334%, "junk journal" up 249%, "scrapbooking" up 94%, "craft paper" up 196%. **Archive Era** is the one to read twice: faded polaroids beside classical landscape paintings and marble statues, ordinary things treated as museum objects, deep red borders framing old paintings and porcelain like a catalogue cover, your own photographs stacked and scattered alongside old paintings until your life looks like it belongs in the same collection. **Slow Burn** is the palette lesson underneath it — burgundy, rust, mustard, olive and dusty rose, described as "colors that feel inherited rather than picked off a trend board," which is a sentence worth putting on the wall. And the two quoted practitioners say the quiet part: Lisa Smith, Global Chief Design Officer at Uncommon Creative Studio — "The more perfect and frictionless technology becomes, the more exciting it is to see the fingerprints"; Maria Izvestkina, Canva's trends design lead — "In 2027 a design with a point of view will travel further than one that is merely well executed."
**Pros:**
- It names the mechanism rather than the mood: a visible process, a real source, a working mechanism, a clear point of view. Those are things you can brief against; "authentic" is not.
- The volumes are large and the numbers are hard — millions of searches and template/element usage, not a mood board assembled by an agency.
- Archive Era is a *compositional* direction, not a filter: the move is putting an ordinary thing next to a museum object at the same scale, which is a decision about hierarchy and cropping.
- "Colors that feel inherited rather than picked off a trend board" is the single most useful colour rule published this week, and it is one line.
**Cons:**
- Every trend ships with a design kit, which guarantees the archive look will be executed identically by everyone who reads the report. The fix for sameness, packaged for distribution, becomes the next sameness.
- The instrument is biased by construction: it measures what people search for *inside Canva*, not what the wider culture is doing, and a template marketplace rewards the searchable and the imitable.
- The trend names are brandable neologisms — "Wonderstruck," "Rebel by Design" — which are hard to hand to a designer as a brief without translating them first.
- Archive Era arrives at the exact moment models can fabricate convincing archival ephemera, so most executions will be invented memorabilia — the precise opposite of the thesis it is selling.
**Should we...**
- ✅ **Adopt it** — use it now; it raises our design ceiling
**Why:** Adopt the direction and refuse the kit. This is the closest thing to external confirmation of a principle already in use here — the standing note on the rec hero is "curated scrapbook, not random collage," and Archive Era is that same instinct stated as a compositional system by a platform with the data to back it. The transferable mechanics are precise: treat ordinary institutional objects as museum objects, at the same scale as the precious ones, and let deep red or navy borders do the framing that a layout grid would otherwise do. For the residency program that means real teaching artifacts — a hand-written call schedule, a worn badge, a first-year's notes, a well-used cystoscope — placed next to formal portraiture at equal weight, rather than in a scrapbook corner. The Slow Burn palette also solves a live problem: inherited colour is exactly how you get a warm, human palette that is not the beige-serif-terracotta combination that has become its own tell. The one rule to hold: real sources only. The moment the archival material is generated, the whole argument collapses into the thing it was arguing against.

### Claude Mobile — a tab bar designed *against* the platform default
**Link:** [designcompass.org/en/2026/11/23/claude-mobile-app-tab-bar-redesign](https://designcompass.org/en/2026/11/23/claude-mobile-app-tab-bar-redesign/)
**Type:** Technique
**What it is:** The new Claude mobile design, unveiled November 20, which puts Chat, Projects, Code and Artifacts in a bottom tab bar — a tab bar Anthropic built itself after testing iOS's default and rejecting it, in order to put the weight on the message input field.
**What's good about it:** The stated reasoning is the whole item. Product designer Daniel DeStefanis says the team tested the operating system's default tab bar across several drafts and ultimately designed their own, specifically to draw more attention to the message input field. That is a designer choosing a hierarchy — conversation first, navigation second — and paying the cost of a custom component to protect it, which is the exact decision most product teams skip because the platform default is free and defensible. The interaction design is disciplined too: four destinations, tapped to open, swiped left and right across the bar to move between them, and press-and-hold reveals recently used items in a panel just above the bar — holding Projects expands recent project names with "New Project" at the bottom of the list. One bar, four permanent homes, and a gesture layer for recency instead of a fifth tab or a hamburger. They also reworked the coding entry point: from the home screen you choose a connected device to run on and start a new Claude Code session there. Rollout begins on iPhones running iOS 26 or later.
**Pros:**
- Testing the platform default in several drafts before rejecting it is the correct order of operations, and it is rare to see the rejected alternative named publicly alongside the reason.
- Protecting the primary action from the navigation chrome is a hierarchy argument, not a taste argument — the kind of rationale you can defend in a review.
- Recents on press-and-hold keeps the bar at four destinations while still giving fast access, instead of growing a fifth tab every time a feature ships.
- Swipe-across-the-bar adds a second navigation mode without claiming a separate gesture region or an edge-swipe that would collide with the OS.
**Cons:**
- A hand-built tab bar is a permanent maintenance obligation: it must be re-verified against every OS release, and the iOS 26 floor shows how tightly it is coupled to the platform it replaced.
- Press-and-hold is an undiscoverable gesture. Nothing on screen suggests recents live there, and it duplicates a function the projects list already performs.
- The stated goal is achieved partly by suppressing the tab bar's own affordances, which can make an app feel like it has fewer places to go — a real cost that goes unmeasured here.
- There is no published evidence at all: no task-completion, no wayfinding study, no before-and-after. The rationale is a designer's account, not a result, and designers are fluent in post-hoc reasoning.
**Should we...**
- ✅ **Adopt it** — use it now; it raises our design ceiling
**Why:** Adopt the procedure, which is four words long: test the default, then reject it for a stated reason. The Unified Platform's nav has exactly this question buried in it — every item currently competes equally, and the thing a resident opens the app to do is not equally weighted with the things they open twice a year. Anthropic's answer was to build their own chrome so the message field won the screen; the equivalent here is deciding what the one primary action per role is and letting the navigation get quieter around it. The recents-on-hold pattern is also directly stealable for a schedule surface: hold Schedule, see the months you actually look at. What to leave behind is the custom component unless the hierarchy argument justifies the maintenance — and on a departmental platform maintained by one person, it usually does not.

### Canva MCP — generally available
**Link:** [canva.com/newsroom/news/canva-mcp-update](https://www.canva.com/newsroom/news/canva-mcp-update/)
**Type:** Tool
**What it is:** As of November 23, Canva's MCP server is generally available with no waitlist, alongside new REST APIs, an expanded Canva CLI and service accounts with API billing — letting agents and outside applications create editable designs, generate images and video, remove backgrounds, turn flat images into layered designs, resize creative, export, and retrieve brand kits and templates.
**What's good about it:** The architecture is right for keeping volume generation on brand: the tools an agent sees include `list-brand-kits`, so the constraint on generation is the brand kit rather than the prompt. Work stays editable in Canva, which keeps human review in the loop instead of ending in a flattened export. The MCP was redesigned specifically for agents — overlapping capabilities consolidated so a model running on a weaker base can still pick the right tool and reach a result using less context — which is a real and underrated engineering problem. They exposed Canva Craft, their own image model, through MCP and API, and opened a CLI path for bulk work rather than forcing everything through a large tool schema; 72% of typed CLI commands in October came from agents. The adoption numbers are substantial: nearly 500,000 signups through MCP since October, usage up 93% month over month, four in ten of those users new to Canva.
**Pros:**
- Brand kits as a first-class tool makes on-brand the path of least resistance for an agent, rather than a constraint you restate in every prompt.
- Three connection modes for three jobs — MCP for open-ended outcomes, REST for precise control inside your own product, CLI for bulk work — is a mature split rather than one API pretending to do everything.
- Editable output preserves the human review step, which is the difference between an asset you can fix and an asset you have to regenerate.
- Service accounts with API billing make unattended, scheduled creation possible without tying the work to one person's login.
**Cons:**
- This is infrastructure for products, not a craft upgrade. Nothing here improves a composition; it improves the distribution of Canva's existing template logic.
- The brand kit becomes the ceiling. If the kit is thin or the templates are generic, an agent will now produce generic work faster and with better compliance — the fastest possible route to consistent mediocrity.
- 500,000 signups and a 300% increase in expected ARR are distribution metrics, and the announcement is written for them. Nothing in it addresses output quality.
- It deepens Canva lock-in precisely where the work most needs to look least like Canva. The template aesthetic is one of the tells this brief exists to escape.
**Should we...**
- 👀 **Watch it** — promising, not proven yet
**Why:** Watch, for a specific reason rather than a vague one. The transferable lesson is already usable with no code: if generation is going to happen at volume — monthly social creative, recurring residency announcements, the Sub-I packet refresh — the brand kit has to carry the art direction, because the prompt will not survive being handed to an agent at 2am. That makes the useful work this month an audit rather than an integration: what does the kit actually encode? Is the accent the only colour available, is there a real type scale, does any template carry a signature detail, or is it a logo and four swatches. A thin kit plugged into an MCP is a slop factory with good auth. Revisit when there is a measurable case — one recurring asset that currently costs hours — rather than an available integration.

## Slop Radar
- **Tailwind's indigo is the default because one person picked it once.** Adam Wathan's `bg-indigo-500` button from ~2019 got copied into tutorials and starter projects, models learned it, and he has since apologized — mostly joking — that "every AI generated UI on earth" is indigo. The lesson is not "ban purple": the model returns the most common version it has seen, so the only real fix is replacing the default with a decision of your own.
- **Banning the tell produces the next tell.** Strip violet and you reliably get beige, a serif headline and a terracotta button — now so common it functions as its own AI signature. Any rule list that only forbids will be defeated by substitution within a release cycle, which is why this brief keeps preferring directions to bans.
- **Mitigation delivered as a kit.** Every 2027 trend now ships with a design kit, and a slop-fixing method spawned a derivative with 72 numbered fixes and a 0-to-100 score within a week of release. The moment a corrective is packaged for everyone, applying it produces everyone's result.
- **Tool launches are now content about tool launches.** The unslop announcement was a ten-second clip that passed 1.9 million views; a popular account rewrote Damir's prompt days later and published it with the editing placeholder `[ALEX: run it first and add one true line here]` still sitting in the text. Verify a method against its repo, not its thread.

## Overall Assessment
The week's real movement is that the anti-slop position stopped being a purist stance and became an industry consensus, arriving from three directions that do not usually agree: a template platform publishing, from its own search data, that anything generated in seconds is what people are now avoiding; a design tool putting six options on one canvas so a human can pick instead of describe; and a product team rejecting the OS default navigation in order to protect the one action its users actually came for. That last one is the thread running through all of it. Every item this week is a decision about where the human judgment goes — before the generation, in the comparison, or in refusing the convenient default — and in each case the craft is in the choosing, not the making. The single highest-leverage move for Shareef this week is to write one direction down before anything gets generated: five lines naming the audience, two character words that are not "clean" or "modern," the physical world the piece belongs to, where colour is allowed to get loud, and one signature detail that repeats. Apply it to the residency program's marketing creative, because that surface's recurring problem — too dark, too grid-clean, collage instead of scrapbook — is a missing-direction problem rather than a talent problem. Archive Era supplies the compositional answer and the palette; the five lines supply the discipline that keeps it from being a kit.
