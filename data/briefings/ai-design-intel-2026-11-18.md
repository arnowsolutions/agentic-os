### Pentagram × Alpha Bank — a typeface drawn from the proportions of a coin
**Link:** https://www.designweek.co.uk/pentagram-draws-on-ancient-greek-coinage-for-alpha-bank-identity-refresh/ · published **17 November 2026** · Design Week · work by **Pentagram** (Domenic Lippa and team) with brand strategy by Simple Revolution and branch experience by Dalziel & Pow, for Alpha Bank, Athens, est. 1879
**Type:** Idea (a complete identity system, and a method)

**What it is:** A refresh of Greece's oldest listed bank, built on one object: the **silver stater of Aegina** — the coin minted by the first city-state in Europe to strike its own currency, and the source of Alpha Bank's original 1972 emblem. Pentagram redrew that emblem, framed it in an expressive roundel that preserves the coin's *asymmetries* rather than tidying them away, and then derived everything else from it: **Alpha Sans**, a custom five-weight typeface drawn with Tom Baber in Greek and Latin, whose proportions come from the symbol; **Alpha Text** as the functional companion for body copy; a bespoke icon suite whose geometry follows the mark; abstract and logo-derived patterns; and a palette built out of existing **Alpha Blue** (hex `10038C`, Pantone 072C) and **Galazio Blue** (`80E5FF`) plus secondary Thalassi Turquoise, Heritage Pink and Alpha Gold, used to distinguish business and private banking. The most consequential structural decision is stated plainly by Lippa: *"The symbol needed to become the emotional heart of the brand, as it was originally… For this to happen, we needed to liberate it from the wordmark."*

**What's good about it:** The typeface. Every identity deck claims a "custom typeface"; almost none of them can tell you what it was drawn *from*. Here the answer is a measurable object — the letterforms were built to the proportions of a coin that has been sitting in the client's own vault since 1972, which means the type cannot be swapped for another sans without the whole system coming apart. That is the difference between a type licence and a type system. Second: the discipline of keeping the coin's irregularities. *"I wanted to make the symbol reconnect to its source — the original silver stater,"* Lippa says, and the roundel's slight asymmetries are the receipt: someone looked at the object instead of tracing a circle. Third: the palette does the institutional work that a palette should. Alpha Blue is not decorative, it is the bank's oldest asset; the secondary three exist to differentiate business from private banking — colour as org chart, which is how a 200-branch institution actually needs colour to behave.

**Pros:**
- **A derivational chain you can audit.** Symbol → typeface proportions → icon geometry → patterns → palette, each step traceable to the coin. That is what "system" means, and it is vanishingly rare in a market of assembled moodboards.
- The wordmark was made *less formal* while the symbol got louder — the correct answer for an institution that has to read as both trustworthy and contemporary, and the opposite of the usual timid refresh that changes nothing and claims everything.
- Greek and Latin in one five-weight family is real production work, not a display cut. Bilingual banks are where type systems usually break, and this one was designed at the seam.
- Photography direction is specified as a system — everyday moments, consistent depth of field across day and night, portraits and landscapes — which is what stops a brand from becoming a stock library with a logo on it.
- Physical application is part of the system, not an afterthought: Dalziel & Pow rebuilt the Kolonaki branch, and the identity survives a fascia, an ATM surround and a dark-mode floor directory.

**Cons:**
- **It is Pentagram's own account of Pentagram's own work, relayed by trade press.** No client metrics, no recognition research, no cost, no before/after testing — and the quotes are all the creative director's.
- Custom Greek-and-Latin type with a five-weight family plus a text companion is a six-figure, multi-month commitment. It is a standard to admire, not a package to install, and there is no smaller version of it in the article.
- The positioning language is the weak link, exactly as it was with Landor's Crystal Ski: *"Building progress, together"* and *"A new together"* fail the swap test — substitute any bank, insurer or utility and both lines still work.
- "Liberating the symbol from the wordmark" is presented as a discovery; it is also the single most common move in financial branding over the last decade, and no evidence is offered that it did anything here.
- Three secondary colours plus five digital-only brights is a palette that will drift. The article shows the boards, not the governance.

**Should we...**
- ✅ **Adopt it** — the derivation chain, and specifically the type-from-mark move.
**Why:** Two days ago this brief argued for deriving a brand device from a property only the subject owns; today's best item is the full worked example, and it goes one step further than Landor did by letting the derivation reach the *type*. That step is directly available to Shareef. The Unified Platform runs on Inter — correctly, because a hospital cannot invent a text face — but the pieces that carry voice are not the body copy: they are the display moments, the Grand Rounds series title, the residency programme's annual identity, the Chapter Player wordmark. Those can be drawn from something real: the geometry of a uroflow curve, the circular field of a cystoscope, the 28 interviews' framing, the department's own numerals. The concrete exercise is a half-day, and it is the same one the Landor note proposed, extended: list five properties true of this programme and no other; pick one with actual geometry; then build not just a mark from it but a **display cut** whose proportions come from it, and let the body copy stay Inter. The other thing to steal is Lippa's working maxim — *"we are eternally searching for the truth in the solution"* — which is the only sentence in the piece that explains why the coin's asymmetries survived. And the honest bound: we cannot commission a bilingual five-weight family, so the deliverable is one display cut in two or three weights, licensed or drawn, used only where voice matters.

### Mercury Builder Mode — prototyping inside the product's own code, with the numbers attached
**Link:** https://x.com/laurenloprete/status/2122746422680506761 · published **17 November 2026** · by Lauren LoPrete, design at Mercury (217 likes, 13 replies, 4 reposts) · the launch it describes: Mercury Agent Controls
**Type:** Workflow (an in-house method, documented in public)

**What it is:** An account of the internal tooling behind a shipped feature. Mercury built **Builder Mode** so designers and PMs work with a coding agent *inside Mercury's real product code*, rather than in a sandbox that resembles the product or a local dev environment that takes hours to stand up. From a browser panel they switch between versions, user states and feature flags, collect feedback on the prototype itself, and hand it to the agent. Prototypes live in **GitHub as draft PRs**, so engineers build from the same code instead of rebuilding. Three components sit on top of Claude Code / Codex / Cursor: a toolkit of agent instructions and skills; **states, flags and mock data** (increasingly wired to a mock database, so "a freshly approved company" renders with empty states nobody designed); and **"tapes"** — saved agent conversations that replay in the product, because the interesting moments happen several messages in and the model will not answer the same way twice.

**What's good about it:** The admission that every enterprise design team has been avoiding out loud: *"Every enterprise product holds more states than a design canvas can carry. An account can be new, frozen, under review, over a limit, or waiting on someone else's approval, and even a mature design system won't know about any of that."* That is the exact reason generated screens look designed and behave fake, and Mercury's answer is not a better mockup — it is mocking the *data layer* so the states arrive for free. Second: the eval loop. They had **agents play designers**, run a standard set of prototyping tasks, and report where they got stuck — and the friction they found (setup first, then the rest) shaped each version. That is the same method Underlay arrived at from production, used here as discovery rather than audit. Third: "tapes" is the first genuinely new primitive I have seen for reviewing agentic interfaces. A saved, replayable conversation is how you review a flow whose middle is unreachable.

**Pros:**
- **The numbers are production numbers, not pilot numbers:** 300+ prototypes across the company; one designer producing roughly **200 views** of an accounts page in two days, each with its own link; a designer acting as designer, PM and engineer shipping Agent Controls **three weeks** from first idea. Compare that with 2–3 hours of setup failure per attempt before.
- "Prototypes live in GitHub as draft PRs" makes design and engineering one artifact. That single decision removes the handoff step that Figma's own research says has been broken for years, and it means the prototype is *inheritable* — teammates branch from each other's work.
- States and flags are declared, not drawn. A designer prompts for an account state instead of drawing forty frames and keeping them in sync.
- The honest section that most vendors omit — **"Why we didn't buy it"** — with the specific diagnosis: off-the-shelf tools *"build you a new app, and the best of them hand the agent your UI kit and stop there. That gets the right component onto a superficial view of your product without any context into how the product works."*
- The closing line is the thesis of the entire fortnight: *"We don't have to trade craft for speed anymore. The ceiling now is how much context your agents have."*

**Cons:**
- **It is a single post by the team that built it, describing work they shipped.** No failure cases, no list of what the agent still gets wrong, no measure of prototype quality — only of speed and volume.
- Everything depends on a bespoke internal tool and an agent-eval harness that a department cannot replicate. The transferable part is the architecture, not the product.
- Draft PRs as the design medium has a real cost nobody discusses: non-technical stakeholders cannot review a branch, and the review surface becomes GitHub comments. That may be fine — but it is a trade, not a free win.
- "300+ prototypes" is a quantity, and quantity is exactly the metric this brief distrusts. Two hundred views in two days could be a rigour win or a slot machine with a scroll bar; the post does not distinguish.
- Tapes depend on deterministic replay of a nondeterministic system; the post says the conversation plays back, not what happens when the model's behaviour has moved on.

**Should we...**
- ✅ **Adopt it** — the state-and-mock-data architecture, targeted at the platform's worst screens.
**Why:** The Unified Platform's real quality problem is named in this post: our generated screens are designed for the one state where everything works. A call-out flow drawn with three residents present, one absent and a successful submit; a schedule grid with a swap pending; a resident profile mid-rotation — these are the screens that decide whether the department trusts the software, and they are the ones no mockup ever shows. Mercury's architecture says: don't draw them, **mock the data and let them fall out**. Concretely, in our terms, for zero cost and no new tooling: write the mock fixtures first — a resident who has not submitted, one with a name that overflows the column, a shift with no eligible swappers, a request submitted twice from a phone on a bad connection — then render the real components against them and review what appears. That is `craft-states` done in code instead of prose, and it is the same insight as Underlay's audit from a different direction: the truth lives in the data, not the picture. What we cannot copy is Builder Mode itself; what we must not copy is the volume metric. Judge the result by one question: did mocking a state reveal a screen we had never designed?

### Tom Johnson — "Am I still a designer?"
**Link:** https://x.com/tomjohndesign/status/2120936441853386865 · published **12 November 2026** · by Tom Johnson, Staff Product Designer at Vercel (108 likes, 12 replies)
**Type:** Idea (the most honest essay of the week)

**What it is:** A long, unpolished, entirely first-person account of what happened when the work moved from a canvas into a coding agent. Johnson's framing: he used to be a *player* — someone who held the instrument and produced the artifact — and now he is a *conductor*, giving abstract direction to competent musicians whose playing he cannot perform. The realisation arrives via his portfolio: remaking it, he found a mountain of design detritus from every earlier period, and then **"in my recent work there was… just the app. Where were the explorations? The decisions? The abandoned ideas along the way?"** He is not mourning productivity — he says he made more design work in the last three months than the previous three years. He is mourning the **evidence**: *"I can't show the 'here's how I got here'… not because they didn't exist, but because they were removed as soon as I prompted 'nah, get rid of that idea, let's try it this way.'"*

**What's good about it:** It isolates a loss that no tool review has named, and it is the exact inverse of yesterday's brief. Underlay said: write your reasons down where the machine can read them. Johnson says: the machine is where the reasons now live, and it does not keep them. Between those two statements sits the whole discipline — the decision trail has to be *externalised on purpose*, because the workflow that produces the fastest work is the one that leaves the fewest traces. The second strong thing is the honesty of the self-diagnosis. He does not claim the new way is worse; he asks whether the old artifacts were ever the point, and does not answer. *"Does being a designer mean that you have a canvas of forgotten or half-baked ideas?… Or does it mean that you found a real problem, made a real product, that you created, that you shipped?"* The unresolved question is more useful than a tidy conclusion would have been.

**Pros:**
- **It identifies the artifact problem precisely:** a design file used to be a by-product of thinking, and now there is no by-product. Ten years of process evidence, replaced by a final build.
- The conductor metaphor is the best description of the new role I have read — and it comes with the sting everyone avoids: *"the conductor is not a musician."*
- It names the failure of the old prompting style with a concrete before/after: from *"This div needs to be aligned to the right side inside of this container"* to *"improve the settings page it's bloated"* — and notes that the second one only works because the agent can see the whole instrument.
- It is written without a product to sell. No framework, no course, no skill pack.
- 108 likes for a 900-word post with no images is a small piece of evidence that designers are starved for exactly this conversation.

**Cons:**
- **It offers no method,** which is both its integrity and its limitation. "I lost the trail" is a diagnosis; it does not tell you what to keep, where, or in what format.
- One designer, one stack, one very recent workflow — and a designer senior enough that shipping alone is possible. The conductor role presupposes an agent good enough to be the orchestra, which is not true of everyone.
- The music metaphor runs for two-thirds of the piece and is doing real work, but it also lets the harder questions stay abstract — "how does a conductor demonstrate competence" is answered with another question.
- It is about *process legibility*, not design quality. A beautifully archived bad decision is still a bad decision, and the post risks implying otherwise.
- Nothing here measures anything, and the one number it offers — more design work in three months than three years — is unfalsifiable.

**Should we...**
- ✅ **Adopt it** — the prescription, not the essay: reinstate the decision trail as a deliberate artifact.
**Why:** This is the third angle on the same urgent thing, and it is the one we can act on this week without installing anything. The brief has spent a fortnight cataloguing enforcement — checks that exit non-zero, caps that stop a good score being talked up, audit scripts that find what screenshots hide — and Johnson supplies the missing half: the part that a check cannot reconstruct is *why*. For Shareef's surfaces the loss is concrete and slightly alarming. Sixteen months of the Unified Platform's look exists as components, tokens and a git history; almost none of the reasons exist anywhere a human will read them. When someone asks in a year why the call-out flow has three confirmations, or why the schedule grid is that dense, the answer is in nobody's head and no commit message. What to do, and it takes an afternoon: open a single decision log — a plain markdown file, one line per decision, the rule plus the reason plus the date, in Underlay's format — and backfill it from memory for the ten decisions that would be expensive to re-litigate: one navy accent and where the gold sits, 44px controls with 18px icons, Inter and the sizes actually in use, the empty-state copy rules, why call-out is called call-out. Then make one rule: any change to a shared surface adds a line. That file is the portfolio of the thinking, and unlike the essays above, it cannot be lost in a context window.

### Everleaf × LOVE. — the label made by laying real plants on light-sensitive paper
**Link:** https://www.designweek.co.uk/sunlight-glassmaking-and-cyanotypes-inside-everleafs-redesign-by-love/ · published **18 November 2026** · Design Week, by Pushkar Mankar · work by **LOVE.** (Manchester / New York) for Everleaf, the non-alcoholic aperitif founded by conservation biologist Paul Mathew
**Type:** Idea / Technique (art direction through a physical process)

**What it is:** An evolution, not a rebellion — LOVE. was briefed to refine Everleaf's existing botanical world and take it into off-trade and DTC. The package: a bespoke bottle whose glass weight drops from **500g to 385g (a 23% material saving)** in 100% post-consumer recycled Verallia Vista glass, with the "Forever Leaf" icon **moulded directly into the glass** and acacia leaf relief embossed around the base; labels on wood-free stock from 100% recycled pulp; single-colour halftone botanical illustration on the shippers to keep them recyclable. And then the unusual part: to differentiate the three expressions, the team **stepped away from the screen entirely** and used cyanotype — the 19th-century process pioneered by botanist Anna Atkins. Real saffron and orchid leaves for Forest, rosehip and cherry blossom for Mountain, seaweed and olive branch for Marine were laid on light-sensitive paper and exposed to sunlight, then scanned, layered and refined.

**What's good about it:** The argument for analogue texture, made by a commercial studio in a sentence: *"Commercial projects rarely offer the freedom to step away from the screen and get hands-on. There's something magical about cyanotype… it lets plants leave their own beautiful imprints."* That is the technical answer to slop, and it is worth stating plainly: **a cyanotype cannot be a model's average of a cyanotype**, because it is a physical transfer from a specific plant on a specific day. Every generated botanical texture is a median of the category; this one is an index of an object. Second: the sustainability work is design work, not a badge. Losing 115g of glass is a structural redesign with production consequences — better manufacturing efficiency, fewer weak points — and the halftone translation exists so the illustration survives single-colour flexo *and* keeps the packaging recyclable. Third: the hierarchy was resolved physically — *"mood boards, linocut experiments, early glass renders and packaging nets"* — which is how a system gets decided before it gets drawn.

**Pros:**
- **A texture source that is structurally un-generatable**, at low cost: paper, sunlight, plants, a scanner. No licence, no model, no GPU, and the output is legally and morally clean in a way generated imagery is not.
- The embossed-glass decision is the systematic one: the icon is not printed beside the object, it is *in* the object, so the brand cannot be removed by a label change.
- Sustainability expressed as a number with a structural cause — 500g → 385g, 100% PCR, recycled pulp, foil-free — rather than as a leaf icon and a green word.
- Single-colour halftone as a constraint that improved the work: it forced the cyanotypes to be tonal, which is why the shippers look designed rather than printed.
- Colour-coding across front and top for bartenders *"during busy service"* — an identity decision made by watching the actual user under pressure.

**Cons:**
- **It is a studio-supplied case study.** Every quote is LOVE.'s, there are no sales or shelf-conversion numbers, and no indication of what the bespoke mould cost — which for a bottle is the number that decides whether anyone can copy this.
- The technique is one shot. Once you have cyanotyped three botanicals, you cannot cyanotype the fourth campaign without repeating yourself, and the brand now owns an asset that does not scale to volume.
- Cyanotype has a strong palette of its own — Prussian blue — and the labels are watercolour washes in pink, yellow and blue, so the "sun-printed" story arrives at retail as three pastel washes, not as the process. The making and the artifact are further apart than the copy admits.
- A hospital cannot put plants on paper and call it a campaign system: two or three hero images, yes; a hundred social posts, no.
- No AI in it at all. Included as a process benchmark, and the brief should be honest that it is one.

**Should we...**
- ✅ **Adopt it** — the analogue-texture principle, on one campaign, at small scale.
**Why:** The whole point of this brief is moving away from output that looks like it came from a template, and the most reliable way to do that is to put something in the work that could not have come from a template. Cyanotype is the cheapest such thing available to a marketing team: botanical or clinical material, sunlight, paper, a scanner. For Shareef's residency recruitment work the direct application is the **Sub-I welcome materials and the year's campaign identity** — take real material from the programme (a printed uroflow trace, a surgical drape's weave, an actual instrument laid on paper, the residents' own handwriting on an evaluation form) and expose it, scan it, and use *that* as the texture layer under the type. It is a day's work with a sunny window, it produces assets no other department has, and it gives the marketing programme a story about how the work was made — which is exactly the thing that reads as premium and the thing a model cannot supply. What to leave behind is the packaging economics: we are not redesigning glass. What to carry forward is the discipline behind the halftone decision: translate the analogue asset into the production constraint you actually print under, rather than shipping a full-colour image into a one-colour world.

### nakkash — motion graphics as a scene file, checked in numbers
**Link:** https://github.com/lefinehq/nakkash · created **13 November 2026** (renamed from `motion` on **17 November**), pushed 18 November · Apache-2.0 or MIT · **8 stars, 0 forks, 0 releases** · status: early development, build from source
**Type:** Tool

**What it is:** A Rust engine for programmable motion graphics and video, built the way this brief has been arguing things should be built. A video is **a small scene file**; nakkash checks it, edits it and renders it with no browser and no other install. *"Scenes are data. A few lines say what should happen; nakkash works out every frame."* It is explicitly agent-facing — *"one-line edits, checks that answer in numbers, and no frames to look at"* — renders natively with float-precision compositing and true motion blur out to H.264/HEVC/ProRes/AV1, and imports existing work: **`.aep` projects and `.mogrt` templates come in as scenes.** The showcase is six rendered scenes in the repo — product-launch, brand-system, data-story, kinetic-type, shapes, camera-and-depth — each with its JSON beside it.

**What's good about it:** "Checks that answer in numbers, and no frames to look at" is the most important sentence in any video tool I have scanned. Every other motion workflow ends in a human watching a render and saying "the timing feels off," which is un-reviewable, un-diffable and un-automatable. A scene file can be linted: is the title on screen long enough to read, does the type enter before the frame it belongs to, does the brand colour appear before the logo does. That is `artboards`' `lint` applied to motion, and it is the correct shape for the medium. Second: `.aep` and `.mogrt` import is a real bridge. It means an existing After Effects library can become data — which is the only realistic path from where a department actually works today to where this is useful.

**Pros:**
- The scene-as-data model makes motion **diffable and reviewable** — a version bump is a text diff, not two 40MB files nobody can compare.
- Numeric checks are the enforcement mechanism this brief keeps asking for: a rule about legibility that can *fail*, instead of a guideline that gets ignored.
- Native rendering with true motion blur and ProRes/AV1 out means the output is delivery-grade, not a browser preview.
- `.aep`/`.mogrt` migration means the tool can meet an existing library rather than demanding a rebuild.
- The rename is documented in the repo's own decision log (`docs/design/decisions.md`, D73) with a reason — a project that records its own naming decision is a project that will record the rest.

**Cons:**
- **8 stars, zero releases, zero tags, one week old, build from source.** Early development is the project's own word for it, and a Rust build on a locked-down hospital image is not a realistic install.
- Six showcase GIFs prove the renderer runs; they prove nothing about whether the *timing* is good. Motion quality is taste, and no repo has ever shipped taste in a JSON schema.
- A scene DSL is a language to learn, and unlike `artboards` it has an incumbent: After Effects and its entire ecosystem. The import path is a one-way door until export exists.
- Nothing here addresses type — for an engine whose showcase includes kinetic type, there is no mention of optical alignment, tracking curves or hinting, which are most of what makes type move well.
- `lefinehq` has no other public work, no site, no named author in the README.

**Should we...**
- 👀 **Watch it** — and adopt the numeric-check idea for the social motion work now.
**Why:** The residency programme has 28 filmed faculty interviews, and the social output derived from them is eventually motion: name cards, quote builds, a match-day bumper, a graduation sequence. That work is currently done the way everyone does it — a human watches a render and guesses. nakkash's contribution is not the renderer, it is the assertion that **a motion design can be tested**: title dwell time against word count, entrance order against hierarchy, brand colour present before the logo. Those rules can be written down and checked by hand this week, without the tool, and that alone would raise the floor of every clip we make. Hold the software at watch for the obvious reasons — 8 stars and a cargo build is not infrastructure — but check back at the point it either ships a release or exports anything, and note the `.mogrt` import as the one feature that would make it usable by a team with an existing After Effects library.

### PosterProof — a pre-flight gate for the printed poster
**Link:** https://github.com/ufodia/poster-proof · live: https://poster-proof.vercel.app · created **13 November 2026**, pushed 15 November · MIT · **1 star, 0 forks, no releases** · by Ufodia
**Type:** Tool

**What it is:** A browser workbench for the last five minutes before a poster goes to print, built around a four-word headline: **"Beautiful poster. Wrong date?"** Drop in a generated or designed poster and it runs **local English OCR** (Tesseract.js, in-browser, nothing uploaded) against **approved copy you supply**, decodes the **QR code and checks its destination**, and measures **effective DPI** at the intended print size. Findings are listed for review, the analyzed PNG is exported, and a report can be exported for the record. Masking is applied to decoded QR pixels before OCR so the code does not pollute the text comparison; keyboard focus is retained while reviewing findings; Playwright browser tests cover the real workbench flow.

**What's good about it:** It names the correct failure. Not "AI posters look bad" — they increasingly don't — but **the specific, expensive, human error that survives every aesthetic review**: a date, a room number, a phone number, a QR pointing at the wrong registration page. For a hospital marketing surface, that is the only failure with real consequences. Second: the privacy architecture is right. OCR runs locally in the browser, no upload, no API key, no SaaS seat — which is the only kind of tool that gets approved inside a health system. Third: it is a *comparison*, not a judgment. Approved copy in, extracted text out, differences listed. That is deterministic and auditable, which is precisely what a model-based aesthetic review is not.

**Pros:**
- Checks the thing no design review catches: exact string fidelity between the approved copy and the rendered artifact.
- QR destination verification is the highest-value check in the tool — a poster that sends applicants to last year's page is a silent, total loss.
- Effective DPI at output size catches the classic generated-image trap: a 1024px render upscaled to A2, which looks fine on screen.
- Entirely local, MIT, no account, and it runs in a browser — deployable inside a network that will never approve another SaaS tool.
- Real engineering hygiene for a one-star project: Playwright flow tests, an e2e directory, a changelog, third-party notices, keyboard-focus handling.

**Cons:**
- **One star, one contributor, no releases, four days old.** This is a person's weekend, not a product; the OCR accuracy on stylised display type at poster scale is unmeasured and is exactly where Tesseract is weakest.
- English only, and poster typography is where OCR fails most — all-caps tracking, outlined type, type over imagery. The failures will be false positives that cost more review time than they save.
- It cannot see hierarchy, spacing, colour or contrast. Everything this brief cares about most is out of scope; it is a proofreader, not a designer.
- No batch mode and no CLI, so it is a manual step per poster — which means it will be skipped the first busy week unless it is wired into something.
- "Effective DPI" depends on the user entering the true print size, which is the input most likely to be wrong.

**Should we...**
- 👀 **Watch it** — but copy the checklist into the print workflow now.
**Why:** The department generates a growing volume of printed and posted material — Grand Rounds flyers, Sub-I welcome packets, recruitment posters, conference signage — and the failure mode that actually hurts is not an ugly gradient. It is a wrong date, a dead QR code, a phone number one digit off, or a portrait rendered at 96 DPI and printed at A2. Those four checks cost nothing to run by hand and are exactly the checks a generated artifact fails, because a model will happily invent a plausible room number. Adopt the checklist immediately as a pre-print gate: approved copy compared string by string, every QR scanned on a phone, effective DPI computed at the real trim size, and one human reading the artifact aloud. Hold the tool at watch until someone runs it against a real poster we have already printed and reports how many of its findings were true; if the false-positive rate is low, it is a genuine gate for the marketing pipeline, and if it is high, the checklist is still the win.

### ui-design-minimal — Apple's look as a preset, with an audit that enforces Apple's look
**Link:** https://github.com/tammo1007/ui-design-minimal · created **12 November 2026** · MIT (repo metadata says Other) · **4 stars, 0 forks, 0 releases** · by Tammo Elsner · also listed on alyn.dev and freedesignmd.com
**Type:** Tool (design system + agent skill)

**What it is:** A meticulously engineered, agent-facing design system: **`DESIGN.md`** with every token in front matter and every rule in prose, two type scales derived from Apple's iOS and macOS text styles (marketing: 17px body, 123px hero; product: 14px body, 32px controls, 44px rows), **48 React components** including `DockNav`, `CardRail`, `AppShell` with a floating glass sidebar, light/dark/increased-contrast from Apple's system palette, W3C DTCG tokens, Heroicons outline at stroke 1.35, seven scroll showpieces (`MediaExpand`, `ZoomThrough`, `StackCards`, `PinnedGallery`, `ScrollStory`, `TextReveal`, `ParallaxColumns`) — and **`scripts/lg-audit.mjs`, which fails the build on raw colours, default font sizes, the wrong type scale, glass on cards, missing CardRails and too many pinned sections.** Installer copies 49 files and refuses to overwrite without `--force`.

**What's good about it:** The engineering is the best of any design-skill repo scanned this fortnight, and it should be said plainly before it is dismissed. The audit is the real thing — it fails on raw colours and on the wrong type scale, which means it enforces a *system* rather than hoping an agent reads one. Two densities from one token set is a genuinely good idea: marketing scale for pages, product scale for dashboards, never mixed, is a rule most teams learn the hard way. The Liquid Glass rules are lifted from the actual HIG, including the fallbacks for reduced transparency and increased contrast that almost every glass implementation omits, and the copy is honest about the Apple dependency ("No Apple fonts, icons or assets are included… everyone else gets Inter").

**Pros:**
- A build-failing audit for a design system is rarer than it should be, and this one checks exactly the drift this brief keeps documenting: raw hex, off-scale type, glass where it doesn't belong.
- Two type scales, one token set, explicitly not mixable — the cleanest statement of the marketing/product split I have seen in a skill.
- Accessibility fallbacks for glass (reduced transparency, increased contrast) are specified rather than assumed, which is more than the platform they imitate does in most implementations.
- The installer is careful: never overwrites without `--force`, prints the remaining manual steps.
- Honest attribution and a stated limit — Apple's system font where available, Inter everywhere else.

**Cons:**
- **The entire premise is borrowing a look.** "Apple-like Liquid Glass" is not a derived identity, it is the most-recognised contemporary surface in software, and every screen built with it will read as an Apple screen built by someone else. That is the definition of the slop this brief exists to leave behind, wearing a very well-tailored suit.
- The seven scroll showpieces — CardRail on every landing page, StackCards, ZoomThrough, PinnedGallery — are precisely the AI landing-page kit this brief has been cataloguing since Monday, now with an audit that *requires* them.
- **The audit enforces the preset, not our system.** Run it against the Unified Platform and it fails on our navy, our Inter scale, our 44px/18px rules — it is a machine for converting a bespoke platform into Apple's grammar.
- 4 stars, one author, four days old, and the download counters on two third-party directories are doing the credibility work.
- MIT in the README, "Other" in the repository metadata — a licence ambiguity that matters the moment anything ships.

**Should we...**
- ❌ **Skip it** — take the audit's shape, none of the files.
**Why:** This is the most competent artifact in today's scan and the cleanest example of the trap, which is why it earns a breakdown rather than a line in the dropped list. Its craft is real: the audit, the two densities, the accessibility fallbacks are all things we should have. But everything it enforces is Apple's decisions, and a urology platform that adopts it will look like every other well-made 2026 dashboard — which is the exact outcome the brief is built to prevent. The distinction worth keeping is one this brief has been circling for a week: a **design system is a set of decisions you made**, and this one is a set you installed. What to take, concretely and for free: the audit's *categories*. Our own `design-system.md` should fail a build on literal hex instead of tokens, on a font size outside the scale, on a control under 44px, on a focus ring that is missing, on an icon stroke that does not match its size. That is four checks and an afternoon, it uses our values instead of Apple's, and when it passes it means something about our work rather than about a preset.

## Slop Radar
- **The most dangerous slop is well-engineered slop.** ui-design-minimal has a build-failing audit, two type scales and real accessibility fallbacks — and it still produces Apple's look on every screen. Quality of implementation is not evidence of originality; a very good machine for making the same thing is still a machine for making the same thing.
- **The age of the artifact.** Tom Johnson's essay names it: the decision trail used to be a by-product of working in a canvas, and in a chat it is removed by the next prompt. Any workflow that leaves no trace of what was rejected cannot be reviewed, taught, or defended — and it will drift back toward the model's average, because nothing is holding a position.
- **Speed reported as quality.** "300+ prototypes", "200 views in two days", "three weeks from idea to launch" — every one is a throughput claim. The question that follows is never asked: of the 200 views, how many were worth looking at? Ask for the rejections, not the count.
- **Preset-plus-audit as a category.** Three separate repos this fortnight ship a locked visual style with a script that fails the build when you deviate from it. The audit makes the preset feel rigorous. It is rigor in service of somebody else's taste.

## Overall Assessment
Today's best finds are not AI tools at all — they are two brand systems and one essay — and that is the signal. Pentagram drew a five-weight typeface from the proportions of a client's own coin; LOVE. made label artwork by laying real plants on light-sensitive paper in the sun. Neither could have been generated, not because the model is weak but because **both are records of contact with a specific object**, and a model's output is by definition a summary of everything it has seen rather than an index of one thing. The AI-native work that cleared the bar sits on the other side of the same principle: Mercury's Builder Mode is valuable because it puts the prototype in the product's *actual* code and mocks the *actual* data, and nakkash is interesting because it turns motion into a scene file that can be checked rather than a render that can only be watched. Craft, in every case, is the presence of a real referent — a coin, a plant, a codebase, a data state. The single highest-leverage move for Shareef this week is to add a referent to the next thing he ships: pick one property the residency programme actually owns — a uroflow curve, a cystoscope's circular field, a faculty member's handwriting on an evaluation form, a real instrument laid on paper — and let it generate the texture, the mark or the display cut, instead of choosing a style. Everything else in this brief, including the audits, is downstream of having something true to point at.
